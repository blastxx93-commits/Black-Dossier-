So which used EVs are losing value fastest – and why?

The biggest reason is new car prices. In April 2026, carsales pointed out that you can now buy a brand-new EV for as little as $23,990 plus on-road costs – the BYD Atto 1. When new cars get cheaper, used cars have to get cheaper too.

Carsales also said higher-priced EVs have generally lost value first, and that Tesla's repeated price cuts on the Model 3 and Model Y quickly reset used prices. Early long-range EVs, like the Hyundai Kona Electric and the Tesla Model 3 Long Range, have lost some of their premium. And because buyers want the latest versions, earlier models can look dated quickly.

Let's put some real numbers on that. Savvy, using RedBook data, said in March 2026 that a 2022 Tesla Model Y rear-wheel drive was about forty-eight per cent cheaper than when it was new, and a BYD Atto 3 was down about thirty-nine per cent. Savvy's Adrian Edlington said it "creates real opportunities for Australians looking at the used EV market." And that's the flip side. Every dollar an owner loses is a dollar a used buyer saves.

There's another factor: competition. Hyundai cut up to $13,857 from some versions of the new Kona Electric in 2026. Brands are under pressure from the emissions rules to sell more EVs, and that often means discounting. Every cut on a new car puts more pressure on the price of a used one.

On the other hand, cheaper EVs and models with fewer direct rivals, like the GWM Ora, have been less exposed to sudden price swings, according to carsales.
