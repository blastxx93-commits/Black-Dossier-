So what should you do if you own an EV and you're thinking of selling?

### ch09
And if you're buying a used EV in 2027, here's your checklist.

Step one: ask for a battery health report. Many dealers and auction houses can provide one, and some brands can generate a report through their service centres. If the seller won't provide one, ask why. Don't just trust the range on the dashboard.

Step two: check the battery warranty. Many EV batteries have a warranty of around eight years, often with a kilometre limit. Find out exactly how much is left, and whether it transfers to you.

Step three: check the charging equipment. Does the car come with a charging cable? Does it support fast charging? And where will you charge it at home?

Step four: compare with new. With new EVs from about $24,000 before on-road costs, make sure the used one is genuinely cheaper once you add everything up – including warranty, servicing and running costs.

Step five: check the model's history. Has it had recalls? Has the model been updated recently, making the older version look dated? And does the brand have service and parts support near you?

Step six: get a pre-purchase inspection, ideally from a workshop that knows EVs. Check the tyres too – EVs are heavy and can wear tyres faster – and look for any sign of accident damage underneath, especially near the battery.

Step seven: if you're in New South Wales, factor in the planned road user charge from July 2027 – roughly a few hundred dollars a year for an average driver, based on the proposed rate.
