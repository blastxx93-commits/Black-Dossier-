### ch01
First, something that surprises a lot of people. Australia doesn't have a specific lemon law for cars. Some American states do. We don't.

What we have instead is the Australian Consumer Law. Under it, everything you buy – from a phone to a sixty-thousand-dollar ute – comes with automatic consumer guarantees. It has to be of acceptable quality. It has to be fit for purpose. It has to match the description you were given. And it has to last a reasonable time.

That last part matters more than most people realise. The guarantees don't stop when the warranty stops. If a major part fails earlier than it reasonably should for the price you paid, you can still have rights, even after the factory warranty has run out.

If your car has a minor fault, you're entitled to a free repair within a reasonable time. If it has a major failure – or the same problem keeps coming back – you can choose a refund or a replacement. And the key part: these rights cannot be excluded, restricted or changed by any business. Not by the dealer. Not by the car maker. Not by the small print on the contract you signed.

So what counts as a major failure? According to the ACCC's guidance, a fault is major if you wouldn't have bought the car had you known about it. Or if the car is significantly different from the description or the demo you were shown. Or if it's substantially unfit for its normal purpose and can't be fixed easily or within a reasonable time. Or if it's unsafe.

In real life, that could be an engine that has to be replaced in the first year. A transmission that keeps failing after repeated repairs. Electrical faults that leave you stranded again and again. Or a car that spends more time in the workshop than in your driveway.

And there's one more important idea: a series of minor faults can add up to a major failure. If your car keeps going back for different problems, and together they mean you wouldn't have bought it had you known, you can argue you have a major failure – even if no single fault would be enough on its own.

It sounds simple. So why do so many owners end up in a fight? Let's look at what's actually happened in 2026.

### ch02
Case number one: BYD and the wrong build year.

In July 2026, the ABC and the Sydney Morning Herald reported that BYD had sold cars to Australian customers as 2026 models when they had actually been built in 2025. The affected models included the Atto 3, the Sealion 8 and the Shark 6 ute. BYD said around 3,800 vehicles had been incorrectly listed, and roughly 1,200 of them had already been sold or allocated to customers.

BYD's explanation was that it had used the date the cars left the factory, rather than the date they were built, in sales contracts. BYD Australia's public relations director called it an administrative error and said there was no deceit. To be fair to BYD, the specifications of the cars were the same, and the correct build date was in the vehicle identification number all along.

But here's why owners were angry. A car's build year affects its resale value. Erin Turner from the Consumer Policy Research Centre said these owners had effectively taken on an extra year of depreciation, and warned they could be seriously out of pocket when they sold. The Insurance Council of Australia even advised affected owners to contact their insurers, because the error could affect their cover.

BYD's first offer was $1,100. Owners didn't accept that quietly. Within days, BYD added the option of a full refund. Then it raised the payment to $3,500 – and made it retrospective, so owners who had already accepted the lower amount would get the difference too. Owners could also swap their car for a like-for-like vehicle built in 2026, with BYD covering the costs.

By late July, BYD said around seventy-two per cent of owners had taken the payment, and more than 350 had chosen a refund or a replacement.

That's what fighting back looks like. The owners who spoke up didn't just get a better deal for themselves. They got a better deal for every owner affected.

### ch03
Case number two: the rusty car that went all the way to the Supreme Court.

In a case published in March 2026, a Victorian buyer discovered that the car she'd bought from a dealer had serious rust. The tribunal found it was a clear breach of the consumer guarantee of acceptable quality, and ordered a full refund of the purchase price.

The dealer didn't accept that. It challenged the decision. And the Supreme Court of Victoria backed the buyer. A full refund – confirmed by the state's highest court.

Then, in January 2026, another buyer won against the same dealer, this time over the cost of repairs on a second-hand car. And that's an important point most people miss. The consumer guarantees don't only apply to brand-new cars. If you buy a used car from a licensed dealer, it still has to be of acceptable quality for its age, price and kilometres.

The lesson from both cases is simple. When a business says no, that isn't the end of the road. Tribunals and courts can – and do – side with the buyer.

### ch04

In October, Ford recalled 1,085 Rangers and Everests from the 2024 to 2026 model years, because the 10.1-inch centre screen can go blank – which means the reversing camera image may not appear. CarExpert notes that Ford issues more recalls in Australia than any other car maker.

Suzuki's new Fronx small SUV was halted from sale after a rear seatbelt failure in ANCAP crash testing. And Deepal had a stop-sale order on its E07.

Here's what you need to know. A recall is the manufacturer admitting a fault and fixing it for free – and that's the system working. You should always get recall work done. But a recall doesn't cancel your consumer rights. If the fix can't be done within a reasonable time, or the same problem keeps coming back after the repair, you may be entitled to more than just a recall repair. In some cases, that can mean a refund or a replacement.

You can check whether your car is affected on the government's Product Safety Australia recall website, using your car's make and model, or by asking your dealer to run your VIN.

### ch06
If the law is on your side, why does it feel impossible? Because the system around it is slow and confusing.

In July 2026, the Australian Automotive Dealer Association released an independent review of how the Australian Consumer Law works for cars, prepared by Emeritus Professor Jenny Buchan. Its conclusion was blunt. Consumer protections for new vehicles are no longer delivering fair or timely outcomes – not for owners, and not for dealers either.

The review found that vague definitions and unclear procedures are driving up costs, putting pressure on tribunals and, most importantly, leaving drivers without their cars for long periods. Dealers told the review that workshop backlogs could stretch six to eight weeks just to diagnose a problem – especially with electric vehicles. Not fix it. Just find out what's wrong.

There's another twist most buyers don't know about. When something goes wrong, you deal with the dealer. But the dealer didn't build the car. The dealer association says dealers often end up carrying the legal risk, while the manufacturer stays at arm's length. So it's calling on the federal government to force manufacturers to get involved in disputes earlier.

And it's not just about cars. A report from the Consumer Policy Research Centre and the Consumer Action Law Centre found that making a complaint about a faulty car can involve up to sixty steps – and that's if everything goes smoothly. No wonder so many people give up.

### ch07
So why should you believe fighting back actually works? Because of the case that put the whole car industry on notice.

Between 2013 and 2017, nine customers bought new Mazdas that developed serious, recurring faults. One car had its engine replaced three times. When the owners asked for refunds or replacements, Mazda refused, or offered only part of the money back.

The Australian Competition and Consumer Commission took Mazda to the Federal Court. The court found Mazda had made forty-nine false or misleading statements to those customers about their rights. And in 2024, Mazda was ordered to pay $11.5 million in penalties, plus compensation to the owners.

That case is the reason every car company in Australia now knows the regulator is watching. It's also why you should never accept a business telling you that a refund is "not our policy". Under the consumer law, a business can't make up its own rules about your rights.

### ch08
And here's the good news: the rules are about to get tougher.

In November 2025, the state and federal consumer affairs ministers agreed to strengthen the consumer guarantees. The plan includes civil penalties and expanded enforcement powers for businesses that don't comply. Right now, a business that wrongly refuses a valid claim often just has to pay up later if the customer fights hard enough. Penalties would change that maths completely.

The ministers also agreed to strengthen what's called supplier indemnification. In plain English, that means dealers should be able to recover their costs from the manufacturer when a car is faulty. That matters to you, because a dealer who isn't stuck with the bill has much less reason to stall.

Treasury is now drafting the new laws, and there will be another round of consultation before they pass. So the changes aren't in force yet. But the direction is clear. Refusing to fix a faulty car is going to get a lot more expensive for the businesses that do it.

### ch09
So here's exactly what to do if your new car turns out to be a lemon.

Step one: document everything. Every fault, every date, every service visit, every phone call. Take photos and videos of the problem when it happens. Keep every invoice and job card.

Step two: put it in writing. Phone calls are easy to forget or deny. Emails aren't. A simple trick: after every phone call, send a short email that says "as discussed today", and list what you were told. If they disagree with your summary, they have to say so in writing.

Keep a fault diary too. Note the date, the kilometres, what happened, and how long the car was off the road. If a tribunal ever has to decide whether a repair took a "reasonable time", a diary with real dates and real days without a car is one of the strongest pieces of evidence you can have.

Step three: contact the dealer – and the car maker's Australian head office at the same time. It keeps everyone accountable.

Step four: use the right words. Say you're making a claim under the consumer guarantees of the Australian Consumer Law. If the fault is serious, or keeps coming back, say you believe it's a major failure and you're requesting a refund or a replacement.

Step five: know what they can't do. They can't charge you to fix a fault the law covers. You don't have to pay anything towards the remedy you're entitled to. And your warranty doesn't replace your consumer rights.

And here's a myth worth busting. A survey found that around forty per cent of new car owners believe they have to service their car at the dealer to keep their consumer guarantee rights. You don't. Using a qualified independent mechanic who follows the logbook with the right parts doesn't cancel your rights.

Step six: if you get nowhere, contact your state's fair trading agency. If that fails, take it to your state tribunal – NCAT in New South Wales, VCAT in Victoria, QCAT in Queensland, or the equivalent where you live. Tribunal fees are usually modest, and you don't need a lawyer for most claims. You can also report the business to the ACCC.

And while you're waiting, don't forget your costs. Under the Australian Consumer Law, you can also be entitled to compensation for losses that were reasonably foreseeable because of the fault. That can include things like towing, or a hire car if you genuinely needed one while yours was off the road. Keep every receipt, and include them in your written claim.

It also helps to be specific about what you want. Don't just say "fix it". Say whether you're asking for a repair, a refund or a replacement, give a reasonable deadline – say fourteen days – for a written reply, and ask for the job card from every visit, showing what was found and what was done. Businesses respond differently when they can see you know the process and you're keeping records.

And one more tip from the BYD case: don't be afraid to go public. Owners who spoke to journalists saw the offer improve within weeks.

### ch10
Finally, let's talk about the hidden cost of a faulty car: resale value.

As the BYD case showed, even a paperwork problem can knock thousands off what a car is worth. A car with a history of major faults can be harder to sell, and buyers will use every problem as a bargaining chip.

So if you're dealing with a faulty car, think about the end game. A repair might fix today's problem, but a refund or a replacement may protect you from losing money later. And if you're offered compensation, ask how the amount was worked out. BYD itself said it raised its payment after checking reputable valuation guides. You're allowed to ask for that same evidence.

And there's a lesson here for anyone buying a car right now. Before you sign, check the build date on the compliance plate, not just the model year on the contract. Ask the dealer to confirm in writing that the car has no outstanding recalls. And if you're buying second-hand, run a check on the national register to make sure there's no money owing on it and that it hasn't been written off. Five minutes of checking before you buy can save you months of fighting afterwards.
