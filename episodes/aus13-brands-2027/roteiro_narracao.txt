### ch08
So here's the most important part. What actually happens to you if your car's brand leaves Australia?

First, your rights. Under the Australian Consumer Law, manufacturers and importers must make spare parts and repair facilities reasonably available for a reasonable time after you buy a product. That doesn't disappear just because a brand stops selling new cars.

Second, look at what happened in real exits. When General Motors announced the end of Holden in 2020, it said it would provide spare parts and servicing for "at least the next 10 years," and that it would cover all warranty commitments. When Citroen stopped selling new cars in Australia on the 1st of November 2024, it said servicing would continue through its authorised service centres, with genuine parts and factory-trained staff, and that the factory warranty would continue.

What about finance and insurance? If your brand leaves, your car loan doesn't change – you still owe the same amount, on the same terms. Your insurance continues too. But insurers price policies partly on repair costs and parts availability, so if parts become harder to get, that could show up in premiums over time.

And if your car is on a novated lease, the lease continues as normal. The bigger question is the residual value – the lump sum you'll owe at the end. If the car's market value falls because the brand has left, it could be worth less than the residual. That's worth discussing with your lease provider early.

But here's the reality. Even with promises, owners of departed brands often report it gets harder over time. Fewer dealers means longer drives for servicing. Some parts can take longer to arrive. And independent mechanics may be less familiar with the brand.

And the biggest hit is usually resale value. When a brand leaves, or looks like it might, used buyers get nervous – and prices can fall. That's often a bigger cost for owners than any servicing problem.

### ch09
So if you own a car from one of these brands, here's what to do now.

Step one: don't panic-sell. None of these brands has announced an exit. Selling in a hurry could cost you more than waiting.

Step two: check your warranty and service plan. Write down exactly what's covered and until when. Keep every service record, because a full service history matters even more if the brand's future is uncertain.

Step three: find your nearest service centres – not just one. If your dealer closes, you want to know where the next authorised workshop is, and whether a good independent mechanic in your area knows the brand.

Step four: keep up with recalls. Check the Product Safety Australia website for your car's make and model, and make sure any recall work is done while the dealer network is still in place.

Step five: think about common parts. If you're keeping the car for a long time, it's worth knowing how easy it is to get things like brake pads, filters and windscreens for your model.

Step six: if you're planning to sell in the next year or two anyway, watch the market. If there's an official announcement that a brand is leaving, used values can fall quickly. Being prepared – with your service history ready and a realistic valuation – means you can act fast if you need to.

And if you're buying a new car from a struggling brand, use it to your advantage. Discounts can be big. But ask the questions we covered in our Black Friday video: who stands behind the warranty, where is the nearest service centre, and how fast can they get common parts? Then factor a lower resale value into the price you're willing to pay.

Before we wrap up, here are the five warning signs to watch for in 2027 – for any brand, not just the ones in this video.

One: sales collapsing month after month, not just one bad month. Two: dealers closing, especially in regional areas. Three: a change of importer or distributor – like Peugeot. Four: the model range shrinking, with popular models paused or axed and no clear replacements. And five: huge, ongoing discounts on new cars just to move stock.

One warning sign on its own doesn't mean a brand is leaving. But when you see three or four together, it's time to pay attention – especially if you're about to buy.
