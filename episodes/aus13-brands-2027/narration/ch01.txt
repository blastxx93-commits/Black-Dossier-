First, the big picture. Why are so many brands struggling at once?

Reason one: the Chinese brands. In the first half of 2026, according to CarExpert, BYD sales rose 124 per cent to 52,335. Chery was up almost seventy-seven per cent. Tesla was up almost sixty-seven per cent. GWM was up about twenty per cent. In September 2026, Geely was up 870 per cent on the same month a year earlier, and Omoda Jaecoo was up 344 per cent. Every one of those sales has to come from somewhere.

Reason two: the government's emissions rules. Under the New Vehicle Efficiency Standard, brands with too many high-emitting vehicles build up penalties. In the first six months of the scheme, Mazda's estimated liability was around $25.4 million, Nissan's around $10.7 million, Subaru's almost $7 million and Hyundai's about $4.2 million. And the targets get much tougher in 2027.

Reason three: the sheer number of brands. With around seventy brands in a market of about 1.2 million sales, there simply isn't enough room for everyone to make money.

And it's not just the smaller brands feeling it. Even Toyota – still by far the biggest brand in the country – was down 21.4 per cent in the first half of 2026, to 95,141 sales, according to CarExpert. Suzuki was down almost twenty-one per cent. Porsche was down almost thirty per cent, Cupra more than thirty-two per cent and Maserati thirty-three per cent. When the market leader is down by a fifth, the brands at the bottom have very little room to move.

Back in late 2024, the Australian Automotive Dealer Association's chief executive, James Voortman, predicted: "I think we will see a number leave, some of them might not necessarily make the front pages." He said the real question was "how many important brands are going to leave." And he pointed out that during his time leading the association, Australia had already lost Infiniti, Chrysler, Citroen and Opel. Before that, of course, there was Holden – the biggest exit of all, in 2020. Brands leaving Australia isn't a theory. It happens regularly.
