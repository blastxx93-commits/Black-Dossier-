### ch01
Let's start with the big one: the Electric Car Discount.

Since 2022, electric cars provided by an employer, including through a novated lease, have been exempt from fringe benefits tax – as long as they're under the luxury car tax threshold for fuel-efficient vehicles. That threshold is currently $91,387.

If you've never used one, here's how a novated lease works in simple terms. It's an agreement between you, your employer and a leasing company. Your employer pays the car's costs – the lease, and often running costs like insurance, servicing and charging – out of your salary before income tax is taken out. That lowers your taxable income. Normally, that benefit attracts fringe benefits tax, which eats into the saving. For eligible EVs, that tax has been zero.

For a lot of Australians, this has been the single biggest reason to buy an EV. A novated lease lets you pay for the car out of your pre-tax salary, and with no fringe benefits tax on top, the savings can be huge. The National Automotive Leasing and Salary Packaging Association's chief executive, Rohan Martin, has called it "Australia's most effective demand-side lever for EV uptake."

But it's been expensive for the government. A statutory review found that "the revenue costs of the Electric Car Discount are increasing significantly", and that "alternative policy approaches may be more fiscally efficient and equitable."

And it's been popular. Leasing industry groups have pointed to the discount as a major reason EV sales took off, especially among middle-income workers who could suddenly afford a new car they'd never have considered paying cash for.

So in May 2026, the government confirmed the discount would be wound back in three stages.

### ch02
Here's exactly how it works.

Stage one: until the 31st of March 2027, nothing changes. EVs under the fuel-efficient luxury car tax threshold keep the full exemption.

Stage two: from the 1st of April 2027 to the 31st of March 2029, the full exemption only applies to EVs costing $75,000 or less. Electric cars above $75,000, but still under the luxury car tax threshold, get a twenty-five per cent discount on their fringe benefits tax instead of a full exemption.

Stage three: from the 1st of April 2029, all eligible EVs get the twenty-five per cent discount. The full exemption is gone.

And there are two important details. First, existing leases are protected. If you're already in an eligible novated lease before the change, you're grandfathered. What does "grandfathered" mean in practice? It means the rules that applied when your lease started keep applying for that lease. You don't suddenly lose the exemption halfway through. But when that lease ends and you take out a new one, the new rules will apply.

Second, plug-in hybrids already lost their exemption on the 1st of April 2025. So this is only about fully electric cars.

The NRMA estimated that some novated lease EV owners could lose more than seventeen thousand dollars in tax savings under the changes. Your own number depends on your salary, the car's price, the lease length and the kilometres you drive.

The Electric Vehicle Council welcomed the fact that the discount was extended rather than scrapped outright. Its chief executive, Julie Delvecchio, said it was "good news for everyday Australians who are doing the sums on going electric."

### ch03
So what does this mean for you?

If you're looking at an EV under $75,000, the change on the 1st of April 2027 doesn't affect you – at least until 2029. That covers a huge chunk of the market now. In February 2026, CarsGuide listed seven electric cars available for under forty thousand dollars drive-away, including the BYD Dolphin, the MG4, the GWM Ora, the Chery E5, the Leapmotor B10 and the Hyundai Inster. And the BYD Atto 1 started from $23,990 before on-road costs.

But if you're looking at an EV between $75,000 and the luxury car tax threshold – think larger SUVs and premium models – the date matters a lot. Sign an eligible novated lease before the 1st of April 2027, and you lock in the full exemption. Sign after, and you only get the twenty-five per cent discount.

That's why I'd expect a rush of more expensive EV leases in the first three months of 2027. And it's why you should talk to your employer and your lease provider now, rather than in March, when everyone else is trying to do the same thing.

One warning: delivery times. A lease generally needs the car to actually be provided to you for the benefit to apply, so check with your provider how the timing works. If the car you want has a long wait, ordering in late March might be too late.

### ch04
Now, the second change: the luxury car tax.

Right now, cars over a certain price pay an extra tax of thirty-three per cent on every dollar above the threshold. For fuel-efficient vehicles, including electric cars, that threshold is $91,387.

On the 24th of March 2026, Prime Minister Anthony Albanese announced a free trade deal with the European Union. As part of it, Australia agreed to remove the five per cent tariff on European passenger cars. And CarExpert reported a new luxury car tax category for zero-emissions vehicles, with a threshold of $120,000. According to CarExpert, that new zero-emissions threshold is due to start on the 1st of July 2027. The trade deal still needs to be formally ratified.

What does that mean in dollars? Here's a simple illustration. Today, an EV priced at $100,000 is $8,613 over the threshold. At thirty-three per cent, that's about $2,842 in luxury car tax. Under a $120,000 threshold, the same car would pay no luxury car tax at all.

But don't get too excited. James Voortman, chief executive of the Australian Automotive Dealer Association, welcomed the trade deal but described the luxury car tax change as "a narrow change that will only affect less than one per cent of vehicles sold." So this helps buyers of premium EVs – think Polestar, BMW, Mercedes-Benz, Porsche and Audi – much more than buyers of an everyday BYD or MG.

### ch06
Now, the change that's causing the biggest argument: paying per kilometre.

Every time you fill up with petrol or diesel, you pay fuel excise. That money goes to the federal budget, and a large share is spent on roads. EV drivers don't buy fuel, so they don't pay it.

And fuel excise has been in the spotlight in 2026. During the fuel crisis, the federal government temporarily halved it, from 52.6 cents a litre to 26.3 cents, from the 1st of April until the 1st of July. The NRMA also noted that the share of fuel excise reinvested in roads fell to seventy per cent in the 2026 budget, down from seventy-nine per cent forecast a year earlier. So the question of who pays for roads is only getting bigger. Chasing Cars estimated that EVs cost the federal government roughly $130 million to $165 million a year in lost fuel excise.

New South Wales plans to fix that with a road user charge from July 2027. Chasing Cars reported the proposed rates as about 2.974 cents per kilometre for electric vehicles and 2.379 cents per kilometre for plug-in hybrids. AAP reported it was forecast to raise $440 million in its first three years.

What would that cost you? At 12,000 to 15,000 kilometres a year, Chasing Cars calculated an EV driver would pay about $360 to $450. For comparison, a petrol car using seven litres per hundred kilometres would pay around $440 to $550 a year in fuel excise over the same distance.

And notice something: plug-in hybrids would pay the charge too, even though they also burn petrol and pay fuel excise when they do. That's one of the reasons critics say the design needs work.

But there's a legal problem. Victoria introduced a similar charge – 2.8 cents per kilometre for EVs – and in 2023 the High Court found that only the federal government can levy that kind of tax. So how can NSW go ahead?

NSW Treasurer Daniel Mookhey has said NSW would prefer a national system. But, quote, "in the absence of a national system, it remains the intent of the NSW government to enact the law." He suggested the Commonwealth could refer its powers to NSW to avoid another court defeat. As of mid-2026, NSW hadn't said exactly how it would collect the money.

### ch07
And what about a national charge?

In 2025, Treasurer Jim Chalmers said the government was "accelerating work" on an EV road user charge. But in April 2026, Transport Minister Catherine King told the ABC she wasn't sure there was a path to pass one through parliament, and that "it may not be the time for it right now." The May 2026 federal budget didn't include one. The government said it was "continuing to work with states and territories."

So in 2027, you could end up with one state charging EV drivers and the rest of the country not – unless Canberra steps in.

The reaction has been strong on both sides. A NSW Government spokesperson argued that "everybody who uses roads should contribute to them." The NRMA and the Electric Vehicle Council have both supported a road user charge in principle – but the Electric Vehicle Council argued it should only start once EVs make up thirty per cent of the national fleet. Its chief executive, Julie Delvecchio, said the NSW tax "fails on all three fronts." The council cited Sydney University research finding twenty-eight per cent of likely EV buyers would be dissuaded, and pointed to New Zealand, where it said the EV share of sales fell from 20.6 per cent to 3.8 per cent after a similar move. Polestar Australia's boss Scott Maynard called a national charge on EVs a "thinly veiled tax."

It's worth putting those numbers side by side. An average EV driver in NSW might pay a few hundred dollars a year under the proposed charge. A petrol car doing the same distance pays a similar amount, or more, in fuel excise – plus the much higher cost of the fuel itself. So even with a road user charge, running an EV on home charging is still likely to be cheaper for most people. The question is whether the charge arrives before EVs are affordable for everyone.

My take? Some form of road user charge for EVs looks very likely in the long run, because fuel excise is shrinking as more people go electric. The real fight is about when it starts, how much it is, and whether it's fair compared with what petrol and diesel drivers pay.

### ch08
Now, here's the change that could actually make EVs cheaper in 2027: the emissions rules.

Under the New Vehicle Efficiency Standard, the target for passenger cars drops from 117 grams of carbon dioxide per kilometre in 2026 to 92 grams in 2027. That's a cut of more than twenty per cent in a single year. Then 68 grams in 2028, and 58 in 2029.

Car makers that miss their targets build up penalties of roughly fifty dollars per gram per vehicle. The best way to avoid them is to sell more electric and hybrid cars. And the fastest way to sell more EVs is to cut prices.

We've already seen it. Hyundai cut up to $13,857 from some versions of the Kona Electric in 2026. And CarsGuide reported that the government's Clean Energy Finance Corporation committed sixty million dollars to Hyundai Capital Australia to help offer cheaper finance on Hyundai and Kia EVs.

And it isn't just about price cuts. The tougher the targets get, the more EV and hybrid models car makers need to bring to Australia. More than sixty new EV models were expected to enter the Australian market as the scheme started. More choice usually means more competition, and more competition usually means better deals.

The market is already moving fast. The Australian Automobile Association reported that battery electric and plug-in hybrid vehicles made up 31.61 per cent of new light vehicle sales in the June quarter of 2026. By September 2026, battery electric cars made up more than twenty-four per cent of new car sales. CarExpert reported that EVs outsold both petrol and diesel vehicles in August and September 2026. And as we covered in our last video, the federal opposition has promised to scrap the NVES if it wins government – so this part of the picture could change too.

And there's one more change from the 2026 budget worth knowing about. The government committed forty million dollars to new regional and kerbside chargers. That matters, because a lack of charging is still one of the biggest reasons people give for not buying an EV, especially outside the big cities.

### ch09
So, should you buy an EV before 2027, or wait? Here's how I'd think about it.

If you're buying an EV over $75,000 through a novated lease: the deadline is the 31st of March 2027. Get your lease in place before then, and check how delivery timing works with your provider.

If you're buying a premium EV between about $91,000 and $120,000, paying cash or with a normal loan: the new luxury car tax threshold is due from the 1st of July 2027. Waiting could save you thousands – but only if the trade deal is ratified on time. Ask the dealer how they'll handle the timing, and get any promise in writing.

If you live in New South Wales: budget for a possible road user charge from July 2027 – roughly $360 to $450 a year for an average driver, based on the proposed rate. That's still less than the fuel excise most petrol cars pay, but it's a new cost.

If you're buying a cheaper EV, under $75,000: the fringe benefits tax change doesn't affect you until 2029. And with tougher emissions targets in 2027, there's a good chance car makers will keep pushing EV discounts. Shop around, compare drive-away prices, and don't rush.

And think about resale. Rules that make new EVs cheaper or more expensive can also move the value of used ones. If luxury car tax falls on premium EVs from July 2027, for example, buyers may compare a used premium EV with a cheaper new one. Nobody can predict exactly how prices will move, so buy a car you're happy to keep for a while.

And whatever you do, don't let a deadline push you into the wrong car. A tax saving doesn't help if the car doesn't suit your life.
