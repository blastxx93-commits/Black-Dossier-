### ch00_hook
Forty-two thousand and forty-five. That's how many new vehicles built in China were sold in Australia in a single month – September 2026. More than every car shipped from Japan and Thailand put together.

Read that again. Japan had been Australia's biggest source of imported cars since 1998. Toyota, Mazda, Mitsubishi, Subaru, Nissan – the brands your parents drove, the brands that built the Australian car market. And in 2026, China blew past all of them.

Almost four in every ten new vehicles sold here in September were made in China. Four of the ten best-selling models in the country were Chinese. One Chinese brand grew more than eight hundred per cent in a year. Another went from a few hundred sales to more than twelve thousand.

This isn't a slow change. It's a takeover – and it happened in about twelve months.

So in this video, I'll show you exactly how it happened, month by month. Who's winning, who's getting crushed, and why Australians are switching so fast. And then I'll show you the side of this story that most people aren't talking about – the risks, the brands that might not last, and what all of this means for the price you pay for your next car.

### ch01
Let's start with how fast this has moved, because the timeline is genuinely shocking.

At the start of 2025, Chinese-built vehicles were a growing slice of the market, but Japan was still comfortably on top. In May 2025, Chinese-made cars made up 19.5 per cent of new-vehicle sales. A year before that, in May 2024, it was just fifteen per cent.

Then came February 2026. According to the Federal Chamber of Automotive Industries, 22,300 vehicles sold in Australia that month came from China – a 25 per cent share. Japan was second with 21,600, and Thailand third with 19,400. For the first time since 1998, Japan was no longer Australia's number one source of new cars.

By May, CarExpert reported that Chinese-made vehicles had reached 34.8 per cent of the market – more than one in three. Sales of Japanese-built vehicles that month were down almost 29 per cent on the year before. As CarExpert put it: "The increase in Chinese-made vehicle sales has come largely at the expense of Japan."

In June, something even bigger happened. China-built sales hit 55,331 for the month – more than Japan and Thailand combined, at 50,395. Part of that was a single ship carrying almost five thousand BYD and Denza vehicles. But in September, it happened again, without any special shipment. China: 42,045. Japan and Thailand together: 40,933. That's 38.9 per cent of the entire market, according to Cars24, using VFACTS and Electric Vehicle Council data.

From fifteen per cent to nearly forty per cent in a little over two years. In the history of the Australian car market, nothing has moved this fast.

And there's a twist of history here. Fifty years ago, it was Japanese cars that were the cheap newcomers. Plenty of Australians laughed at them at first – small, unfamiliar, from a country that wasn't known for cars. Then they turned out to be reliable, well-equipped and affordable, and over the following decades they pushed aside the local giants. By the time Ford, Holden and Toyota closed their Australian factories in 2016 and 2017, Japanese and Thai-built cars ruled the market.

Now the same story is playing out again – only this time, Japan is the established giant, and China is the newcomer that people underestimated. The difference is speed. The Japanese takeover took decades. The Chinese one is happening in a couple of years.

### ch02
Now let's look at the brands doing the damage, using official VFACTS figures for the first nine months of 2026.

BYD is the headline. Year to date, it has sold 76,614 vehicles, up 102 per cent. It's now Australia's second-best-selling brand, behind only Toyota. And in June, it came within 243 cars of Toyota in a single month – 18,881 to 19,124. A few years ago, most Australians had never heard of BYD.

Then there's Chery, up 58.2 per cent to 38,022. Its Tiggo 4 small SUV was the sixth-best-selling vehicle in the whole country in September.

Geely is the most extraordinary story of all. Its sales are up 639 per cent this year, to 23,632. In September alone, Geely was up 870 per cent on the same month in 2025. Its EX5 electric SUV sold 2,008 cars that month – up 620 per cent – and finished eighth on the national sales chart.

Geely's premium brand, Zeekr, went from just 730 sales at this point last year to 12,120. Omoda Jaecoo was up almost twelve hundred per cent in the first half. GWM, the longest-running Chinese brand here, was up about twenty per cent, and its Haval Jolion was seventh in September. Even MG, owned by China's SAIC, kept growing.

And the Chinese brands are moving upmarket fast. BYD's luxury brand, Denza, had passed 1,600 sales since arriving, according to CarsGuide. And in late October 2026, CarExpert reported that the Denza B5 – a big plug-in hybrid four-wheel drive aimed squarely at the Toyota LandCruiser Prado – had been cut to under seventy-five thousand dollars drive-away. A Chinese luxury 4x4 taking on the Prado would have sounded ridiculous five years ago. Now it's on sale at your local dealer.

Tony Weber, chief executive of the Federal Chamber of Automotive Industries, summed it up in July: "The Australian automotive market has shifted on its axis during the first months of 2026." And BYD's chief operating officer, Stephen Collins, put it more simply: "We asked our customers to back us in, and they did, and we delivered."

### ch03
Look at the top of the September sales chart, and you can see the new Australia.

The Ford Ranger was number one, as it's been for much of the last few years. Then the Toyota RAV4, the Tesla Model Y, and the Toyota HiLux. But right behind them: the BYD Sealion 7 with 2,687 sales, the Chery Tiggo 4 with 2,059, the GWM Haval Jolion with 2,018, and the Geely EX5 with 2,008. Four Chinese models in the national top ten.

And it goes further than Chinese brands. The Tesla Model Y sold in Australia is built in China, too – and it was Australia's best-selling vehicle in May, June and August 2026, the first electric car ever to top the monthly chart. So when you count where cars are actually made, China's reach is even bigger than the brand names suggest.

There's also a flood of new models coming. Cars24 reported that the number of Chinese models on sale in Australia is expected to grow from 72 to around 120 by 2027. More utes, more seven-seat SUVs, more plug-in hybrids, more luxury brands. Chery's upmarket brand, Exlantix, is due here in 2027, and Avatr has revealed a luxury six-seat SUV for Australia for 2028.

If you think the takeover is finished, it's barely started.

And the next battleground is the most Australian one of all: utes. The BYD Shark 6 plug-in hybrid has already sold more than thirteen and a half thousand units this year. GAC has confirmed a ute for 2027, Chery has a diesel-electric ute on the way, GWM is preparing a plug-in hybrid version of its smaller Cannon, and LDV's new Terron 9 was up 195 per cent in September. The Ranger and the HiLux have owned this segment for a decade. That's exactly the territory Chinese brands are now coming for.

### ch04
So who's paying the price? Every Chinese sale has to come from somewhere – because the market isn't growing.

Total new-vehicle sales for the first nine months of 2026 were 914,312, almost exactly the same as the year before – just 127 cars fewer. That means this is a zero-sum game. When BYD gains forty thousand sales, someone else loses forty thousand.

Here's who's losing. Toyota is down 15.9 per cent year to date, to 152,944, with its market share falling from 19.9 per cent to 16.7. Mazda is down 16.2 per cent. Mitsubishi is down 30.2. Subaru is down 29.3. Nissan is down 33.3 per cent.

Every one of those is a Japanese brand. According to analysis by John Cadogan at Auto Expert, Japanese-built vehicle sales in the first eight months of 2026 fell around 22 per cent – from almost 220,000 to around 170,000. Over the same period, Chinese-built sales rose about 71 per cent, from roughly 122,000 to almost 209,000.

It's not just the Japanese, either. Korean brands Hyundai and Kia were both down around twenty per cent in September. Volkswagen was down almost 37 per cent. The established brands that dominated Australian driveways for decades are all going backwards at the same time.

To put the scale of this into perspective: Toyota alone has sold almost 29,000 fewer vehicles this year than at the same point in 2025. Mazda is down more than eleven thousand. Mitsubishi, Subaru and Nissan have lost tens of thousands between them. Meanwhile BYD has added almost thirty-nine thousand, Geely twenty thousand and Chery fourteen thousand. The money hasn't left the market. It's simply moved to different showrooms.

### ch06
So why are Australians switching so fast? There are four big reasons.

The first is price. Chinese brands are offering a lot of car for the money. BYD's little Atto 1 electric hatch starts from $23,990 before on-road costs. In October 2026, BYD launched four thousand dollars cashback on much of its range, taking the Sealion 5 plug-in hybrid SUV down to $29,990 before on-road costs. When a well-equipped plug-in hybrid SUV costs less than many small petrol cars, buyers pay attention.

The second is technology. Most Chinese brands lead with electric cars and plug-in hybrids, with big screens, long equipment lists and lots of power. In September, electric vehicles outsold petrol cars in Australia for the month, and plug-in hybrid sales more than doubled. Chinese brands make most of those cars.

The third is timing. In 2026, fuel prices spiked – diesel hit as high as $3.50 a litre in parts of Australia in May, according to CarExpert. At the same time, the government's emissions rules are pushing car makers toward low-emission vehicles, and the Electric Car Discount has made electric cars cheaper to salary package. Chinese brands had exactly the right cars, at exactly the right moment.

And the fourth is trust. A few years ago, a lot of buyers were nervous about Chinese cars. That's changing fast. Omoda Jaecoo has said publicly that the stigma around Chinese cars is mostly gone – and the sales numbers suggest a lot of Australians agree.

Long warranties have helped too. GWM, for example, offers a seven-year, unlimited-kilometre warranty on its vehicles, with eight years on its hybrid and plug-in hybrid batteries. When a brand is new to you, a long warranty is a powerful way to say: trust us. And the more neighbours, friends and work mates who buy these cars without drama, the faster that trust spreads. That's how a market flips – not with one big moment, but one driveway at a time.

### ch07
But here's the side of the story you don't hear in the ads.

There are far too many Chinese car brands for all of them to survive. In March 2026, accounting firm BDO told a car dealer industry event that the current expansion is unsustainable. BDO's automotive partner, Sam Venn, was blunt: "It's not sustainable. It's as simple as that." BDO pointed out that well over 150 car makers operate in China, and that number is widely tipped to fall sharply. It named the likely survivors as the biggest groups: BYD, Geely, SAIC, GWM and Chery.

The dealer numbers show why. BDO reported that in early 2026, a typical Toyota dealer was selling around 72 new cars a month. A BYD dealer, 48. But some newer Chinese brands were selling just a handful per dealer – Geely seven, JAC three, Deepal two. Even the boss of one Chinese brand, Leapmotor, has admitted that not all Chinese brands will survive in Australia.

Then there's resale value. Many of these cars are electric, and electric cars have generally lost value faster. A 2025 Pickles and Datium analysis found a two-year-old BYD Atto 3 kept about 54 per cent of its value, compared with about 88 per cent for a Toyota RAV4. And as we covered in our video on BYD crash repairs, parts and approved repairers can be a real issue for fast-growing brands.

None of that means Chinese cars are bad. It means you need to choose the brand, not just the deal.

There's also a bigger-picture question that some buyers are asking: what happens if trade tensions or politics change? In late October 2026, the federal government said Australia would not follow the United States and other countries in banning Chinese cars from military sites. For everyday buyers, there's no sign of restrictions here. But it's a reminder that the car market is now tied up with global politics in a way it never used to be.

### ch08
So what are the established brands doing about it?

Toyota insists it's not in decline. In October 2026, its Australian sales boss, John Pappas, was asked whether Toyota was preparing for a smaller future. His answer: "No. We don't plan to be declining. We plan to continue to grow." He said Toyota's order books remain "very, very healthy", and blamed much of the 2026 drop on supply problems with the new RAV4 and HiLux.

And the RAV4 is bouncing back. In September, it was the second-best-selling vehicle in the country, with 4,624 sales, up 81 per cent. Toyota also has a cheaper RAV4 plug-in hybrid coming in 2027, and it's offering a rare four-thousand-dollar bonus on the HiLux to fight back.

Others are cutting prices hard. Kia slashed up to fifteen thousand dollars off its Tasman ute. Ford cut its Ranger plug-in hybrid by more than ten thousand. Mazda cut thousands off its big SUVs. The old guard isn't giving up – but it's being forced to fight on price, and that's exactly the battlefield where Chinese brands are strongest.

As Pappas pointed out, there are now nearly seventy car brands in Australia. That's a lot of companies chasing a market that isn't getting any bigger.

### ch09
So what does the Chinese takeover mean for you?

If you're buying, it's good news – for now. More competition means sharper prices, from every brand. As Auto Expert's John Cadogan put it, a car maker losing market share "needs sales", and "they need your sale more than they did when demand was booming." His advice: don't start from the sticker price. Use a comparable Chinese model as your reference point, make a serious offer, and be ready to walk away.

If you're considering a Chinese car, do your homework on the brand, not just the car. How long has it been in Australia? How many dealers and service centres does it have near you? How fast can it get parts? What does the warranty actually cover? And what will the car be worth in three or five years? Stick to brands with real scale and a real local network, and you'll massively reduce your risk.

It's also worth checking whether the brand has a track record of sudden price cuts. Several Chinese brands have cut prices sharply soon after launch. That's great if you're buying after the cut – but painful if you bought just before it, because the value of your car drops the moment the new price is announced. Ask the dealer when the model was last repriced, and whether an update is due soon.

And if you own a Japanese or Korean car, don't panic. These are still huge, well-supported brands. But expect more deals, more price cuts and more pressure on resale values as competition heats up.

### ch10
So, did Chinese cars just take over Australia?

By country of manufacture, yes. China overtook Japan as Australia's biggest source of new cars in February 2026. By September, almost four in ten new vehicles sold here were built in China, and four of the top ten models were Chinese. BYD is now second only to Toyota. Geely, Chery and Zeekr are growing at rates the industry has never seen.

And it's not over. The Australian Automotive Dealer Association has forecast that Chinese-made vehicles could make up 58 per cent of new cars on Australian roads by 2035. The number of Chinese models on sale is set to almost double by 2027.

But this takeover will also have casualties – including some of the Chinese brands themselves. And for the biggest brand of all, the pressure has never been higher.
