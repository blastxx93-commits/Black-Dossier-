But here's the side of the story you don't hear in the ads.

There are far too many Chinese car brands for all of them to survive. In March 2026, accounting firm BDO told a car dealer industry event that the current expansion is unsustainable. BDO's automotive partner, Sam Venn, was blunt: "It's not sustainable. It's as simple as that." BDO pointed out that well over 150 car makers operate in China, and that number is widely tipped to fall sharply. It named the likely survivors as the biggest groups: BYD, Geely, SAIC, GWM and Chery.

The dealer numbers show why. BDO reported that in early 2026, a typical Toyota dealer was selling around 72 new cars a month. A BYD dealer, 48. But some newer Chinese brands were selling just a handful per dealer – Geely seven, JAC three, Deepal two. Even the boss of one Chinese brand, Leapmotor, has admitted that not all Chinese brands will survive in Australia.

Then there's resale value. Many of these cars are electric, and electric cars have generally lost value faster. A 2025 Pickles and Datium analysis found a two-year-old BYD Atto 3 kept about 54 per cent of its value, compared with about 88 per cent for a Toyota RAV4. And as we covered in our video on BYD crash repairs, parts and approved repairers can be a real issue for fast-growing brands.

None of that means Chinese cars are bad. It means you need to choose the brand, not just the deal.

There's also a bigger-picture question that some buyers are asking: what happens if trade tensions or politics change? In late October 2026, the federal government said Australia would not follow the United States and other countries in banning Chinese cars from military sites. For everyday buyers, there's no sign of restrictions here. But it's a reminder that the car market is now tied up with global politics in a way it never used to be.
