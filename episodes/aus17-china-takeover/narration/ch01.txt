Let's start with how fast this has moved, because the timeline is genuinely shocking.

At the start of 2025, Chinese-built vehicles were a growing slice of the market, but Japan was still comfortably on top. In May 2025, Chinese-made cars made up 19.5 per cent of new-vehicle sales. A year before that, in May 2024, it was just fifteen per cent.

Then came February 2026. According to the Federal Chamber of Automotive Industries, 22,300 vehicles sold in Australia that month came from China – a 25 per cent share. Japan was second with 21,600, and Thailand third with 19,400. For the first time since 1998, Japan was no longer Australia's number one source of new cars.

By May, CarExpert reported that Chinese-made vehicles had reached 34.8 per cent of the market – more than one in three. Sales of Japanese-built vehicles that month were down almost 29 per cent on the year before. As CarExpert put it: "The increase in Chinese-made vehicle sales has come largely at the expense of Japan."

In June, something even bigger happened. China-built sales hit 55,331 for the month – more than Japan and Thailand combined, at 50,395. Part of that was a single ship carrying almost five thousand BYD and Denza vehicles. But in September, it happened again, without any special shipment. China: 42,045. Japan and Thailand together: 40,933. That's 38.9 per cent of the entire market, according to Cars24, using VFACTS and Electric Vehicle Council data.

From fifteen per cent to nearly forty per cent in a little over two years. In the history of the Australian car market, nothing has moved this fast.

And there's a twist of history here. Fifty years ago, it was Japanese cars that were the cheap newcomers. Plenty of Australians laughed at them at first – small, unfamiliar, from a country that wasn't known for cars. Then they turned out to be reliable, well-equipped and affordable, and over the following decades they pushed aside the local giants. By the time Ford, Holden and Toyota closed their Australian factories in 2016 and 2017, Japanese and Thai-built cars ruled the market.

Now the same story is playing out again – only this time, Japan is the established giant, and China is the newcomer that people underestimated. The difference is speed. The Japanese takeover took decades. The Chinese one is happening in a couple of years.
