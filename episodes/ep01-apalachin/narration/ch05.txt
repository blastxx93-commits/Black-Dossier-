CHAPTER FIVE. The roadblock.

On November 14th, Croswell set out with his partner, Trooper Vincent Vasisko, and two agents from the U.S. Treasury Department.

They drove up McFall Road in an unmarked car.

What they saw stopped them cold.

Parked around Barbara's property were dozens of expensive cars. Big, dark, freshly polished. License plates from all over the country. In a town where most people drove pickup trucks, it looked like a luxury car showroom had landed in a cow pasture.

The officers began writing down license numbers.

At around twenty to one in the afternoon, someone inside the house noticed the unmarked car in the driveway. According to later court records, it was Barbara's wife who saw it.

Word spread through the property in seconds.

Croswell knew what came next. He called for backup. More troopers raced to the area, and the police set up a roadblock on the road below the estate, the only practical way out by car.

Then, at about twenty past one, the exodus began.

Some of the guests jumped into their cars and drove down the hill, straight into the roadblock. The first car to arrive carried two men from the region, Emanuele Zicari and Dominick Alaimo.

Others chose a different escape.

They ran.

Picture it. Men who controlled unions, casinos and entire city districts, sprinting across open fields in tailored overcoats. Expensive shoes sinking into mud. Hats lost in the brush. Some headed for the woods along the creek, hoping to slip away and catch a ride on a back road.

Some of them made it. Many did not.

One by one, the troopers stopped cars and gathered the men who came stumbling out of the trees. Every one of them was taken to the state police barracks for identification.

By the end of the day, the police had identified fifty-eight men. Estimates suggest that around a hundred had come to the meeting. Dozens escaped on foot. Among the names later linked to those who got away, writers have often mentioned Sam Giancana of Chicago.

But the men who were caught were extraordinary enough.

Vito Genovese, the man at the center of New York's power struggle. Carlo Gambino, soon to become one of the most powerful bosses in American history. Joseph Profaci and Joseph Magliocco of Brooklyn. Santo Trafficante, with his interests in Havana. Joseph Civello, representing Dallas. Russell Bufalino, the organizer. And their host, Joseph Barbara.

In a single afternoon, a state trooper in a small town had done what the FBI had not. He had put names, faces and addresses to a national network.

Now came the hard part. Explaining what they were all doing there.
