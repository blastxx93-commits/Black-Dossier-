CHAPTER EIGHT. The trooper who woke a nation.

The meeting at Apalachin was supposed to bring order to the underworld. Instead, it brought daylight.

For the men involved, the consequences came in other ways.

Joseph Barbara, the host, found his business life collapsing under scrutiny. He faced tax trouble, lost his liquor license, and saw his legitimate business suffer. In June 1959, he died of a heart attack. His estate on McFall Road was later sold.

Vito Genovese, the man many believe called the meeting, did reach the top. But not for long. In 1959, he was convicted in a federal narcotics case and sentenced to fifteen years in prison. He would die behind bars a decade later. Some in the underworld have long whispered that the case against him was helped along by his own rivals. That has never been proven.

And Sergeant Edgar Croswell? He became, for a time, one of the most famous policemen in America. A small-town investigator who trusted his instincts, followed a few rooms in a motel and a meat order from a local butcher, and exposed something the most powerful police agency in the country had downplayed for decades.

The men at Apalachin had spent their lives building an organization designed to stay invisible. No written records. No public faces. A code of silence.

And it all came undone because a few dozen men, in a moment of panic, ran into the woods.

After that day, the word "Mafia" was no longer a rumor. It was a headline. And the people hunting it now knew exactly whose names to write down.

But the biggest blow was still to come.

Six years later, a low-level soldier from Vito Genovese's own family would sit in front of a Senate committee, with television cameras rolling, and do something no made man had ever done in public.

He would describe the organization from the inside. Its ranks. Its rituals. Its name.

His name was Joseph Valachi. And that... is a story for the next Black Dossier.
