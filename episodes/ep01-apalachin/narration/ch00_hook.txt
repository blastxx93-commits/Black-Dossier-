November 14th, 1957.

A cold, gray Thursday, in a farming town so small that most Americans had never heard its name.

Up a muddy country road, behind a stone house on a hill, sit dozens of long, black cars. Cadillacs. Lincolns. Imperials. Their license plates read New York... New Jersey... Ohio... Illinois... Texas... California... Florida.

Inside, around a barbecue pit and a mountain of steaks, stand some of the most powerful criminals in the United States.

And then, someone looks down the driveway... and sees a car that should not be there.

Within minutes, men in silk suits and handmade shoes are running across cow pastures. Crashing through the brush. Disappearing into the woods. Bosses who commanded small armies, scrambling through the mud like schoolboys.

And waiting for them at the bottom of the hill is one stubborn state trooper, a man the FBI had ignored for years.

The meeting never even really began. But by the end of that day, the most powerful lawman in America would be forced to face something he had brushed aside for decades.

The Mafia was real. And it was national.

And the strangest part of this story? Almost nobody went to prison for it.

This is Black Dossier. And this... is Apalachin.
