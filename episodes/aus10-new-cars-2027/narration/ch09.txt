Before we get to what it all means, a few honourable mentions that just missed the list.

The new Nissan Patrol is switching from its big V8 to a 3.5-litre twin-turbo V6 with 317 kilowatts and 700 newton-metres. CarsGuide reported orders opening in late 2026, with cars arriving in late 2026 or early 2027 – and Nissan says Australia will be the first right-hand-drive market for it.

The Zeekr 8X is a plug-in hybrid SUV with a claimed 660 kilowatts combined. CarsGuide estimated it at around eighty thousand dollars. The Polestar 5 is a big electric sedan with up to 650 kilowatts, priced from $171,100. And at the other end of the market, the Renault Symbioz is a compact hybrid SUV from $36,990.

And one that won't be coming: Isuzu has cancelled its plans for the electric D-Max in Australia. As Isuzu's Rod Caldwell put it: "Customers don't want it." Not every brand thinks the electric ute's time has come.

So what does all this mean for you?

If you're buying a ute: 2027 will give you more choice than ever before. The Navara Pro plug-in hybrid, the GAC ute, Chery's hybrid utes and eventually the Hyundai ute will all be fighting the Ranger, the HiLux and the BYD Shark 6. That kind of competition usually means sharper prices and better equipment. And as we covered in our video on 2027 ute prices, the emissions rules are pushing every brand in the same direction.

If you're buying an SUV: the plug-in hybrid fight is about to get serious. A cheaper RAV4 plug-in hybrid, the Omoda 7, and dozens of Chinese plug-in hybrids mean more choice under sixty thousand dollars than ever before.

If you're buying an electric car: watch the timing. As we covered in our last video, the fringe benefits tax exemption for EVs over $75,000 changes on the 1st of April 2027, and a new luxury car tax threshold for zero-emissions vehicles is due from July 2027.

And whichever new model catches your eye, do your homework on the brand behind it – especially if it's a brand that's new to Australia. As we covered in our BYD videos, a great car can still be a frustrating ownership experience if parts take months and the nearest approved repairer is hours away. Before you buy, ask three questions. Where is the nearest service centre? Are crash parts for this model stocked in Australia? And how long is the warranty – and who stands behind it if the local importer changes?

And for any electrified ute or SUV, look for real-world tests, not just brochure numbers. Electric range, fuel use while towing and real payload with a canopy and accessories fitted can be very different from the figures on the website.

And if you're in no hurry: waiting a few months can pay off. New models often push down the prices of the cars they replace. And brand-new models often have early teething problems. There's nothing wrong with letting someone else find them first.
