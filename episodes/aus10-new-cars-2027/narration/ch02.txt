Number ten: the Honda Super-One.

This one is small, cheap and a little bit crazy. It's an electric kei-style car – a tiny Japanese city car – with around seventy kilowatts. In June 2026, CarsGuide listed it as a 2027 model, with an estimated price of around thirty-five thousand dollars. That's an estimate, not a confirmed price.

Why does it matter? Because the cheap end of the electric market has exploded. The BYD Atto 1 started from $23,990 before on-road costs in 2026, and CarsGuide counted seven electric cars available for under forty thousand dollars drive-away. If Honda can bring something fun and well-built into that space, it gives buyers another option that isn't Chinese – and it shows how far even the most traditional brands are being pushed towards electric.

It also says something about where the industry is heading. When Honda, one of the most conservative car makers in the world, starts building tiny electric city cars for markets like ours, you know the shift isn't a fad. It's the new normal.

Number nine: the BMW iX3.

This is BMW's new-generation electric SUV, and it's important for one simple reason: price. CarsGuide reported the iX3 50 xDrive at $109,900, arriving from July 2026, and the iX3 40 at $89,900 before on-road costs, due in the last quarter.

That $89,900 price is interesting, because it's just under the luxury car tax threshold for fuel-efficient vehicles of $91,387. And with a new $120,000 luxury car tax threshold for zero-emissions vehicles due from July 2027, premium electric SUVs like this could get even more competitive next year. It's a sign that the luxury brands are taking Australia's EV rules seriously.
