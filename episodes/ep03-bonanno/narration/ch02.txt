CHAPTER TWO. Thirty years on top.

For three decades, Joseph Bonanno did something rare in his world. He survived.

He married Fay Labruzzo two months after taking power, and raised three children. He invested in cheese companies, garment factories and real estate. To his neighbors, he looked like a prosperous businessman.

The newspapers had another name for him: Joe Bananas. He hated it. He saw himself as an aristocrat of the old Sicilian tradition, a man of rules, of loyalty, of silence.

Under him, the organization grew far beyond Brooklyn. It reached into Montreal, where heroin and gambling money flowed across the border. It reached all the way to Tucson, Arizona, where Bonanno bought a home in the desert and spent his winters away from New York.

In 1956, his son Bill married Rosalie Profaci, the niece of Joe Profaci, boss of another New York family. The reception filled the Hotel Astor in Times Square. It was a wedding, and it was also an alliance between two crime clans.

But by the early 1960s, the world that made Bonanno was disappearing.

Lucky Luciano died in exile in Naples in 1962. Vito Genovese was locked in a federal prison. Joe Profaci died of cancer the same year as Luciano.

Of the original bosses who sat on the Commission in 1931, Bonanno was almost the last one left.

And the newer men on the Commission did not trust him.

Carlo Gambino. Tommy Lucchese. And in Buffalo, the boss of western New York, Stefano Magaddino. Bonanno's own cousin, a relative of the same Peter Magaddino who had crossed the ocean with him decades earlier.

They watched him plant flags in Canada, in Arizona, in California. To them, it looked like a man building an empire.

In the autumn of 1963, while Bonanno's rivals counted his moves, a former soldier named Joe Valachi sat before the United States Senate. The man from our last file. On live television, he drew a chart of the five New York families.

One of the names on that chart was Bonanno.
