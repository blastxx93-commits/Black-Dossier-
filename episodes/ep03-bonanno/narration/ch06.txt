CHAPTER SIX. Nineteen months.

Whatever happened in that farmhouse, the story did not end there.

By his own account, his captors let him go after several weeks. But Joe Bonanno did not go home. He did not call the police. He did not show up in court.

He simply disappeared.

Weeks became months. Months became more than a year. Rumors spread that he was dead, buried somewhere upstate, or lying at the bottom of the ocean.

Meanwhile, his empire was tearing itself apart.

On one side stood Gaspar DiGregorio, the boss the Commission had chosen. On the other stood the men still loyal to Bonanno, led by his son, Bill.

The press found a name for the conflict that the gangsters themselves never used. They called it the Banana War.

In January 1966, DiGregorio sent word that he wanted peace. A meeting was arranged at a house on Troutman Street in Brooklyn.

It was a trap.

As Bill Bonanno and his men walked toward the house, gunmen opened fire from the windows and the street. By some accounts, hundreds of rounds were fired that night.

Not one man was hit.

Four months later, on a spring morning in May 1966, a well-dressed man walked into the federal courthouse at Foley Square in Manhattan, with a lawyer at his side.

It was Joe Bonanno. Alive. Healthy. And ready to explain that he had been kidnapped.

Almost nobody believed him.

The Magaddinos, the investigators pointed out, had been under FBI surveillance during that time. Most historians would come to the same conclusion: there probably was no kidnapping at all. It was a disappearing act, staged to escape both the grand jury and the Commission.

Even his son described those weeks in a strange way. Not as a nightmare, but as a kind of rest, a stay as a guest of his cousin.
