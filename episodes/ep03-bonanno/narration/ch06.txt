CHAPTER SIX. Bombs in the desert.

His return did not end the fighting. It made it worse.

Over the next two years, men from both sides were shot in Brooklyn and Queens. DiGregorio was wounded by machine-gun fire, then fell ill. The Commission replaced him with another loyal choice, Paul Sciacca.

Then, in 1968, Bonanno's body made the decision for him. He suffered a heart attack.

He sent word to the Commission: he would retire, leave New York forever, and live in his house in Tucson. The bosses accepted. After four years, the war was over.

The desert was supposed to be his exile. It became a target.

On the night of July twenty-first, 1968, an explosion ripped through a garage on a ranch outside Tucson, owned by Peter Licavoli, a leader of the Detroit Mafia. The next night, two bombs went off outside Joe Bonanno's home, knocking down a wall of his patio.

More bombs followed across the city, all aimed at men connected to the Mafia. Nobody was killed. But the message seemed clear. Someone wanted a new mob war.

When the bombers were caught, the truth was stranger than anyone imagined.

Testimony pointed to the man behind the campaign: David Hale, a special agent of the FBI, the bureau's own organized crime specialist in Tucson. According to the witnesses, his plan was to make the mobsters believe they were attacking each other, and let them finish the job.

Some of those who planted the bombs were convicted. Hale left the bureau. He never faced trial.

Bonanno had survived the Commission, a mob war, and the federal government. Now he had survived an FBI agent's private war.

And then, the old man did something nobody saw coming.
