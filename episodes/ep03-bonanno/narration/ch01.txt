CHAPTER ONE. The youngest boss in America.

To understand why anyone would want Joe Bonanno to disappear, you have to go back thirty-three years. To another night, on the very same avenue.

September 1931. High above the avenue, in an office of the New York Central Building, a group of men flash badges and walk in, pretending to be federal agents.

They are killers.

Within minutes, Salvatore Maranzano, the self-declared boss of all bosses, lies dead on the floor. Stabbed, then shot.

The order came from a rising gangster named Lucky Luciano.

That day, Maranzano's crew lost its leader. And the man they chose to replace him was only twenty-six years old.

Joe Bonanno.

An orphan from Sicily who had crossed the sea hidden on a fishing boat, with no visa. A bootlegger who had fought at Maranzano's side in a vicious gang war for control of New York.

Now he had a crown. And a seat at a brand-new table.

Luciano had created the Commission: a council where America's top crime lords would settle disputes with votes, instead of machine guns. Bonanno was its youngest member.

He kept that seat for more than thirty years. No prison. No bullets. The press called him Joe Bananas. He hated the name. His empire stretched from Brooklyn to Montreal, all the way to a house in the Arizona desert.

But by 1963, almost every man from that first table was dead or behind bars. The younger men wanted his seat.

And he was about to make the most dangerous bet of his life.
