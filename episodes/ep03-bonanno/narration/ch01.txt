CHAPTER ONE. The youngest boss in America.

The man who vanished that night was not a street thug. He was Mafia royalty.

Giuseppe Bonanno was born in 1905, in Castellammare del Golfo, a fishing town on the coast of Sicily, where the old families called themselves men of honor. By fifteen, he had lost both of his parents.

At nineteen, with no visa and no money, he hid aboard a Cuban fishing boat and slipped into Florida. He made his way to New York, in the middle of Prohibition, and went to work for a fellow Sicilian named Salvatore Maranzano, smuggling illegal liquor.

In 1930, Maranzano went to war against the most feared gangster in the city. Bonanno fought at his side. They won.

Five months later, Maranzano was shot and stabbed to death in his own office, on orders from a rising gangster named Lucky Luciano.

Luciano did not make himself king. He created something smarter: a Commission, a board of directors where the biggest bosses in America would settle their disputes at a table, instead of with machine guns.

Maranzano's men needed a new leader. They chose Bonanno.

He was twenty-six years old. The youngest Mafia chief in the country, with a permanent seat on the Commission.

For the next thirty years, he did something very few men in his world ever managed. He stayed alive, and he stayed out of prison.

The newspapers called him Joe Bananas. He hated the name. He saw himself as an aristocrat, a man of rules and silence. He invested in cheese companies, clothing factories and real estate. His organization stretched from Brooklyn to Montreal, and all the way to Tucson, Arizona, where he kept a house in the desert.

But by 1963, the old world was dying. Luciano was dead. Other legends were in prison or in the grave. Bonanno was one of the last original bosses still standing.

And he was about to make the most dangerous bet of his life.
