CHAPTER EIGHT. The book that broke the Commission.

In his old age, Joe Bonanno decided to do something no boss of his rank had ever done.

In 1983, he published his memoirs. He called the book A Man of Honor.

In it, he described the Mafia's history and traditions. And he wrote openly about the Commission, the ruling council of bosses, its members, its rules, its meetings.

He went on 60 Minutes. He sat across from Mike Wallace and talked about his life, without ever admitting to a crime.

In New York, a federal prosecutor named Rudy Giuliani read the book with great interest.

For decades, the government had struggled to prove that a national ruling body existed at all. Now a founding member had described it, in print, under his own name.

The book helped inspire one of the biggest prosecutions in American history: the Commission case. In 1986, the heads of several New York crime families were convicted, and sentenced to a hundred years in prison.

Giuliani wanted Bonanno to testify. Bonanno refused. At eighty years old, he was jailed for contempt of court, and spent fourteen months behind bars rather than break his silence.

He was released in 1986, and went home to the desert.

Joseph Bonanno died in Tucson, on May eleventh, 2002. He was ninety-seven years old. A man who once sat beside Luciano died in his own bed, decades after almost every rival.

He never changed his story. Until the end, he insisted that on that rainy night in 1964, he had been kidnapped.

So which was it? Was the boss who vanished really a prisoner? Or did he stage the most famous disappearance in Mafia history, and walk away laughing?
