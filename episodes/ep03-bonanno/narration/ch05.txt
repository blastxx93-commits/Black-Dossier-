CHAPTER FIVE. The night on Park Avenue.

The dinner ran late. When the group finally stepped outside, it was nearly midnight, and a cold drizzle was coming down over Manhattan.

Bonanno shared a taxi with two of his lawyers. One of them, William Power Maloney, lived on Park Avenue, at Thirty-Sixth Street. Bonanno was going to spend the night there.

The cab stopped. Bonanno and Maloney got out.

According to Maloney, two men appeared from the shadows. One took Bonanno by the arm and said, "Come on, Joe. My boss wants to see you."

Maloney protested. One of the men fired a shot into the sidewalk, and the lawyer dove for cover. When he looked again, Bonanno had been pushed into a waiting car, and the car was gone.

The next day, the story was everywhere. The newspapers had a word for it that sounded almost absurd: a Mafia boss, kidnapped.

The FBI started looking. So did the police. So did his own soldiers.

Years later, Bonanno would tell his own version. He said the men ordered him to crouch on the floor of the car. He felt it turn and turn, then speed onto a highway and cross the George Washington Bridge into New Jersey.

He said the men in the car were his own relatives: Nino Magaddino, and Peter Magaddino, the son of the Buffalo boss.

He said they took him to a farmhouse somewhere in the countryside, and kept him there for weeks.

And he said the man behind it all was the one boss who knew him better than anyone.

In 1983, on national television, a reporter asked him who had kidnapped him. Bonanno answered with three words.

"My dear cousin."
