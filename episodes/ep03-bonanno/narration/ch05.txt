CHAPTER FIVE. Nineteen months.

By his own account, his captors released him after several weeks. But Joe Bonanno did not go home. He did not call the police. He did not show up in court.

He disappeared.

Weeks became months. Months became more than a year. Rumors said he was buried upstate, or lying at the bottom of the ocean.

Back in Brooklyn, his organization was tearing itself apart. On one side, Gaspar DiGregorio, the Commission's choice. On the other, the soldiers who stayed loyal to the missing man, led by his son, Bill.

The newspapers called it the Banana War.

In January 1966, DiGregorio's side asked for a peace meeting, at a house on Troutman Street in Brooklyn.

It was a trap.

As Bill Bonanno and his men walked toward the door, gunmen opened fire from the windows and the street. By some accounts, hundreds of rounds were fired that night.

Not one man was hit.

Four months later, on a May morning in 1966, a well-dressed man walked up to the federal courthouse at Foley Square in Manhattan, with a lawyer at his side.

Joe Bonanno. Alive. Healthy. Ready to testify that he had been kidnapped.

Almost nobody believed him.

Investigators pointed out that the Magaddinos had been under FBI surveillance during that period. Moving a kidnapped don across the state without the bureau noticing would have been close to impossible. Most historians reached the same conclusion: the kidnapping was probably staged. A disappearing act, to escape both the grand jury and the Commission.

Even his son described those weeks in strange words. Not a nightmare. More like a rest, as his cousin's guest.

Joe Bonanno was back. But the war he left behind was not finished with him.
