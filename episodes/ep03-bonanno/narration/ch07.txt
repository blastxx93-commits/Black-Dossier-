CHAPTER SEVEN. The bombs in the desert.

His return did not end the fighting. It made it hotter.

Over the next two years, men from both sides were shot in Brooklyn and Queens. DiGregorio was wounded in a burst of machine-gun fire, then fell ill. The Commission replaced him with another of its own choices, Paul Sciacca.

And then, in 1968, the decision was made for Bonanno by his own body. He suffered a heart attack.

He sent a message to the Commission. He would retire. He would leave New York, and move permanently to his home in Tucson, Arizona. The bosses accepted. After four years, the Banana War was over.

But the desert did not bring him peace.

On the night of July twenty-first, 1968, an explosion tore through a garage on a ranch outside Tucson, owned by Detroit boss Peter Licavoli. The next night, two bombs exploded outside Joe Bonanno's home, knocking down a wall of his patio.

Over the following months, more bombs went off across Tucson, targeting men connected to the Mafia. No one was killed, but the message was clear. Someone wanted a new war.

When the bombers were finally caught, the truth was stranger than any mob story.

Testimony pointed to the man behind the campaign: an FBI agent. David Hale, the bureau's own organized crime specialist in Tucson. According to the witnesses, his plan was simple: make the mobsters believe they were attacking each other, and let them finish the job.

Some of the men who planted the bombs were convicted. Hale left the bureau. He never faced trial.

The old boss had survived the Commission, a mob war, and the federal government. Now he had survived the FBI's own private war.
