October 1964. Park Avenue, New York. Almost midnight.

A light rain is falling. A taxi pulls up at the corner of Thirty-Sixth Street, and two men in overcoats step out onto the wet sidewalk.

One of them is a lawyer. The other is his client, a silver-haired man of fifty-nine, who in a few hours is due to appear before a federal grand jury.

He never makes it to the door.

Two strangers come out of the dark. One grabs him by the arm.

"Come on, Joe. My boss wants to see you."

The lawyer shouts. A gunshot cracks into the pavement at his feet, and he runs for cover. By the time he looks up, the car is gone. And so is Joe Bonanno.

Boss of one of the five families of New York. A member of the Mafia's ruling Commission for more than thirty years.

For nineteen months, nobody knows if he is alive. Not the FBI. Not his own son. Not the men who run the Mafia.

And when he finally walks back into the light, the story he tells may be the greatest lie in the history of Cosa Nostra.

CHAPTER ONE. The youngest boss in America.

Giuseppe Bonanno was born in January 1905, in Castellammare del Golfo, a fishing town on the northwest coast of Sicily. In that town, the old families did not call themselves criminals. They called themselves men of honor.

His father was one of them.

When Giuseppe was a small child, the Bonannos crossed the ocean to Brooklyn, to Williamsburg. A few years later they sailed home again. By the time he was ten, his father was dead. A few years after that, his mother was gone too.

In 1924, nineteen years old and alone, he decided to try America a second time. He had no visa. So he slipped aboard a Cuban fishing boat bound for Florida, in the company of a friend from home named Peter Magaddino.

Remember that name. Forty years later, the Magaddinos will be at the center of this story.

Bonanno landed in Tampa, made his way north, and found work in the business that ran New York in the age of Prohibition: illegal liquor. His protector was a fellow Castellammarese, an educated, ambitious boss named Salvatore Maranzano.

In 1930, a war broke out between Maranzano and Joe Masseria, the strongest boss in the city. It became known as the Castellammarese War. Bonanno fought on Maranzano's side, and Maranzano won.

His victory lasted five months.

In September 1931, Maranzano was murdered in his own office. The killers were sent by a younger generation led by Lucky Luciano.

Luciano did not crown himself king. Instead, he built a board of directors: a Commission, where the bosses of the major families would settle disputes together, instead of with machine guns.

Maranzano's old crew needed a new leader. They chose Bonanno.

He was twenty-six years old. The youngest boss in America, with a seat on the ruling council of the biggest criminal organization the country had ever seen.

CHAPTER TWO. Thirty years on top.

For three decades, Joseph Bonanno did something rare in his world. He survived.

He married Fay Labruzzo two months after taking power, and raised three children. He invested in cheese companies, garment factories and real estate. To his neighbors, he looked like a prosperous businessman.

The newspapers had another name for him: Joe Bananas. He hated it. He saw himself as an aristocrat of the old Sicilian tradition, a man of rules, of loyalty, of silence.

Under him, the organization grew far beyond Brooklyn. It reached into Montreal, where heroin and gambling money flowed across the border. It reached all the way to Tucson, Arizona, where Bonanno bought a home in the desert and spent his winters away from New York.

In 1956, his son Bill married Rosalie Profaci, the niece of Joe Profaci, boss of another New York family. The reception filled the Hotel Astor in Times Square. It was a wedding, and it was also an alliance between two crime clans.

But by the early 1960s, the world that made Bonanno was disappearing.

Lucky Luciano died in exile in Naples in 1962. Vito Genovese was locked in a federal prison. Joe Profaci died of cancer the same year as Luciano.

Of the original bosses who sat on the Commission in 1931, Bonanno was almost the last one left.

And the newer men on the Commission did not trust him.

Carlo Gambino. Tommy Lucchese. And in Buffalo, the boss of western New York, Stefano Magaddino. Bonanno's own cousin, a relative of the same Peter Magaddino who had crossed the ocean with him decades earlier.

They watched him plant flags in Canada, in Arizona, in California. To them, it looked like a man building an empire.

In the autumn of 1963, while Bonanno's rivals counted his moves, a former soldier named Joe Valachi sat before the United States Senate. The man from our last file. On live television, he drew a chart of the five New York families.

One of the names on that chart was Bonanno.

CHAPTER THREE. The plot.

To this day, what Bonanno was planning in 1963 is told in two very different ways.

According to the Commission, and to most historians, it was a plan to kill.

The targets were the bosses who stood in his way: Carlo Gambino, Tommy Lucchese, Stefano Magaddino, and Frank DeSimone, the boss of Los Angeles. With them gone, Bonanno would be the undisputed master of the American underworld.

He could not do it alone. He needed a partner.

He found one in Joseph Magliocco, the heavy, sickly man who had taken over the Profaci crew after Joe Profaci's death. Magliocco had his own grudges against the Commission. He agreed.

The contract was handed to one of Magliocco's most trusted captains, a young, ambitious mobster named Joe Colombo.

It was the biggest mistake of their lives.

Colombo looked at the plan, and he did the math. Killing the bosses of the Commission was a gamble. Betraying the plot to them was a promotion.

He went straight to the targets, and told them everything.

The Commission summoned Magliocco and Bonanno to explain themselves.

Magliocco came. He was ill, frightened, and out of options. He confessed. The bosses decided that the real mind behind the plot was not his, and they spared his life. He paid a fine of fifty thousand dollars, and he was ordered to step down.

His crew went to the man who had betrayed him: Joe Colombo. The Profaci family would one day carry his name.

Magliocco did not enjoy his retirement. Within months, his heart gave out.

Bonanno did not come at all.

CHAPTER FOUR. A king without a crown.

In the Mafia, ignoring the Commission is not a small thing. It is a declaration.

Bonanno's answer was to leave the country.

In 1964 he went to Montreal, where his organization had deep roots, and he applied to become a Canadian citizen. The Canadian authorities had other plans. He was arrested, held for almost three months in Montreal's Bordeaux Prison, and finally forced to leave.

He came home to a different world.

In his absence, the Commission had taken the only step it had left. Joe Bonanno was no longer boss of his own men. In his place, the bosses recognized one of his captains, a quiet, older man named Gaspar DiGregorio.

DiGregorio had been best man at Bonanno's wedding. Now he sat in his chair.

At the same time, the federal government wanted to speak with him too. A grand jury in Manhattan was investigating organized crime, and Bonanno was ordered to testify.

So by October 1964, Joe Bonanno was trapped between two courts. The Mafia had stripped him of his crown. The United States wanted him under oath.

On the night of October twentieth, he went out to dinner with his lawyers, to prepare for his testimony.

The next morning, he was supposed to be in court.

---

CHAPTER FIVE. The night on Park Avenue.

The dinner ran late. When the group finally stepped outside, it was nearly midnight, and a cold drizzle was coming down over Manhattan.

Bonanno shared a taxi with two of his lawyers. One of them, William Power Maloney, lived on Park Avenue, at Thirty-Sixth Street. Bonanno was going to spend the night there.

The cab stopped. Bonanno and Maloney got out.

According to Maloney, two men appeared from the shadows. One took Bonanno by the arm and said, "Come on, Joe. My boss wants to see you."

Maloney protested. One of the men fired a shot into the sidewalk, and the lawyer dove for cover. When he looked again, Bonanno had been pushed into a waiting car, and the car was gone.

The next day, the story was everywhere. The newspapers had a word for it that sounded almost absurd: a Mafia boss, kidnapped.

The FBI started looking. So did the police. So did his own soldiers.

Years later, Bonanno would tell his own version. He said the men ordered him to crouch on the floor of the car. He felt it turn and turn, then speed onto a highway and cross the George Washington Bridge into New Jersey.

He said the men in the car were his own relatives: Nino Magaddino, and Peter Magaddino, the son of the Buffalo boss.

He said they took him to a farmhouse somewhere in the countryside, and kept him there for weeks.

And he said the man behind it all was the one boss who knew him better than anyone.

In 1983, on national television, a reporter asked him who had kidnapped him. Bonanno answered with three words.

"My dear cousin."

---

CHAPTER SIX. Nineteen months.

Whatever happened in that farmhouse, the story did not end there.

By his own account, his captors let him go after several weeks. But Joe Bonanno did not go home. He did not call the police. He did not show up in court.

He simply disappeared.

Weeks became months. Months became more than a year. Rumors spread that he was dead, buried somewhere upstate, or lying at the bottom of the ocean.

Meanwhile, his empire was tearing itself apart.

On one side stood Gaspar DiGregorio, the boss the Commission had chosen. On the other stood the men still loyal to Bonanno, led by his son, Bill.

The press found a name for the conflict that the gangsters themselves never used. They called it the Banana War.

In January 1966, DiGregorio sent word that he wanted peace. A meeting was arranged at a house on Troutman Street in Brooklyn.

It was a trap.

As Bill Bonanno and his men walked toward the house, gunmen opened fire from the windows and the street. By some accounts, hundreds of rounds were fired that night.

Not one man was hit.

Four months later, on a spring morning in May 1966, a well-dressed man walked into the federal courthouse at Foley Square in Manhattan, with a lawyer at his side.

It was Joe Bonanno. Alive. Healthy. And ready to explain that he had been kidnapped.

Almost nobody believed him.

The Magaddinos, the investigators pointed out, had been under FBI surveillance during that time. Most historians would come to the same conclusion: there probably was no kidnapping at all. It was a disappearing act, staged to escape both the grand jury and the Commission.

Even his son described those weeks in a strange way. Not as a nightmare, but as a kind of rest, a stay as a guest of his cousin.

---

CHAPTER SEVEN. The bombs in the desert.

His return did not end the fighting. It made it hotter.

Over the next two years, men from both sides were shot in Brooklyn and Queens. DiGregorio was wounded in a burst of machine-gun fire, then fell ill. The Commission replaced him with another of its own choices, Paul Sciacca.

And then, in 1968, the decision was made for Bonanno by his own body. He suffered a heart attack.

He sent a message to the Commission. He would retire. He would leave New York, and move permanently to his home in Tucson, Arizona. The bosses accepted. After four years, the Banana War was over.

But the desert did not bring him peace.

On the night of July twenty-first, 1968, an explosion tore through a garage on a ranch outside Tucson, owned by Detroit boss Peter Licavoli. The next night, two bombs exploded outside Joe Bonanno's home, knocking down a wall of his patio.

Over the following months, more bombs went off across Tucson, targeting men connected to the Mafia. No one was killed, but the message was clear. Someone wanted a new war.

When the bombers were finally caught, the truth was stranger than any mob story.

Testimony pointed to the man behind the campaign: an FBI agent. David Hale, the bureau's own organized crime specialist in Tucson. According to the witnesses, his plan was simple: make the mobsters believe they were attacking each other, and let them finish the job.

Some of the men who planted the bombs were convicted. Hale left the bureau. He never faced trial.

The old boss had survived the Commission, a mob war, and the federal government. Now he had survived the FBI's own private war.

---

CHAPTER EIGHT. The book that broke the Commission.

In his old age, Joe Bonanno decided to do something no boss of his rank had ever done.

In 1983, he published his memoirs. He called the book A Man of Honor.

In it, he described the Mafia's history and traditions. And he wrote openly about the Commission, the ruling council of bosses, its members, its rules, its meetings.

He went on 60 Minutes. He sat across from Mike Wallace and talked about his life, without ever admitting to a crime.

In New York, a federal prosecutor named Rudy Giuliani read the book with great interest.

For decades, the government had struggled to prove that a national ruling body existed at all. Now a founding member had described it, in print, under his own name.

The book helped inspire one of the biggest prosecutions in American history: the Commission case. In 1986, the heads of several New York crime families were convicted, and sentenced to a hundred years in prison.

Giuliani wanted Bonanno to testify. Bonanno refused. At eighty years old, he was jailed for contempt of court, and spent fourteen months behind bars rather than break his silence.

He was released in 1986, and went home to the desert.

Joseph Bonanno died in Tucson, on May eleventh, 2002. He was ninety-seven years old. A man who once sat beside Luciano died in his own bed, decades after almost every rival.

He never changed his story. Until the end, he insisted that on that rainy night in 1964, he had been kidnapped.

So which was it? Was the boss who vanished really a prisoner? Or did he stage the most famous disappearance in Mafia history, and walk away laughing?
