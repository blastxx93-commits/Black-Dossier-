CHAPTER THREE. Cellmates.

In 1959, Vito Genovese and Vincent Gigante were convicted together in a federal heroin trafficking case. Genovese was sentenced to fifteen years. Gigante received seven.

They were sent to the same federal penitentiary. Mob historians say they even shared a cell.

For the former fighter, it was an education. Every day, he sat across from the boss of the most powerful crime family in America, and listened. How to give orders without leaving fingerprints. Never to say too much. And above all, to let other people take the blame.

Genovese would never walk free again. He died in federal custody in 1969, still sending orders to the outside through messengers.

Gigante was luckier. Roughly halfway through his sentence, he was paroled early. Residents of Greenwich Village had written to the judge asking for leniency. The neighborhood wanted its boy back.

He came home in the mid-1960s as a captain, with his own crew. The men under him ran gambling, loansharking and protection rackets across lower Manhattan, and a share of every dollar flowed upward.

His headquarters was a modest storefront on Sullivan Street, with a name that sounded almost like a charity: the Triangle Civic Improvement Association. Inside, old men played cards and drank espresso. Outside, the street was always watched.

From that little room, Gigante began to build his power, quietly, carefully, the way Genovese had taught him.

But a captain with a criminal record was an easy target for prosecutors. Sooner or later, they would come for him again.

And then, at the end of the 1960s, the neighbors began to notice something strange about Vincent Gigante.
