CHAPTER ONE. The boxer from Sullivan Street.

Vincent Gigante was born in New York City in March 1928, the son of immigrants from Naples. His father was a watchmaker. His mother, Yolanda, called him Cinzino. On the streets, the nickname became "Chin."

He grew up in Greenwich Village, south of Washington Square. In those days it was a maze of tenements, bakeries, churches and social clubs, where Italian families lived shoulder to shoulder, where the feast of Saint Anthony filled Sullivan Street every June, and where everybody knew who actually ran the block. The police were outsiders. The men in the social clubs were not.

He was one of five brothers. One of them, Louis, would grow up to become a Catholic priest in the Bronx. Several of the others would later be linked by investigators to the rackets. Vincent took the hardest path of all.

In that world, the boys who mattered were noticed early. They ran numbers for the bookmakers, carried messages, kept their mouths shut. A kid who could fight and could keep a secret was worth watching.

As a teenager, he was big, strong and fearless. He dropped out of school and became a professional boxer, a light heavyweight. Between 1944 and 1947 he climbed into the ring twenty-five times, and won twenty-one fights. Promoters liked his punch. Local gamblers liked him even more.

But the ring was never going to make him rich.

Someone else had his eye on him.

Vito Genovese was a senior figure in the organization once led by Lucky Luciano. Ruthless, patient and ambitious, he had fled to Italy in the 1930s to escape a murder charge, and came back after the war determined to take what he believed was his. When Gigante's mother needed surgery, it was Genovese who helped pay for it.

In that neighborhood, a favor like that was never free.

By the age of twenty-five, the former fighter had been arrested seven times, mostly for gambling and stolen goods. Almost every case ended with a dismissal or a small fine. He married in 1950 and started a family of his own. On paper, he was a small-time hood.

In reality, he was being groomed. Genovese liked men who were loyal before they were clever, and who never asked why. The young fighter fit perfectly.

Genovese had a problem, and the problem had a name. Frank Costello. The man who sat on the throne Genovese wanted for himself.

And in the spring of 1957, Genovese decided that the young man from Sullivan Street was ready to solve it.
