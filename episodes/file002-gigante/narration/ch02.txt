CHAPTER TWO. The shot on Central Park West.

Frank Costello was not a typical gangster. He wore tailored suits, dined with politicians, and spoke in a famously raspy voice. After Luciano was deported to Italy, Costello ran the organization as if it were a corporation: slot machines, nightclubs, casinos, and friends in City Hall. Judges and politicians took his calls.

In 1951, he was called before a Senate committee investigating organized crime. His lawyer insisted that his face could not be shown on television. So the cameras filmed his hands instead. Millions of Americans watched those nervous hands twist and fidget for hours, while that hoarse voice dodged question after question. At one point, he simply walked out. The hearing made Costello famous across the country. Inside the underworld, it made him look weak, and exposed.

Vito Genovese saw his chance.

On the night of May 2nd, 1957, Costello had dinner with friends in Manhattan, then took a cab home to the Majestic, overlooking the park. A Cadillac followed the taxi. Behind the wheel, it was later claimed, sat one of Genovese's loyal men. In the passenger seat sat Vincent Gigante.

Gigante walked into the lobby behind Costello, raised his gun and shouted, "This one's for you, Frank."

That shout probably saved Costello's life. He turned his head at the last second. The bullet grazed his skull, from behind his right ear to his neck.

Gigante thought he had killed him. He hadn't. Costello was bleeding, dizzy, but alive.

At the hospital, Costello told the police he had not seen anything. "I don't have an enemy in the world," he said.

But detectives found something in his pocket: a handwritten note, listing casino winnings that matched a new Las Vegas casino, the Tropicana. Costello refused to explain it, and went to jail for contempt.

As for the gunman, he vanished. Police searched for weeks. Months later, Gigante walked into a police station with his lawyer and turned himself in.

At the trial in 1958, the doorman of the Majestic pointed at Gigante as the shooter. Then Frank Costello took the stand, looked at the man who had tried to kill him, and said he could not recognize him.

The jury acquitted him.

Costello understood the message. He retired, and handed the family to Vito Genovese.

Genovese had his crown. Gigante had a reputation. But within a year, both men would be sharing something much smaller: a prison cell.
