CHAPTER SIX. The trial of the madman.

On May 30th, 1990, federal prosecutors indicted Vincent Gigante, along with members of four of New York's five families, in a scheme to rig contracts for replacing windows in public housing.

The government finally had its case. What it didn't have was a defendant.

Gigante's lawyers argued that he was far too sick to defend himself. They brought records of twenty-eight hospital stays between 1969 and 1995. Psychiatrists testified about his delusions, his confusion, his blank stare.

The case dragged on and on. Hearings. Experts. Reports. More hearings. Doctors for the defense described a man who heard voices. Doctors for the government described a man who knew exactly what he was doing. Meanwhile, the other defendants went to trial without him.

Then, in 1996, the government brought two witnesses who had sat in the same rooms as Gigante.

One was Alphonse D'Arco, who had briefly served as acting head of the Lucchese clan. The other was Salvatore "Sammy the Bull" Gravano, John Gotti's former underboss, who had turned against Gotti and become the most famous Mafia informant in America. His testimony had already helped send Gotti to prison for life in 1992. Now he was pointing at another boss.

Gravano described meetings where the so-called madman discussed business, money and murder without hesitation. D'Arco said the same. Neither had ever seen him confused when it mattered.

Both men told the court the same thing. At top-level meetings, the supposed madman was sharp, focused and perfectly lucid. And according to their testimony, he had privately described his behavior as an act.

In August 1996, a federal judge ruled that Gigante was competent. After seven years, he would finally face a jury.

In June 1997, the trial finally began. Gigante arrived in a wheelchair, gaunt, silent, and looking far older than sixty-nine.

The jurors were not convinced. On July 25th, 1997, Gigante was convicted of racketeering and of conspiring to murder rival mobsters.

In December, the judge sentenced him to twelve years in prison and a fine of one million two hundred and fifty thousand dollars. He said that Gigante had finally been "brought to bay in his declining years, after decades of vicious criminal tyranny."

He was acquitted of three of the murders he was accused of plotting, and the jury could not agree on others. But for the first time in his life, the act had failed in front of twelve ordinary New Yorkers.

But even behind bars, the act did not stop.

And neither, prosecutors believed, did his control of the family.
