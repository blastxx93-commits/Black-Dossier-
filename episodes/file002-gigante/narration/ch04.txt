CHAPTER FOUR. The man in the bathrobe.

In 1969, Gigante was indicted in New Jersey, accused of trying to bribe the entire police force of a small town called Old Tappan.

It should have been a simple case. Instead, it collapsed.

Psychiatrists examined the defendant and reported that he was mentally unfit to face a jury. His psychiatrist recorded a diagnosis that would follow him for the rest of his life: schizophrenia, paranoid type. His family said his IQ was somewhere around seventy.

The charges were dropped.

And Vincent Gigante changed.

He began walking the streets of Greenwich Village in a ratty bathrobe, pajamas and slippers, sometimes with a floppy hat. He mumbled to himself. He stared into space. Witnesses saw him talking to parking meters, and relieving himself in the street.

He checked into psychiatric hospitals again and again, more than twenty times over the next two decades, often just before a court date or a grand jury appearance. Each stay produced new medical reports. Each report made the next prosecution harder.

The tabloids gave him a new name: the Odd Father.

According to one widely repeated story, detectives once arrived to serve him papers and found him standing naked in the shower, holding an open umbrella over his head.

To most people, it looked like a tragedy. A former athlete, destroyed by mental illness. Neighbors felt sorry for him. His mother told anyone who would listen that her son was sick.

But inside the Mafia, a different set of rules applied.

His soldiers were forbidden to say his name out loud, on the phone or in the street. When they needed to talk about him, they simply touched their chin.

At night, according to investigators, the shuffling figure in slippers was driven uptown to an elegant townhouse, where he lived another life with a second family.

Agents watched him for decades. They photographed the bathrobe, the slippers, the mumbling. They never caught him slipping out of character in public.

And when it came to real business, investigators said, he was careful to the point of obsession. He almost never used a telephone. Important conversations happened in whispers, on walks, or behind closed doors, often in his mother's apartment. The bugs and wiretaps that destroyed so many other bosses picked up almost nothing.

The FBI had a phrase for him: crazy like a fox.

But knowing it and proving it were two completely different things. And while the government argued with his doctors, the madman of Sullivan Street was quietly climbing to the very top.
