May 2nd, 1957. Central Park West, New York. Five minutes to eleven at night.

A black Cadillac pulls up behind a taxi. A big man in a dark suit climbs out and follows a well-dressed gentleman into the lobby of an apartment building called the Majestic.

The gentleman is Frank Costello. The best-connected gangster in America. The press calls him the Prime Minister of the Underworld.

The big man raises a revolver.

"This one's for you, Frank."

One shot. Costello falls. The gunman runs back to the Cadillac, and the car disappears into the night.

The shooter is twenty-nine years old. A former boxer. His name is Vincent Gigante.

Now jump forward three decades.

The same man is shuffling down a street in Greenwich Village, in a bathrobe and slippers, mumbling to himself. He talks to parking meters. Psychiatrists say he is a paranoid schizophrenic. His family says he has the mind of a child.

And the FBI says he is the real ruler of the largest Mafia organization in the United States.

For three decades, Vincent Gigante fooled doctors, judges and juries. Some of his own soldiers were not allowed to say his name.

How could a man in pajamas give orders to hundreds of gangsters? Was he ever really sick? And what did he finally say in a courtroom in 2003 that stunned everyone who had been watching him all that time?

This is Black Dossier. And this is the file of the boss who played crazy.

CHAPTER ONE. The boxer from Sullivan Street.

Vincent Gigante was born in New York City in March 1928, the son of immigrants from Naples. His father was a watchmaker. His mother, Yolanda, called him Cinzino. On the streets, the nickname became "Chin."

He grew up in Greenwich Village, south of Washington Square. In those days it was a maze of tenements, bakeries, churches and social clubs, where Italian families lived shoulder to shoulder, where the feast of Saint Anthony filled Sullivan Street every June, and where everybody knew who actually ran the block. The police were outsiders. The men in the social clubs were not.

He was one of five brothers. One of them, Louis, would grow up to become a Catholic priest in the Bronx. Several of the others would later be linked by investigators to the rackets. Vincent took the hardest path of all.

In that world, the boys who mattered were noticed early. They ran numbers for the bookmakers, carried messages, kept their mouths shut. A kid who could fight and could keep a secret was worth watching.

As a teenager, he was big, strong and fearless. He dropped out of school and became a professional boxer, a light heavyweight. Between 1944 and 1947 he climbed into the ring twenty-five times, and won twenty-one fights. Promoters liked his punch. Local gamblers liked him even more.

But the ring was never going to make him rich.

Someone else had his eye on him.

Vito Genovese was a senior figure in the organization once led by Lucky Luciano. Ruthless, patient and ambitious, he had fled to Italy in the 1930s to escape a murder charge, and came back after the war determined to take what he believed was his. When Gigante's mother needed surgery, it was Genovese who helped pay for it.

In that neighborhood, a favor like that was never free.

By the age of twenty-five, the former fighter had been arrested seven times, mostly for gambling and stolen goods. Almost every case ended with a dismissal or a small fine. He married in 1950 and started a family of his own. On paper, he was a small-time hood.

In reality, he was being groomed. Genovese liked men who were loyal before they were clever, and who never asked why. The young fighter fit perfectly.

Genovese had a problem, and the problem had a name. Frank Costello. The man who sat on the throne Genovese wanted for himself.

And in the spring of 1957, Genovese decided that the young man from Sullivan Street was ready to solve it.

---

CHAPTER TWO. The shot on Central Park West.

Frank Costello was not a typical gangster. He wore tailored suits, dined with politicians, and spoke in a famously raspy voice. After Luciano was deported to Italy, Costello ran the organization as if it were a corporation: slot machines, nightclubs, casinos, and friends in City Hall. Judges and politicians took his calls.

In 1951, he was called before a Senate committee investigating organized crime. His lawyer insisted that his face could not be shown on television. So the cameras filmed his hands instead. Millions of Americans watched those nervous hands twist and fidget for hours, while that hoarse voice dodged question after question. At one point, he simply walked out. The hearing made Costello famous across the country. Inside the underworld, it made him look weak, and exposed.

Vito Genovese saw his chance.

On the night of May 2nd, 1957, Costello had dinner with friends in Manhattan, then took a cab home to the Majestic, overlooking the park. A Cadillac followed the taxi. Behind the wheel, it was later claimed, sat one of Genovese's loyal men. In the passenger seat sat Vincent Gigante.

Gigante walked into the lobby behind Costello, raised his gun and shouted, "This one's for you, Frank."

That shout probably saved Costello's life. He turned his head at the last second. The bullet grazed his skull, from behind his right ear to his neck.

Gigante thought he had killed him. He hadn't. Costello was bleeding, dizzy, but alive.

At the hospital, Costello told the police he had not seen anything. "I don't have an enemy in the world," he said.

But detectives found something in his pocket: a handwritten note, listing casino winnings that matched a new Las Vegas casino, the Tropicana. Costello refused to explain it, and went to jail for contempt.

As for the gunman, he vanished. Police searched for weeks. Months later, Gigante walked into a police station with his lawyer and turned himself in.

At the trial in 1958, the doorman of the Majestic pointed at Gigante as the shooter. Then Frank Costello took the stand, looked at the man who had tried to kill him, and said he could not recognize him.

The jury acquitted him.

Costello understood the message. He retired, and handed the family to Vito Genovese.

Genovese had his crown. Gigante had a reputation. But within a year, both men would be sharing something much smaller: a prison cell.

---

CHAPTER THREE. Cellmates.

Genovese did not enjoy his victory for long. In November 1957, just six months after the shooting at the Majestic, state police stumbled onto a gathering of dozens of Mafia leaders at a country estate in Apalachin, New York. Genovese was one of the men caught there. Suddenly, the government could no longer pretend the Mafia did not exist.

The pressure came fast. In 1959, Vito Genovese and Vincent Gigante were convicted together in a federal heroin trafficking case. Genovese was sentenced to fifteen years. Gigante received seven.

Some historians believe the case had been arranged by Genovese's own rivals, men who were happy to see him behind bars. Whether or not that is true, the result was the same.

They were sent to the same federal penitentiary. Mob historians say they even shared a cell.

For the former fighter, it was an education. Every day, he sat across from the boss of the most powerful crime family in America, and listened. How to give orders without leaving fingerprints. Never to say too much. And above all, to let other people take the blame.

Genovese would never walk free again. He died in federal custody in 1969, still sending orders to the outside through messengers.

Gigante was luckier. Roughly halfway through his sentence, he was paroled early. Residents of Greenwich Village had written to the judge asking for leniency. The neighborhood wanted its boy back.

He came home in the mid-1960s as a captain, with his own crew. The men under him ran gambling, loansharking and protection rackets across lower Manhattan, and a share of every dollar flowed upward.

His headquarters was a modest storefront on Sullivan Street, with a name that sounded almost like a charity: the Triangle Civic Improvement Association. Inside, old men played cards and drank espresso. Outside, the street was always watched.

From that little room, Gigante began to build his power, quietly, carefully, the way Genovese had taught him.

But a captain with a criminal record was an easy target for prosecutors. Sooner or later, they would come for him again.

And then, at the end of the 1960s, the neighbors began to notice something strange about Vincent Gigante.

---

CHAPTER FOUR. The man in the bathrobe.

In 1969, Gigante was indicted in New Jersey, accused of trying to bribe the entire police force of a small town called Old Tappan.

It should have been a simple case. Instead, it collapsed.

Psychiatrists examined the defendant and reported that he was mentally unfit to face a jury. His psychiatrist recorded a diagnosis that would follow him for the rest of his life: schizophrenia, paranoid type. His family said his IQ was somewhere around seventy.

The charges were dropped.

And Vincent Gigante changed.

He began walking the streets of Greenwich Village in a ratty bathrobe, pajamas and slippers, sometimes with a floppy hat. He mumbled to himself. He stared into space. Witnesses saw him talking to parking meters, and relieving himself in the street.

He checked into psychiatric hospitals again and again, more than twenty times over the next two decades, often just before a court date or a grand jury appearance. Each stay produced new medical reports. Each report made the next prosecution harder.

The tabloids gave him a new name: the Odd Father.

According to one widely repeated story, detectives once arrived to serve him papers and found him standing naked in the shower, holding an open umbrella over his head.

To most people, it looked like a tragedy. A former athlete, destroyed by mental illness. Neighbors felt sorry for him. His mother told anyone who would listen that her son was sick.

But inside the Mafia, a different set of rules applied.

His soldiers were forbidden to say his name out loud, on the phone or in the street. When they needed to talk about him, they simply touched their chin.

At night, according to investigators, the shuffling figure in slippers was driven uptown to an elegant townhouse, where he lived another life with a second family.

Agents watched him for decades. They photographed the bathrobe, the slippers, the mumbling. They never caught him slipping out of character in public.

And when it came to real business, investigators said, he was careful to the point of obsession. He almost never used a telephone. Important conversations happened in whispers, on walks, or behind closed doors, often in his mother's apartment. The bugs and wiretaps that destroyed so many other bosses picked up almost nothing.

The FBI had a phrase for him: crazy like a fox.

But knowing it and proving it were two completely different things. And while the government argued with his doctors, the madman of Sullivan Street was quietly climbing to the very top.

CHAPTER FIVE. The boss nobody could name.

By 1980, Gigante had risen to consigliere, the adviser to the boss. A year later, when the old leader stepped down for health reasons, Gigante took control of the Genovese organization.

But he did something no one outside the family expected. He did not take the title in public.

Instead, the world was shown a front man: Anthony "Fat Tony" Salerno, a cigar-chewing veteran who sat in a social club in East Harlem and looked every bit like a Mafia boss. Investigators photographed him, followed him, and planted listening devices in his club, the Palma Boys Social Club. Hour after hour, the tapes recorded Fat Tony talking business, cursing, giving orders. To the FBI, it sounded like the voice of the boss himself.

It was exactly what Gigante wanted.

In 1986, in one of the biggest prosecutions in American history, Fat Tony Salerno was convicted as its supposed leader, and later sentenced to one hundred years in prison.

The real chief kept shuffling down Sullivan Street in his robe, untouched.

Only later did a Genovese mobster named Vincent Cafaro turn informant and tell the FBI the truth. Salerno had been a front all along. The shuffling madman was the one in charge.

By then, Gigante had a new enemy.

On December 16th, 1985, Gambino boss Paul Castellano stepped out of his car outside Sparks Steak House in Manhattan, during the evening rush, a few days before Christmas. Gunmen in matching trench coats and fur hats were waiting. Castellano and his driver were shot dead on the sidewalk. The man behind the murder was an ambitious captain named John Gotti, who then took over the Gambino organization.

Killing a boss without the approval of the Commission broke the oldest rule in the Mafia. Gotti had done it anyway, and then he had done something even worse in Gigante's eyes: he had become a celebrity. Expensive suits, cameras everywhere, a smile for every photographer. The Dapper Don was everything the Chin was not. And Gigante wanted Gotti dead for it.

On April 13th, 1986, a car bomb exploded in Brooklyn. It killed Frank DeCicco, Gotti's new underboss. According to later testimony, the bomb had been ordered by Gigante together with the leader of the Lucchese clan, and it was meant for Gotti too.

But Gotti had canceled at the last minute.

He survived. And before long, it would be Gotti's own right-hand man who walked into a courtroom and told the world what the Chin truly was.

---

CHAPTER SIX. The trial of the madman.

On May 30th, 1990, federal prosecutors indicted Vincent Gigante, along with members of four of New York's five families, in a scheme to rig contracts for replacing windows in public housing.

The government finally had its case. What it didn't have was a defendant.

Gigante's lawyers argued that he was far too sick to defend himself. They brought records of twenty-eight hospital stays between 1969 and 1995. Psychiatrists testified about his delusions, his confusion, his blank stare.

The case dragged on and on. Hearings. Experts. Reports. More hearings. Doctors for the defense described a man who heard voices. Doctors for the government described a man who knew exactly what he was doing. Meanwhile, the other defendants went to trial without him.

Then, in 1996, the government brought two witnesses who had sat in the same rooms as Gigante.

One was Alphonse D'Arco, who had briefly served as acting head of the Lucchese clan. The other was Salvatore "Sammy the Bull" Gravano, John Gotti's former underboss, who had turned against Gotti and become the most famous Mafia informant in America. His testimony had already helped send Gotti to prison for life in 1992. Now he was pointing at another boss.

Gravano described meetings where the so-called madman discussed business, money and murder without hesitation. D'Arco said the same. Neither had ever seen him confused when it mattered.

Both men told the court the same thing. At top-level meetings, the supposed madman was sharp, focused and perfectly lucid. And according to their testimony, he had privately described his behavior as an act.

In August 1996, a federal judge ruled that Gigante was competent. After seven years, he would finally face a jury.

In June 1997, the trial finally began. Gigante arrived in a wheelchair, gaunt, silent, and looking far older than sixty-nine.

The jurors were not convinced. On July 25th, 1997, Gigante was convicted of racketeering and of conspiring to murder rival mobsters.

In December, the judge sentenced him to twelve years in prison and a fine of one million two hundred and fifty thousand dollars. He said that Gigante had finally been "brought to bay in his declining years, after decades of vicious criminal tyranny."

He was acquitted of three of the murders he was accused of plotting, and the jury could not agree on others. But for the first time in his life, the act had failed in front of twelve ordinary New Yorkers.

But even behind bars, the act did not stop.

And neither, prosecutors believed, did his control of the family.

---

CHAPTER SEVEN. The confession.

In January 2002, the government struck again. Prosecutors indicted Gigante and his son, Andrew, accusing the imprisoned don of still running the organization from his cell, passing orders through relatives during visits.

And this time, they had something new: recordings of the old man speaking clearly and coherently, giving instructions, sounding nothing like a confused old man.

On April 7th, 2003, hours before his new trial was set to begin, Gigante stood before a federal judge in Brooklyn and pleaded guilty to obstruction of justice.

And then he admitted what prosecutors had been saying for decades.

The madness had been an act.

The bathrobe, the mumbling, the blank stares during psychiatric exams. A performance, designed to keep him out of a courtroom.

After thirty-four years, the man in the bathrobe had broken character.

The prosecutor called him "a cunning faker." The journalist Selwyn Raab, one of the leading historians of the American Mafia, called the plea an unprecedented surrender for a Mafia boss.

His lawyer, Benjamin Brafman, insisted that the plea did not mean his client had no real mental problems at all. The truth, he suggested, was more complicated.

The judge added three more years to his time in prison.

He never left prison again. He died on December 19th, 2005, at the federal medical center in Springfield, Missouri. He was seventy-seven.

His funeral was held at Saint Anthony of Padua, a church in his old neighborhood, a few blocks from the little club on Sullivan Street.

A former FBI supervisor once described him as probably the most clever organized crime figure he had ever seen.

Think about what the act bought him. While other bosses were caught on tape, betrayed by their own soldiers and buried under evidence, Vincent Gigante stayed free for most of his adult life. He ran his empire for over fifteen years as boss. He watched rivals go to prison, and allies die. He outlasted Frank Costello, Vito Genovese, Salerno and Paul Castellano. And when John Gotti, the Dapper Don, was sentenced to life, the Chin was still shuffling along Sullivan Street.

The price was his own name, his own face, his own sanity in the eyes of the world. He spent decades being laughed at, pitied, and photographed in his pajamas. For him, it seems, that was a fair trade.

The young fighter who fired a single shot in that lobby in 1957 spent the rest of his life pretending to be someone else. And for more than three decades, it worked.

He was never convicted for the shooting of Frank Costello. He was never convicted for the bomb that killed Frank DeCicco. In the end, what finally brought him down was not a bullet or a witness. It was his own voice, on tape, sounding perfectly sane.
