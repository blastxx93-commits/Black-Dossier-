So how big is the diesel ute problem? Let's look at the numbers.

According to September 2026 sales data reported by carsales, Australians bought 184,328 light commercial vehicles in the first nine months of 2026. 155,040 of them were diesel – that's about eighty-four per cent. Electric light commercials? Just 1,630.

The 4x4 ute market is also shrinking. It was down 13.6 per cent, to 140,410 sales. The Ford Ranger 4x4 led with 35,744, ahead of the HiLux 4x4 on 32,238, which was down 6.2 per cent.

And Toyota points out who's buying these vehicles. Nearly fifty-four per cent of HiLux and LandCruiser buyers live outside major cities. Pappas argues that for regional and rural Australians, with long distances and limited charging, diesel is still the practical choice. That's the heart of Toyota's argument for a softer target.

Meanwhile, the wider market is changing fast. The Australian Automobile Association reported that battery electric and plug-in hybrid vehicles made up 31.61 per cent of new light vehicle sales in the June quarter of 2026 – up from 19.07 per cent in the March quarter. Passenger cars are going electric quickly. In fact, CarExpert reported that electric vehicles outsold both petrol and diesel vehicles in August and September 2026. Utes are the part of the market that's furthest behind.

To be fair to Toyota, it says it supports the NVES and wants it to be "fair and equitable". It points out it has spent more than twenty years selling hybrids, and that its strategy is what it calls "multi-pathway" – hybrid, petrol, diesel, electric and eventually hydrogen, depending on the market. Pappas described diesel as "the right tool of trade for regional and rural Australia". The argument isn't really about whether to cut emissions. It's about how fast utes can realistically get there.
