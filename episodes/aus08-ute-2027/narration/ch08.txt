And then there's the politics. Because the future of these rules isn't settled.

On the 29th of September 2026, the federal Coalition pledged to repeal the NVES if it wins government. Shadow energy minister Dan Tehan called it a "family car and ute tax", and said a repeal bill would be introduced within thirty days of taking office. The Coalition argues the rules have affected vehicle prices and choice, but carsales noted it hasn't put a figure on how much repeal would reduce showroom prices.

Supporters of the NVES see it very differently. The Climate Council argued that scrapping it would limit access to more efficient vehicles and increase Australia's reliance on imported oil. Farmers for Climate Action said repeal could reduce future choices for farmers, including electric utes.

And the car industry? The Federal Chamber of Automotive Industries, which represents car makers, said it needs "stable, predictable and workable policy settings" – because vehicle decisions are made years in advance.

So right now, the NVES is law, it's under review, and the opposition wants to scrap it. For ute buyers, that means uncertainty. Prices could rise in 2027 if the targets stay as they are. The targets could be softened by the review. Or the whole scheme could be repealed after the next federal election. Nobody can tell you for sure which way it'll go.
