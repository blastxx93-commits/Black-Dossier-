So who pays? That's the big question.

Think of it like a seesaw. On one side, every vehicle a brand sells that's below the target adds credits. On the other side, every vehicle above the target adds penalties. A brand that sells lots of hybrids and EVs can balance out a lot of diesel utes. A brand that sells almost nothing but diesel utes has nothing on the other side of the seesaw.

Car makers have a few options. They can sell more hybrids and electric vehicles to earn credits. They can buy credits from other brands that have too many. Or they can pay the penalty – and potentially pass some of that cost on to buyers.

We already know who's ahead and who's behind. In February 2026, NRMA reported the first results, for the second half of 2025. On the penalty side, Mazda's liability was around $25.4 million, Nissan's around $10.7 million, Subaru's almost $7 million and Hyundai's about $4.2 million. On the credit side, BYD had more than six million credits, Toyota nearly 2.9 million and Tesla more than 2.2 million.

Toyota is in an interesting position. Overall, it's earning credits, thanks to all those hybrid RAV4s, Corollas and Camrys. But its utes and big four-wheel drives are a different story. And under the 2027 targets, even Toyota's hybrid success may not be enough to cover the gap on the commercial side.

That's why Pappas said Toyota hasn't ruled out buying credits from rivals – but that the company wants to be "self-managed as much as possible". And it's why Toyota says it will use the government's review of the NVES to push for what it calls a more "pragmatic" ute target.

And Toyota isn't alone. According to CarExpert, the NVES has already contributed to price increases on the Ford Ranger and the petrol Ford Mustang. And Mazda, Isuzu and Ram have also warned of higher prices under the scheme.

Isuzu is in a particularly tough spot. Its whole local range is the diesel D-Max and MU-X. Back in 2025, Isuzu's Australian managing director, Junta Matsui, said the company would find a way to survive, balancing what Australians need from a vehicle with the emissions targets. Isuzu has studied a mild hybrid, a smaller diesel and an electric version. But in 2026, it cancelled its plans to bring the electric D-Max to Australia, with Isuzu's Rod Caldwell saying simply: "Customers don't want it."

One more thing worth knowing: the first actual penalty bills, for vehicles sold in 2025, aren't due to be determined until February 2028. Car makers have time to trade credits before then. So nobody knows yet exactly how much will be passed on to buyers.
