First, let's understand the rule that's causing all of this. It's called the New Vehicle Efficiency Standard, or NVES.

It started on the 1st of January 2025, and car makers began earning credits and penalties from the 1st of July 2025. The idea is simple. Every car brand gets an average carbon dioxide target for all the vehicles it sells in Australia each year. Sell cleaner cars, and you earn credits. Sell dirtier ones, and you build up penalties.

There are two separate targets. One for passenger cars and SUVs. And a different one for light commercial vehicles – that means utes, vans and some big four-wheel drives.

And here's the key part: the targets get tougher every single year.

For passenger cars, the target is 117 grams of carbon dioxide per kilometre in 2026. In 2027 it drops to 92. Then 68 in 2028, and 58 in 2029.

For utes and vans, the target is 180 grams per kilometre in 2026. In 2027, it drops to 150. Then 122 in 2028. And 110 in 2029.

That jump from 180 to 150 is the one Toyota is worried about. It's a cut of about seventeen per cent in just one year. And the vast majority of utes sold in Australia are diesel – which means most of them are well above that line.

As Pappas put it: "The big challenge comes from 2027 when it comes to light commercial vehicles."

And timing matters here. The law requires a review of the NVES to begin before the end of 2026. The current targets run through to 2029, and that review will help decide what happens after that – and whether anything changes along the way. So the next twelve months are when the future of these rules gets decided.
