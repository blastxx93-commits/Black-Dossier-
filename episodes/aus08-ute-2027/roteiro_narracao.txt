### ch01
First, let's understand the rule that's causing all of this. It's called the New Vehicle Efficiency Standard, or NVES.

It started on the 1st of January 2025, and car makers began earning credits and penalties from the 1st of July 2025. The idea is simple. Every car brand gets an average carbon dioxide target for all the vehicles it sells in Australia each year. Sell cleaner cars, and you earn credits. Sell dirtier ones, and you build up penalties.

There are two separate targets. One for passenger cars and SUVs. And a different one for light commercial vehicles – that means utes, vans and some big four-wheel drives.

And here's the key part: the targets get tougher every single year.

For passenger cars, the target is 117 grams of carbon dioxide per kilometre in 2026. In 2027 it drops to 92. Then 68 in 2028, and 58 in 2029.

For utes and vans, the target is 180 grams per kilometre in 2026. In 2027, it drops to 150. Then 122 in 2028. And 110 in 2029.

That jump from 180 to 150 is the one Toyota is worried about. It's a cut of about seventeen per cent in just one year. And the vast majority of utes sold in Australia are diesel – which means most of them are well above that line.

As Pappas put it: "The big challenge comes from 2027 when it comes to light commercial vehicles."

And timing matters here. The law requires a review of the NVES to begin before the end of 2026. The current targets run through to 2029, and that review will help decide what happens after that – and whether anything changes along the way. So the next twelve months are when the future of these rules gets decided.

### ch02
So how does the penalty actually work?

Roughly speaking, a car maker pays about fifty dollars for every gram per kilometre a vehicle is over the target, for every vehicle it sells. The real formula is more complicated, because it's based on the brand's whole fleet, and cleaner vehicles can earn credits to offset dirtier ones. But fifty dollars per gram is the number to remember.

Let's take a real example. CarsGuide reports that the HiLux SR5 with Toyota's V-Active mild hybrid diesel is claimed at 191 grams per kilometre. That's already above the 2026 limit of 180. CarsGuide estimated the penalty for a HiLux like that at around $550 per vehicle in 2026.

Now, here's a rough calculation of my own – and it's only an illustration, not an official figure. In 2027, the limit drops to 150. A ute at 191 grams would then be 41 grams over. At fifty dollars a gram, that's around two thousand dollars per vehicle, before any credits. And by 2029, when the target is 110, the same ute would be about eighty grams over – roughly four thousand dollars.

In January 2026, carsales ran its own theoretical numbers for Ford. It estimated the combined penalty for the Ranger and Everest could work out at around $3,706 per vehicle if it was passed straight on to buyers – and that Ford's total theoretical bill could be well over a billion dollars by 2029 if its sales mix didn't change. Carsales was clear that these were assumptions, not predictions, because car makers will change what they sell. But it shows the scale of the problem.

### ch03
So who pays? That's the big question.

Think of it like a seesaw. On one side, every vehicle a brand sells that's below the target adds credits. On the other side, every vehicle above the target adds penalties. A brand that sells lots of hybrids and EVs can balance out a lot of diesel utes. A brand that sells almost nothing but diesel utes has nothing on the other side of the seesaw.

Car makers have a few options. They can sell more hybrids and electric vehicles to earn credits. They can buy credits from other brands that have too many. Or they can pay the penalty – and potentially pass some of that cost on to buyers.

We already know who's ahead and who's behind. In February 2026, NRMA reported the first results, for the second half of 2025. On the penalty side, Mazda's liability was around $25.4 million, Nissan's around $10.7 million, Subaru's almost $7 million and Hyundai's about $4.2 million. On the credit side, BYD had more than six million credits, Toyota nearly 2.9 million and Tesla more than 2.2 million.

Toyota is in an interesting position. Overall, it's earning credits, thanks to all those hybrid RAV4s, Corollas and Camrys. But its utes and big four-wheel drives are a different story. And under the 2027 targets, even Toyota's hybrid success may not be enough to cover the gap on the commercial side.

That's why Pappas said Toyota hasn't ruled out buying credits from rivals – but that the company wants to be "self-managed as much as possible". And it's why Toyota says it will use the government's review of the NVES to push for what it calls a more "pragmatic" ute target.

And Toyota isn't alone. According to CarExpert, the NVES has already contributed to price increases on the Ford Ranger and the petrol Ford Mustang. And Mazda, Isuzu and Ram have also warned of higher prices under the scheme.

Isuzu is in a particularly tough spot. Its whole local range is the diesel D-Max and MU-X. Back in 2025, Isuzu's Australian managing director, Junta Matsui, said the company would find a way to survive, balancing what Australians need from a vehicle with the emissions targets. Isuzu has studied a mild hybrid, a smaller diesel and an electric version. But in 2026, it cancelled its plans to bring the electric D-Max to Australia, with Isuzu's Rod Caldwell saying simply: "Customers don't want it."

One more thing worth knowing: the first actual penalty bills, for vehicles sold in 2025, aren't due to be determined until February 2028. Car makers have time to trade credits before then. So nobody knows yet exactly how much will be passed on to buyers.

### ch04
There's another change on the way that could affect some of Australia's toughest vehicles.

According to CarsGuide, a draft change could bring vehicles with a gross vehicle mass between 3,500 and 3,855 kilograms into the NVES from the 1st of July 2027. Right now, heavier vehicles sit outside the scheme.

Why does that matter? Because some popular heavy-duty vehicles sit right in that band. CarsGuide named the LandCruiser 70 Series, with a GVM of 3,510 kilograms, and the Toyota Tundra, at 3,536 kilograms. Both would be pulled into the emissions scheme if that draft change goes ahead.

And this fight over utes isn't new. Back in 2024, before the NVES became law, the Housing Industry Association warned that "applying stringent efficiency standards with harsh penalties will substantially drive up the price" of utes and vans. At the time, the government's consultation paper claimed compliance would cost manufacturers about $190 per ute or van. Two years later, we're about to find out who was closer to the truth.

It's still a draft. But if you're a farmer, a miner or a serious tourer looking at a LandCruiser 70, it's something to keep an eye on in 2027.

### ch06
So how big is the diesel ute problem? Let's look at the numbers.

According to September 2026 sales data reported by carsales, Australians bought 184,328 light commercial vehicles in the first nine months of 2026. 155,040 of them were diesel – that's about eighty-four per cent. Electric light commercials? Just 1,630.

The 4x4 ute market is also shrinking. It was down 13.6 per cent, to 140,410 sales. The Ford Ranger 4x4 led with 35,744, ahead of the HiLux 4x4 on 32,238, which was down 6.2 per cent.

And Toyota points out who's buying these vehicles. Nearly fifty-four per cent of HiLux and LandCruiser buyers live outside major cities. Pappas argues that for regional and rural Australians, with long distances and limited charging, diesel is still the practical choice. That's the heart of Toyota's argument for a softer target.

Meanwhile, the wider market is changing fast. The Australian Automobile Association reported that battery electric and plug-in hybrid vehicles made up 31.61 per cent of new light vehicle sales in the June quarter of 2026 – up from 19.07 per cent in the March quarter. Passenger cars are going electric quickly. In fact, CarExpert reported that electric vehicles outsold both petrol and diesel vehicles in August and September 2026. Utes are the part of the market that's furthest behind.

To be fair to Toyota, it says it supports the NVES and wants it to be "fair and equitable". It points out it has spent more than twenty years selling hybrids, and that its strategy is what it calls "multi-pathway" – hybrid, petrol, diesel, electric and eventually hydrogen, depending on the market. Pappas described diesel as "the right tool of trade for regional and rural Australia". The argument isn't really about whether to cut emissions. It's about how fast utes can realistically get there.

### ch08
And then there's the politics. Because the future of these rules isn't settled.

On the 29th of September 2026, the federal Coalition pledged to repeal the NVES if it wins government. Shadow energy minister Dan Tehan called it a "family car and ute tax", and said a repeal bill would be introduced within thirty days of taking office. The Coalition argues the rules have affected vehicle prices and choice, but carsales noted it hasn't put a figure on how much repeal would reduce showroom prices.

Supporters of the NVES see it very differently. The Climate Council argued that scrapping it would limit access to more efficient vehicles and increase Australia's reliance on imported oil. Farmers for Climate Action said repeal could reduce future choices for farmers, including electric utes.

And the car industry? The Federal Chamber of Automotive Industries, which represents car makers, said it needs "stable, predictable and workable policy settings" – because vehicle decisions are made years in advance.

So right now, the NVES is law, it's under review, and the opposition wants to scrap it. For ute buyers, that means uncertainty. Prices could rise in 2027 if the targets stay as they are. The targets could be softened by the review. Or the whole scheme could be repealed after the next federal election. Nobody can tell you for sure which way it'll go.

### ch09
So, what does all this mean if you're planning to buy a ute?

First, nobody has announced a 2027 price rise yet. Toyota said it "cannot rule out" increases. That's a warning, not a price list. Don't panic-buy because of a headline.

Second, if you were already planning to buy a diesel ute in the next few months, it's worth knowing that 2026 runout stock and year-end deals are on the table right now. If the price is right and the ute suits you, there's no reason to wait for things to get more expensive.

Third, work out your real costs. With diesel prices where they've been in 2026, a plug-in hybrid ute can save a lot on fuel if you do lots of short trips and charge at home. But if you tow heavy loads long distances, check the real-world range and towing figures very carefully before switching.

And if you're thinking about a plug-in hybrid ute through a novated lease, remember that plug-in hybrids lost their fringe benefits tax exemption on the 1st of April 2025. Only fully electric vehicles still qualify – and as we'll see in the next video, even that changes in 2027.

If towing is your priority, compare the braked towing capacity, not just the price. Some electrified utes now match diesel on paper – the MG U9 electric ute, for example, claims 3,500 kilograms of braked towing. But range drops a lot when you tow, so look for real-world tests before you commit.

Fourth, think about resale. If emissions rules keep tightening, the value of different types of utes could change over the next few years. Nobody knows exactly how. But it's worth asking yourself what your ute will be worth when you sell it in five years.

And fifth, watch the NVES review and the federal politics. If the targets are softened, or the scheme is repealed, the pressure on ute prices could ease. If they stay, 2027 is when the squeeze really starts.
