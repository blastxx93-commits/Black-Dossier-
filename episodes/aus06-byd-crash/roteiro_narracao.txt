### ch00_hook
Picture this. You're stopped at a red light in your new BYD. The car behind you doesn't stop in time. It's not a big crash. Nobody's hurt. The bumper's pushed in, a sensor light is flashing on the dash, and you think: no worries, the insurance will sort it out.

But this is where a very different story begins. Because for a lot of Australians, the most stressful part of owning a new electric car isn't the crash. It's everything that happens after it. The tow truck that has to take the car somewhere special. The repairer that isn't certified to touch it. The battery check nobody can do on the spot. The wait for parts. And then, twelve months later, the insurance renewal letter.

BYD is now one of the biggest car brands in Australia. Hundreds of thousands of Aussies are driving one. So in this video, we're going to follow a BYD from the moment of impact all the way to the next insurance bill – using real 2026 data from insurers, repairers, actuaries and BYD itself. No scare stories. Just what actually happens. And stay to the end, because I'll give you a simple checklist that could save you weeks of waiting and a lot of money.

### ch01
First, why does this matter so much right now? Because the numbers have changed fast.

In September 2026, BYD sold 8,191 new cars in Australia – up sixty-one per cent on the same month last year. That made it the second best-selling brand in the country, behind only Toyota, and it's second for the year to date as well. Ford, Mazda, Kia, Hyundai – BYD is now outselling all of them. Its range now covers almost every type of buyer: the Atto 1 and Dolphin city cars, the Atto 3 and Sealion 7 SUVs, the Seal sedan, the Sealion 6 plug-in hybrid, and the Shark 6 ute.

At the same time, electric and plug-in cars have gone mainstream. In September 2026, battery electric cars made up more than twenty-four per cent of new car sales in Australia – and for the second month in a row, more pure electric cars were sold than petrol-only cars. And insurers are seeing it. NRMA Insurance says requests for electric vehicle insurance quotes were up eighty per cent in the first nine months of 2026.

More of these cars on the road means more of them in crashes. That's not a criticism of BYD. It's simply maths. And it means a lot of drivers are about to discover how an EV crash repair actually works – for the first time.

### ch02
So let's go back to that red light. The first thing that happens after a crash is the tow. And with an electric car, even that is a little different.

An EV has a large high-voltage battery under the floor. Most tow operators will want to move it on a flatbed rather than dragging it on its wheels, and the car has to be handled in a specific way so the drive system isn't damaged. If the battery might have been hit, it can't just be parked next to other cars in a holding yard. Many assessing centres now keep damaged EVs in a separate quarantine area, away from buildings, because of the small but serious risk of a delayed battery fire.

Then comes the next question: who's allowed to fix it? Repairing an electric car properly needs trained technicians, insulated tools, and procedures to safely isolate the high-voltage system before anyone starts cutting or welding. Back in 2024, the Motor Trades Association of Australia said only around ten per cent of the country's repairers were certified to work on electric vehicles. Its chief executive, Matt Hobbs, pointed out that technicians have to be trained to safely de-power systems running at around four hundred volts before they start work. And a report by the association with Deloitte found that only around forty-one per cent of EV technician job vacancies were being filled. That number has been growing, and BYD has its own approved collision repair network. But depending on where you live, the nearest approved shop could be a long way away.

So before a single panel is touched, your car might already have travelled further, and waited longer, than a petrol car with the same damage.

### ch03
Now the car arrives at the repairer. And this is where things get expensive, because modern cars hide a lot behind the bumper.

A rear bumper on a new BYD isn't just a piece of plastic. It can carry parking sensors, a reversing camera and radar units for the safety systems. After a hit, those sensors often need to be replaced or at least recalibrated, and calibration needs special equipment and time. A bump that would have cost a few hundred dollars on a twenty-year-old car can turn into a bill of several thousand.

Then there's the battery. Even if it looks fine, the repairer and the insurer need to know whether the impact has damaged the pack or its protective casing. On some models, that means removing the battery for inspection. And if the pack is damaged, the cost can be huge. In a report published in April 2026, actuarial firm Taylor Fry said the battery can make up around thirty to fifty per cent of an electric car's value. Replace the battery, and you may be looking at most of the cost of the car.

That's why some EVs with relatively modest damage end up written off. Not because the car is destroyed, but because the risk and cost of repairing the battery is too high compared to what the car is worth.

Industry insiders have described cases where electric cars were written off because the battery casing was scratched, even though the cells inside were probably fine – simply because nobody had the diagnostic information to prove the pack was safe. And when an insurer writes off an EV, it doesn't get much back. In 2024, an assessing industry manager said the salvage value of a written-off electric car was only around eight to ten per cent of its replacement value, because there was so little demand for used EV batteries and parts. Every one of those write-offs is a cost that ends up spread across everyone's premiums.

To be clear, not every insurer sees it the same way. Allianz, for example, has said that cosmetic EV repairs generally take about as long as petrol car repairs, and that it had no data showing EVs are written off more often. The problem is concentrated in the serious crashes – the ones that reach the battery and the high-voltage system.

### ch04
To be fair to BYD, its battery design does help here. BYD uses what it calls the Blade battery, a lithium iron phosphate design. Back in 2024, BYD Australia's collision and repair representatives explained that the Blade battery is built in a way that can, in some cases, allow damaged sections to be repaired rather than replacing the whole pack. Lithium iron phosphate cells are also generally considered more stable and less prone to thermal runaway than some other battery chemistries.

That's genuinely good news for owners. But it only helps if the repairer is trained to do it, has the parts, and the insurer agrees that a repair is safe. And that brings us to the part almost every BYD owner talks about.

### ch05
The wait.

In the early years of BYD in Australia, parts delays were one of the most common complaints in owner groups. Some owners publicly described waiting months for crash parts while their cars sat at the repairer. BYD's own executives have acknowledged that the brand had a reputation for slow parts and service, and that it needed to fix it.

And in 2026, BYD has spent serious money trying to do exactly that. In October, BYD Australia said it now holds more than 580,000 parts for BYD and Denza models locally, covering around ninety-five per cent of what dealers need, worth more than twenty-five million dollars, across warehouses in four cities. It says it now stocks Blade battery packs locally too, and that parts not in stock can be sent by air freight rather than waiting for a ship.

To put that ninety-five per cent in context: back in 2024, MG told CarAdvice its own parts fill rate was around ninety-one per cent, with air-freighted parts taking about two working weeks when they weren't in stock locally. Polestar said overseas parts could take four to six weeks. So on paper, BYD's 2026 parts promise is now among the strongest of the newer brands. The real test is whether owners waiting on crash repairs actually feel the difference.

What about servicing? In a September 2026 interview with NRMA's Open Road, BYD said the average wait for a service booking was about seven days, and that it was aiming to bring that down to four or five.

But the wider industry still has a capacity problem. In July 2026, a review commissioned by the Australian Automotive Dealer Association found that some workshops had backlogs of six to eight weeks just to diagnose a problem – especially on electric vehicles. So even with the parts on the shelf, the bottleneck can move to the workshop itself: not enough trained people, not enough bays, and too many cars arriving at once.

### ch07
Now let's talk about who pays.

According to the Insurance Council of Australia, the average cost of motor vehicle repairs has risen around twenty-six per cent since 2022, and repair costs now account for roughly sixty per cent of the money insurers pay out on motor claims. That's across all cars, not just EVs. Sensors, cameras, more expensive paint and panels, and a shortage of skilled labour have all pushed the average bill up.

Electric cars add another layer. A senior figure in BYD's own collision repair network told an industry magazine that EV repairs currently sit in a cost category more like high-end luxury cars. And when repairs cost more, insurers have to charge more to cover the risk.

There's also a hire car issue. While your car is off the road, someone has to pay for a replacement. If the repair takes weeks, that hire bill keeps running. And all of those costs end up in the same place: the premiums everyone pays at renewal.

### ch08
So what has actually happened to BYD insurance prices in 2026?

In April 2026, insurance comparison site Compare the Market published an analysis of premiums across popular hybrid and electric cars. It found that the average premium for battery electric vehicles had risen about ten per cent compared to a year earlier, to around two thousand three hundred dollars a year.

And for some BYD models, the jump was bigger. The average quoted premium for the Sealion 6 rose about eighteen per cent, from around $1,482 to around $1,821. The Dolphin went up about nine per cent, from around $1,437 to about $1,578. The Shark 6 rose about five per cent, to around $2,172. And the Sealion 7 was almost flat, up about half a per cent.

Other research tells a similar story. CHOICE reported in January 2026 that the average premium it found for EVs was about $2,545 a year, compared with about $1,702 for petrol cars. And in the Taylor Fry report, the spread of quotes for an Atto 3 between the cheapest and most expensive insurer was around $900 – compared with roughly $500 for a petrol Mazda CX-30.

Now, two important points. These are averages from specific comparisons, not your personal quote. Your age, postcode, driving history and excess all matter enormously. And BYD isn't the most expensive brand to insure. In earlier comparisons, Teslas cost more. But the trend is clear: for many EV owners, the cost of insurance is rising faster than for the average petrol car. And that big gap between insurers means shopping around has never mattered more.

### ch09
So you've had the crash, the car's at the repairer, and nothing is happening. What can you actually do?

First, know that your insurer has rules to follow. Most Australian insurers have signed up to the General Insurance Code of Practice. It sets out standards for how claims should be handled. Under the code, insurers are generally expected to give you a progress update at least every twenty business days, to respond to routine requests within ten business days, and in most cases to make a decision on your claim within four months. If you feel your claim is stuck, ask your insurer in writing for an update and for a timeline – and mention the code.

Second, if you're not happy with the answer, use the insurer's internal complaints process. Put it in writing and keep a record of every call. Insurers generally have thirty days to respond to a complaint. If that doesn't resolve it, you can take your complaint to the Australian Financial Complaints Authority, known as AFCA. It's free for consumers and it's independent of the insurer. Before you go, gather everything: the claim number, your photos, the repairer's estimates, and your diary of calls and emails. A clear timeline makes a complaint much stronger.

Third, if the delay is about parts, and not the insurer, remember the Australian Consumer Law. Manufacturers and importers have to make spare parts and repair facilities reasonably available for a reasonable time. If the car is new and the problem is a fault rather than crash damage, you also have consumer guarantee rights against the business that sold it to you. Your state's fair trading office can help.

### ch10
So here's the checklist I promised. Save this one.

Before a crash happens: check your policy. Does it cover a hire car, and for how long? Is the hire car capped at a dollar amount or a number of days? Can you choose your own repairer, or does the insurer choose? And find out where your nearest BYD-approved collision repairer actually is.

When you renew: don't just accept the renewal price. Get quotes from at least three insurers. With differences of hundreds of dollars between companies for the same car, a twenty-minute search can pay for itself many times over. Ask how they handle EV battery damage, and whether they have a specialist EV claims process.

After a crash: take photos of everything, including the dashboard warnings. Tell the tow operator it's an electric or plug-in vehicle. Ask the insurer or repairer for a written estimate of the timeline. Keep a diary of every call and email, with dates. And if the car has been hit near the battery, ask whether a battery health check has been done, and get a copy of the result.

And one more thing to check on your policy: is your car insured for an agreed value or a market value? With an agreed value, you and the insurer settle on the amount up front. With market value, the insurer pays what the car is worth at the time of the crash. If an EV is written off because of battery damage, that difference can matter a lot – especially in a market where used EV prices have been falling. So read the policy wording, not just the price.

It's also worth knowing who chooses the repairer. Some policies let you pick your own workshop, while others send you to the insurer's own network. If you want an EV-certified or BYD-approved repairer, make sure your policy allows it before you need it.

And if you're still deciding whether to buy a BYD, or any EV, ask the dealer three questions before you sign. Where is the nearest approved collision repairer? How long are service bookings right now? And are crash parts for this model stocked in Australia? The answers will tell you a lot about what your life looks like after a crash.

### ch11
So, is BYD's biggest problem in Australia the car itself? Not really. The cars sell well and the battery technology is genuinely good. The real challenge starts after the crash: a repair industry that's still catching up with electric cars, a shortage of trained hands, insurers adjusting their prices, and a brand that grew faster than its support network.

The good news is that BYD is clearly spending money to fix its side of it, with local parts, local batteries and faster service targets. The question for 2027 is whether repairers and insurers can keep up as hundreds of thousands more of these cars hit Australian roads.
