So let's go back to that red light. The first thing that happens after a crash is the tow. And with an electric car, even that is a little different.

An EV has a large high-voltage battery under the floor. Most tow operators will want to move it on a flatbed rather than dragging it on its wheels, and the car has to be handled in a specific way so the drive system isn't damaged. If the battery might have been hit, it can't just be parked next to other cars in a holding yard. Many assessing centres now keep damaged EVs in a separate quarantine area, away from buildings, because of the small but serious risk of a delayed battery fire.

Then comes the next question: who's allowed to fix it? Repairing an electric car properly needs trained technicians, insulated tools, and procedures to safely isolate the high-voltage system before anyone starts cutting or welding. Back in 2024, the Motor Trades Association of Australia said only around ten per cent of the country's repairers were certified to work on electric vehicles. Its chief executive, Matt Hobbs, pointed out that technicians have to be trained to safely de-power systems running at around four hundred volts before they start work. And a report by the association with Deloitte found that only around forty-one per cent of EV technician job vacancies were being filled. That number has been growing, and BYD has its own approved collision repair network. But depending on where you live, the nearest approved shop could be a long way away.

So before a single panel is touched, your car might already have travelled further, and waited longer, than a petrol car with the same damage.
