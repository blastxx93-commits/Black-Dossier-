### ch00_hook
Twenty thousand. That's how many utes Kia was talking about selling in the first year of its brand-new Tasman. Fifteen months later, the real number was fewer than eight thousand – and Kia's own global planning boss admitted: "Clearly we are not satisfied yet."

And that's just one of the disasters of 2026. A Tesla that Australians waited almost seven years for – cancelled by email. A big family SUV pulled from sale after a tragedy overseas. A famous French brand dumped by its own importer. An American icon whose sales collapsed by two thirds. And a Japanese giant whose own boss says this year is "the low point".

Here's what makes it even crazier. 2026 is shaping up as a huge year for new cars in Australia. October was the biggest October ever. Buyers have money, and they're spending it. They're just not spending it on these.

So in this video, I'm counting down the ten biggest car disasters of 2026 in Australia, from bad to unbelievable. Real sales numbers, real quotes, and what each disaster means if you own one of these cars – or you're thinking about buying one. And stay until number one, because the gap between what was promised and what actually happened is unbelievable.

### ch01
Before the countdown, a quick look at the battlefield, because it explains almost every disaster on this list.

Australians bought 107,493 new vehicles in October 2026, a record for the month, and the market passed one million sales for the year by the end of October. But the money is moving fast. Cars built in China were up 104 per cent in October. Electric cars were up 237 per cent. BYD sold more than ten thousand cars in a single month, and Geely was up 520 per cent.

At the same time, cars from Japan were down 13 per cent, from Korea down 23, and from Germany down 24. Mitsubishi was down 50 per cent in October, Volkswagen down 46, Audi down 45 and Subaru down 42.

In a market moving this fast, a car doesn't have to be bad to become a disaster. It just has to be too expensive, too late, or in the wrong place at the wrong time. And in 2026, plenty of cars managed all three.

A quick word on how I chose this list. I've looked at sales collapses, cancellations, recalls and broken promises, using official VFACTS sales data and reports from CarExpert, GoAuto and others. I'm ranking them by how big the gap is between what was expected and what actually happened. Let's go.

### ch02
Number ten: the Mazda CX-70.

The CX-70 is Mazda's big, premium five-seat SUV, with a powerful six-cylinder engine. It was designed mainly for the American market, and Mazda brought it to Australia hoping it would sit at the top of its range.

It didn't work. In the first nine months of 2026, the CX-70 sold just 182 cars in Australia, down almost 35 per cent on the same period last year. In the same class, the Land Rover Defender sold more than three thousand. That's seventeen Defenders for every CX-70.

And this is after a big price cut. In July 2026, Mazda cut CX-70 prices by between $3,700 and $8,051, depending on the variant. Sales still didn't recover.

Then in late October, Mazda confirmed that the CX-70 is ending production for the United States – its biggest market. Mazda Australia says the car will continue here. But when a car is built for America and America stops getting it, that's a warning sign for anyone buying one in Australia. Mazda as a whole was down almost 20 per cent in October.

### ch03
Number nine: the Ford Mustang Mach-E.

This one should have been a hit. It has one of the most famous badges in the world, and it's an electric SUV in a year when electric car sales in Australia exploded.

Instead, the Mach-E sold 206 cars in the first nine months of 2026, down more than 34 per cent. In all of 2025, it found fewer than five hundred buyers.

Ford tried to fix it with price cuts. EV Central reported drive-away offers of sixty thousand dollars for the Select, seventy-five thousand for the Premium and eighty-nine thousand for the GT – cuts of up to around nine and a half thousand dollars on earlier list prices. EV Central described it as Ford being "desperate to shift" stock.

To understand how big this disaster is, look at the competition. In October alone, the Tesla Model Y sold 3,105 cars. The Mach-E sold 206 in nine months. In the United States, Ford sold more than fifty thousand Mach-Es in 2025. In Australia, against a wall of cheaper Chinese electric SUVs, it simply disappeared.

### ch04
Number eight: the Honda Accord.

This is the one that shocked a lot of people in my last countdown. In the first nine months of 2026, Honda sold just 59 Accords in the whole of Australia. That's fewer than seven a month.

At around $64,900 drive-away for the single hybrid RS model, it's an expensive mid-size sedan in a market that's walking away from sedans. In the same period, Toyota sold 9,471 Camrys – more than one hundred and sixty Camrys for every Accord.

Honda moved to a fixed-price agency model in 2021, with fewer dealerships and no haggling. It said it wanted fewer, more profitable sales. With the Accord, it certainly got fewer.

Now Honda is trying to clear 2026-built stock with a thousand-dollar gift card and five free services on the Accord. And CarExpert reported the updated 2027 Accord will cost a thousand dollars more. To be fair, GoAuto pointed out that low sales like these are not necessarily about product quality – and the Accord is a well-reviewed car. But fifty-nine sales in nine months is still a disaster by any measure.

### ch05
Number seven: Maserati.

Maserati is one of the most famous names in the car world – the trident badge, the Italian V6 and V8 engines, the luxury. But in Australia in 2026, it's in freefall.

According to CarExpert's analysis of the first half of the year, Maserati sold just 101 cars in six months, down 33 per cent. Its main model, the Grecale SUV, sold 98. For comparison, the Genesis GV70 luxury SUV alone sold 570 in the same period. The GranTurismo and GranCabrio sold three between them.

And the range is shrinking. The Ghibli sedan, the Levante SUV and the Quattroporte limousine have all been discontinued. In October, the Ghibli and the Levante recorded zero sales.

When a luxury brand stops selling, it's not just a problem for the brand. It's a problem for owners. Resale values fall, dealers get fewer customers, and buyers start to worry about parts and servicing. That's how a brand slowly disappears from a market – one quiet month at a time.

### ch06
Number six: Peugeot.

In July 2026, Peugeot's Australian importer, Inchcape, announced that it was ending its distribution deal for the brand. In CarExpert's words, Peugeot was "dumped by its Australian distributor".

The sales numbers explain why. Peugeot's best year in Australia was 2007, with 8,807 sales. In 2025, it was down to 1,350. In the first five months of 2026, it delivered just 373 vehicles – and its best seller was the Partner van. In the first half of the year, the Peugeot 208 small car recorded just one delivery. One.

And in October, the Peugeot 308 sold three cars and the 508 sold none.

Stellantis, Peugeot's parent company, says it intends to "maintain continuity of distribution in Australia". Inchcape says it will keep looking after existing owners during the transition. But this is the second French brand Inchcape has walked away from, after Citroen in 2024 – and Citroen is no longer sold new in Australia. If you own a Peugeot, keep an eye on where your nearest service centre will be.

### ch07
Number five: Nissan.

This is a brand that sold almost eighty thousand cars a year in Australia at its peak in 2012. In 2026, it's having its worst year in a very long time.

From January to July, Nissan delivered 15,445 vehicles, down almost 34 per cent. The Qashqai was down 73 per cent. And the new-generation Navara ute – a car that should have been a fresh start – was down almost 49 per cent.

Then came the cuts. Nissan axed the Juke and the Pathfinder in Australia. The new-generation Leaf electric car was postponed indefinitely. And CarExpert reported that a tougher Navara Warrior model was cancelled.

Nissan's Oceania boss, Steve Milette, was remarkably honest about it. He told CarExpert: "2026 is the low point, and it then increases from there on in." In other words, Nissan itself is calling 2026 the bottom.

To be fair to Nissan, there's a plan. It's lining up new models, including Chinese-built ones, for 2027. But for 2026, the numbers speak for themselves.

### ch08
Number four: Jeep.

The original off-road brand. In September 2026, the whole of Jeep sold just sixteen cars in Australia, across every model.

According to CarExpert, Jeep's sales in the first half of 2026 fell 66 per cent to just 365 vehicles. The Compass was down 84 per cent and is no longer sold here. The Grand Cherokee was down almost 97 per cent, with stock nearly run out. The Gladiator ute was down 56 per cent. And the little Avenger, CarExpert simply described as a flop.

In October, the Jeep Compass sold one car, and the Grand Cherokee and Avenger sold none.

The problem is price. The cheapest four-door Wrangler went from around thirty-seven thousand dollars in 2014 to almost seventy-six thousand before on-road costs. At that money, Australians are buying a Prado, an Everest, or a Chinese four-by-four with far more equipment.

Jeep is now offering four thousand dollars towards your deposit on the Wrangler Rubicon. And reports say Jeep may use Chinese partner Dongfeng for future models. For a brand built on American heritage, that tells you how bad things have become.

### ch09
Number three: the Tesla Cybertruck.

This one is a disaster of a different kind – because the car never even arrived.

In November 2019, Tesla started taking one-hundred-and-fifty-dollar deposits from Australians for the Cybertruck. Thousands of people put their names down. Then they waited. And waited. The reservation portal closed in May 2022 and never reopened.

Then, on the 23rd of October 2026, Tesla emailed deposit holders with this: "After careful consideration, plans to produce Cybertruck for Australia and New Zealand have been discontinued." Almost seven years after the first deposits were taken.

The reason is simple. The Cybertruck is built only in left-hand drive, and it isn't sold in any right-hand-drive country. Tesla is refunding deposits, and offering a free year of its Full Self-Driving software to buyers who order a Model 3 or Model Y instead – CarExpert valued that at $1,788.

To be fair, a hundred and fifty dollars won't break anyone. But seven years of waiting for a truck that was never coming? That's a disaster for Tesla's reputation with some of its most loyal fans.

### ch10
Number two: the Hyundai Palisade.

The new-generation Palisade is Hyundai's big seven- and eight-seat family SUV. It launched in Australia with a lot of excitement. Then, in March 2026, came the news no car company ever wants.

On the 7th of March, a two-year-old child died in an incident involving a Palisade in Ohio, in the United States. Hyundai said the power-operated second- and third-row seats in some models "may not adequately detect contact with an occupant or object as intended".

Hyundai halted sales and deliveries of the affected Palisade in North America, Korea – and Australia. CarExpert reported around 2,260 vehicles were affected here. On the 2nd of April, a formal recall was issued in Australia for 2,242 Palisades from the 2025 and 2026 model years, to fix the software in the electric seat-folding system. The fix is free, and owners can get it at a dealer or, in some cases, over the air.

This is a tragedy first, and a business disaster second. If you own an affected Palisade, check that the recall work has been done – and, as Hyundai advises, always make sure no person or object is in the seat's path before folding it.

### ch11
And number one: the Kia Tasman.

Kia's first-ever ute. It was developed with Australia in mind, and before it launched, Kia was talking big. According to GoAuto, the target was twenty thousand sales, and seven to ten per cent of the ute market, in its first year.

The Tasman arrived in July 2025. And according to the official VFACTS figures, reported by GoAuto, Kia had sold 7,898 Tasmans from launch to the end of September 2026. That's fifteen months, and not even half the first-year target.

Kia hasn't hidden from it. Kia Australia's CEO, Damien Meredith, said early in the year: "We're not making or looking for excuses." And in May, Kia's global planning boss, Spencer Cho, told CarExpert: "Clearly we are not satisfied yet." He said "all the options are on the table" to fix it, including more power or a hybrid.

The discounts have been huge – up to thirteen thousand dollars off the X-Pro earlier in the year, according to CarExpert, and in November the 2025 Tasman SX+ was advertised at $54,990 drive-away, down from $62,390 before on-road costs. And in Korea, local media reported that production was being cut because of weak demand.

Why didn't it work? Critics have pointed to the styling and the lack of a hybrid. Kia has pointed to the aggressive pricing of rival ute makers, at a time when the Ford Ranger, the Toyota HiLux and BYD's Shark 6 are fighting hard for every buyer. Whatever the reason, the Tasman is more than twelve thousand sales short of its first-year plan – even with three extra months. That's why it's number one.

### ch12
Before the verdict, a few dishonourable mentions – disasters that only just missed the top ten.

The Kia EV9. A seven-seat electric SUV from $97,000 before on-road costs. In the first nine months of 2026, it found 120 buyers, down almost 50 per cent. Half its buyers, gone in a year.

The Audi Q4 e-tron. Just two sales in October, down 96 per cent. Audi has now cut the price of the updated model by up to eighteen thousand dollars – but deliveries of that car don't start until 2027.

Polestar. In a year when electric car sales in Australia more than doubled, CarExpert reported that Polestar's sales were down ten per cent year to date. The Polestar 4 was down almost a quarter in September. Polestar's local boss, Scott Maynard, defended the result, saying the Polestar 4 is "absolutely flying" – but the overall numbers went backwards in the middle of an EV boom.

The Toyota Fortuner. Eight sales in October, down more than 97 per cent. Toyota has axed it in Australia, with no replacement. Even the biggest brand in the country has its failures.

Mitsubishi. Sales down 50 per cent in October alone. For a brand that was a top-five player not long ago, that's a brutal month.

And three brands that recorded exactly zero new-car sales in October: Jaguar, Lotus and McLaren. To be fair, Jaguar has stopped selling new cars while it reinvents itself as an electric luxury brand, so its zero is planned. But a month with zero sales is still a striking number for brands that so many people know.

### ch13
So what do these ten disasters have in common?

Most of them made the same mistake: they arrived too expensive, or too late, in a market that changed faster than anyone expected. Chinese brands moved in with cheaper hybrids and electric cars, and buyers stopped paying a premium for a badge.

And for you, that matters in three ways.

First, if you're buying, disasters mean deals. When a car isn't selling, the dealer has the most room to move. Ask for a written drive-away price, ask how long the car has been in stock, and ask about demonstrators.

Second, think about resale. A car that's hard to sell new is usually hard to sell used. Plan to keep it longer, and make sure the discount covers the extra depreciation.

Third, if you already own one of these cars, don't panic. Your warranty and consumer rights don't change because sales are low. Check for recalls, keep your service history up to date, and know where your nearest service centre will be.

So that's the list: the Mazda CX-70, the Mustang Mach-E, the Honda Accord, Maserati, Peugeot, Nissan, Jeep, the Tesla Cybertruck, the Hyundai Palisade – and at number one, the Kia Tasman.
