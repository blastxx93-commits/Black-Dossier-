There's also a version that tries to protect working families.

In May 2026, the McKell Institute released a study called Fairer Roads, backed by the Electric Vehicle Council. It proposed introducing a road-user charge once electric cars reach thirty per cent of Australia's whole fleet – not just new sales. Rates would be set by income, across four levels from 3.74 to 12.86 cents per kilometre. Once electric vehicles passed seventy-five per cent of the fleet, the charge would apply to all vehicles and replace fuel excise. In the meantime, fuel-powered cars would pay an extra carbon surcharge of half a cent to one and a half cents per kilometre.

McKell's chief executive, Edward Cavanough, argued that families in the outer suburbs drive the furthest and have the fewest alternatives, so a flat per-kilometre charge would hit them hardest.

And interestingly, a lot of EV owners don't object. Research released by Transport Australia found 71 per cent of electric and hybrid drivers support a road-user charge – the highest support of any group surveyed. The debate isn't really about whether EVs should pay. It's about whether everyone else should pay more too.
