To understand why this is happening, you need to understand how roads are paid for today.

Every time you buy fuel, you pay fuel excise – a fixed tax per litre. It went back up to around 53.7 cents a litre on the 3rd of August 2026, after a temporary cut during the fuel crisis ended. Before the cut, it was 52.6 cents. And it rises twice a year with inflation. During the 2026 fuel crisis, the government cut excise for three months, then extended a smaller cut for another month, before restoring the full rate in August. That cut alone showed how much money is at stake – and how politically sensitive fuel taxes are.

It's also worth knowing that fuel excise isn't a dedicated road fund. It goes into general government revenue, alongside income tax and GST. Road funding comes out of that bigger pot, from federal, state and local governments. So when people say "EV drivers don't pay for roads", it's a bit more complicated than that – but they're right that EV drivers don't pay the biggest motoring tax.

Fuel excise raises roughly fifteen billion dollars a year for the federal government. According to the ABC, the average petrol or diesel driver pays about $1,400 a year in excise.

But here's the problem. Electric cars don't use fuel. So EV drivers pay no fuel excise at all. And EVs are no longer a niche. In September 2026, electric cars made up 24.2 per cent of all new-vehicle sales in Australia, more than petrol cars. The Tesla Model Y beat the Ford Ranger to become the country's best-selling vehicle in May 2026, and did it again in June and August.

As more people switch, the excise money shrinks. Plug-in hybrids and efficient hybrids burn less fuel too, so they pay less as well. The CEO of the advocacy group Transport Australia, Ehssan Veiszadeh, summed it up: "Fuel excise was designed for a different vehicle fleet and a different economy."

So governments are looking for a replacement. And the leading idea is a road-user charge: a fee for every kilometre you drive.

Australia wouldn't be the first to do this. Across the Tasman, New Zealand made electric cars pay road-user charges from the 1st of April 2024: 76 New Zealand dollars per thousand kilometres for a pure EV, and 53 dollars for a plug-in hybrid, which still pays fuel tax at the pump. New Zealand's Automobile Association estimated that an average driver covering 12,000 kilometres a year would pay about $912 in an EV.

And in the United Kingdom, the government announced in late 2025 that from April 2028, electric cars will pay three pence per mile, and plug-in hybrids one and a half pence – on top of existing vehicle tax. Drivers will estimate their mileage each year, and it'll be checked at the annual roadworthy test. So the direction around the world is clear: if you don't pay at the pump, you'll pay by the kilometre.
