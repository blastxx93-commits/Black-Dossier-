So who's right? Here are the strongest arguments on both sides.

Supporters say a road-user charge is simply fairer. Everyone uses the roads, so everyone should pay for them. Heavier vehicles cause more wear, so weight-based charges make sense. And without reform, the money for roads, potholes and highways will keep shrinking as the fleet goes electric.

Critics say a charge on top of fuel excise is a double tax, at a time when Australians are already struggling with the cost of living and record fuel prices. Regional and outer-suburban drivers, who travel the longest distances and have no public transport, would pay the most. Some worry about privacy, if kilometres are tracked by GPS rather than odometer readings. EV advocates warn that charging electric cars too early could slow the switch away from petrol and diesel. And the High Court ruling means any state scheme could face a legal challenge.

There's also the question of how the money is collected. The simplest approach is odometer readings – at rego renewal, at a service, or when a car is sold. That's what New Zealand and the UK rely on. GPS tracking can be more precise and could allow peak-hour or city-based pricing, but it raises bigger privacy concerns. And any system has to stop people winding back odometers or under-reporting their distance.

Both sides agree on one thing: fuel excise alone won't pay for Australia's roads forever. The fight is over who pays, how much, and when.
