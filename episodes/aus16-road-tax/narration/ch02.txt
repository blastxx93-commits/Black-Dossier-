The first state to try it for real is New South Wales.

NSW already has a road-user charge written into law. For the 2026-27 financial year, the rate is 3.095 cents per kilometre for battery-electric and hydrogen cars, and 2.476 cents for plug-in hybrids – that's eighty per cent of the full rate. The rate is indexed to inflation every year.

It's due to start on the 1st of July 2027, or when electric vehicles reach thirty per cent of new-car sales – whichever comes first. And that trigger is closer than you might think. In the June 2026 quarter, battery EVs made up about 21 per cent of national sales. Add plug-in hybrids, and it was around 31.6 per cent. It's not yet clear exactly how the trigger will be measured.

What would it cost? Drive 15,000 kilometres a year in an electric car in New South Wales, and at today's rate, that's about $464 a year.

But there's a big legal problem. In October 2023, the High Court ruled four to three that Victoria's distance-based charge on electric cars was a duty of excise – and under the Constitution, only the federal government can impose excise. The Victorian scheme was struck down. The NSW scheme has a similar structure, and NSW says it's still assessing the implications. As of September 2026, no EV road-user charge was actually being collected anywhere in Australia.

Western Australia had also announced a planned charge for 2027, of 2.5 cents per kilometre for battery EVs and 2 cents for plug-in hybrids, alongside an EV rebate. Whether those rates still stand is unclear.
