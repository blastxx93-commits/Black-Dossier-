And that's where Tesla comes in – with a twist almost nobody expected.

In a submission to a federal parliamentary inquiry, the Select Committee on Productivity in Australia, Tesla Australia argued that a road-user charge should not apply only to electric cars. It said the charge should "apply it universally to all vehicles on the road, phased-in," with the rate based on "real road funding costs".

Here's the controversial part. Tesla proposed keeping fuel excise as well. That means drivers of newly registered petrol, diesel and hybrid vehicles would eventually pay both: the excise on their fuel, and a charge for every kilometre.

Why would Tesla want that? Its argument is that an EV-only charge "acts as a financial penalty for decarbonising personal transport". It said a charge on EVs alone would "distort" what people think motoring costs. And it argued that fuel excise doesn't really change behaviour, because "consumers remain largely inelastic to the tax component of their travel", while a per-kilometre charge would encourage people to skip unnecessary trips and use public or active transport.

Of course, Tesla sells only electric cars. A system that taxes its customers less, and everyone else more, would suit its business. That doesn't make the argument wrong – but it's worth knowing who's making it.
