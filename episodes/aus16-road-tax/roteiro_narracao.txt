### ch00_hook
Fifty-three point seven cents. That's how much tax you pay on every single litre of petrol or diesel in Australia right now. Fill up a big ute, and you've handed the government around forty dollars before you've even left the servo.

Now imagine paying that – and a new tax for every kilometre you drive on top of it.

That's not a conspiracy theory. In October 2026, Tesla – the world's best-known electric car company – told a federal parliamentary inquiry that petrol, diesel and hybrid drivers should pay a per-kilometre road charge, while keeping fuel excise. The Australian Electric Vehicle Association has proposed something similar. Polestar's Australian boss says a road-user charge is "inevitable". And New South Wales already has a law for a per-kilometre charge on electric cars, due to start in July 2027.

So in this video, I'll explain exactly what's being proposed, who wants it, what it could cost you – whether you drive a Corolla, a LandCruiser or a Tesla – and what you can actually do about it. Because the way Australians pay for roads is about to change, and it's going to affect every driver in the country.

### ch01
To understand why this is happening, you need to understand how roads are paid for today.

Every time you buy fuel, you pay fuel excise – a fixed tax per litre. It went back up to around 53.7 cents a litre on the 3rd of August 2026, after a temporary cut during the fuel crisis ended. Before the cut, it was 52.6 cents. And it rises twice a year with inflation. During the 2026 fuel crisis, the government cut excise for three months, then extended a smaller cut for another month, before restoring the full rate in August. That cut alone showed how much money is at stake – and how politically sensitive fuel taxes are.

It's also worth knowing that fuel excise isn't a dedicated road fund. It goes into general government revenue, alongside income tax and GST. Road funding comes out of that bigger pot, from federal, state and local governments. So when people say "EV drivers don't pay for roads", it's a bit more complicated than that – but they're right that EV drivers don't pay the biggest motoring tax.

Fuel excise raises roughly fifteen billion dollars a year for the federal government. According to the ABC, the average petrol or diesel driver pays about $1,400 a year in excise.

But here's the problem. Electric cars don't use fuel. So EV drivers pay no fuel excise at all. And EVs are no longer a niche. In September 2026, electric cars made up 24.2 per cent of all new-vehicle sales in Australia, more than petrol cars. The Tesla Model Y beat the Ford Ranger to become the country's best-selling vehicle in May 2026, and did it again in June and August.

As more people switch, the excise money shrinks. Plug-in hybrids and efficient hybrids burn less fuel too, so they pay less as well. The CEO of the advocacy group Transport Australia, Ehssan Veiszadeh, summed it up: "Fuel excise was designed for a different vehicle fleet and a different economy."

So governments are looking for a replacement. And the leading idea is a road-user charge: a fee for every kilometre you drive.

Australia wouldn't be the first to do this. Across the Tasman, New Zealand made electric cars pay road-user charges from the 1st of April 2024: 76 New Zealand dollars per thousand kilometres for a pure EV, and 53 dollars for a plug-in hybrid, which still pays fuel tax at the pump. New Zealand's Automobile Association estimated that an average driver covering 12,000 kilometres a year would pay about $912 in an EV.

And in the United Kingdom, the government announced in late 2025 that from April 2028, electric cars will pay three pence per mile, and plug-in hybrids one and a half pence – on top of existing vehicle tax. Drivers will estimate their mileage each year, and it'll be checked at the annual roadworthy test. So the direction around the world is clear: if you don't pay at the pump, you'll pay by the kilometre.

### ch02
The first state to try it for real is New South Wales.

NSW already has a road-user charge written into law. For the 2026-27 financial year, the rate is 3.095 cents per kilometre for battery-electric and hydrogen cars, and 2.476 cents for plug-in hybrids – that's eighty per cent of the full rate. The rate is indexed to inflation every year.

It's due to start on the 1st of July 2027, or when electric vehicles reach thirty per cent of new-car sales – whichever comes first. And that trigger is closer than you might think. In the June 2026 quarter, battery EVs made up about 21 per cent of national sales. Add plug-in hybrids, and it was around 31.6 per cent. It's not yet clear exactly how the trigger will be measured.

What would it cost? Drive 15,000 kilometres a year in an electric car in New South Wales, and at today's rate, that's about $464 a year.

But there's a big legal problem. In October 2023, the High Court ruled four to three that Victoria's distance-based charge on electric cars was a duty of excise – and under the Constitution, only the federal government can impose excise. The Victorian scheme was struck down. The NSW scheme has a similar structure, and NSW says it's still assessing the implications. As of September 2026, no EV road-user charge was actually being collected anywhere in Australia.

Western Australia had also announced a planned charge for 2027, of 2.5 cents per kilometre for battery EVs and 2 cents for plug-in hybrids, alongside an EV rebate. Whether those rates still stand is unclear.

### ch03
Because of that High Court ruling, the real decision sits in Canberra.

Treasurer Jim Chalmers has called a road-user charge "an idea whose time has come," and federal and state treasurers have agreed reform is needed. But in August 2025, Chalmers said the focus would be on electric vehicles, and that the government wasn't trying to "double-tax" petrol and diesel cars.

Then things slowed down. The May 2026 federal budget did not introduce a charge. The government said it "needs more work". Climate Change and Energy Minister Chris Bowen said: "I think a road-user charge is something that Australia should be working towards," but added: "It's got to be done right. It's got to be done carefully, and that includes timing." CarExpert reported that a 2028 start had been planned, but that the announcement was postponed amid record fuel prices and rising EV sales.

Transport Minister Catherine King has said the government doesn't want to discourage people from buying electric cars. The Greens oppose any charge that targets EV drivers. And the Australian Automobile Association, which represents the state motoring clubs, wants a charge legislated in the 2027 budget.

So the federal position, for now, is: a charge is coming eventually, it would mainly target EVs, and nobody is promising when. And remember, the government is also changing EV incentives at the same time. It extended the Electric Car Discount on novated leases to April 2027, after which the full discount will be limited to vehicles priced under $75,000. Put a smaller tax break and a new per-kilometre charge together, and the cost of owning an EV in 2027 and 2028 could look quite different from today.

### ch04
And that's where Tesla comes in – with a twist almost nobody expected.

In a submission to a federal parliamentary inquiry, the Select Committee on Productivity in Australia, Tesla Australia argued that a road-user charge should not apply only to electric cars. It said the charge should "apply it universally to all vehicles on the road, phased-in," with the rate based on "real road funding costs".

Here's the controversial part. Tesla proposed keeping fuel excise as well. That means drivers of newly registered petrol, diesel and hybrid vehicles would eventually pay both: the excise on their fuel, and a charge for every kilometre.

Why would Tesla want that? Its argument is that an EV-only charge "acts as a financial penalty for decarbonising personal transport". It said a charge on EVs alone would "distort" what people think motoring costs. And it argued that fuel excise doesn't really change behaviour, because "consumers remain largely inelastic to the tax component of their travel", while a per-kilometre charge would encourage people to skip unnecessary trips and use public or active transport.

Of course, Tesla sells only electric cars. A system that taxes its customers less, and everyone else more, would suit its business. That doesn't make the argument wrong – but it's worth knowing who's making it.

### ch06
Tesla isn't the only one pushing for a charge on everyone.

On the 17th of September 2026, the Australian Electric Vehicle Association – a volunteer-run, not-for-profit group – released its own proposal. Electric cars would pay a road-user charge. Petrol and diesel vehicles would pay the same charge, plus the existing fuel excise, which the association calls a "highly efficient pollution tax".

Its version is based on weight and distance. The rate would phase in over ten years to three cents per tonne-kilometre. The association's own example: a 2.2-tonne diesel ute or electric SUV driven 13,000 kilometres a year would pay $858 annually. Odometer readings would be taken every six or twelve months.

AEVA policy convenor Dr Chris Jones said that for vehicles of equal weight and distance, "it's only fair that they pay the same road user charge. But if one of those vehicles burns petrol or diesel, they should pay for the cost of that pollution too." He added: "We're not after a free ride, but we are asking for policy which encourages EV adoption."

Polestar agrees with the principle. In October 2026, Polestar Australia's managing director, Scott Maynard, told CarExpert he sees "the inevitable introduction of a road-user charge", and that it should apply to all motorists, based on weight. He pointed out: "We don't have a congestion tax in Australia."

And it's not just electric car companies. The Australian Automotive Dealer Association, led by James Voortman, has called for a national road-user charge for all vehicle types, as part of a full review of vehicle taxes. The Australian Automobile Association has called for a distance-based charge, developed with the states and modelled on the NSW plan.

### ch07
There's also a version that tries to protect working families.

In May 2026, the McKell Institute released a study called Fairer Roads, backed by the Electric Vehicle Council. It proposed introducing a road-user charge once electric cars reach thirty per cent of Australia's whole fleet – not just new sales. Rates would be set by income, across four levels from 3.74 to 12.86 cents per kilometre. Once electric vehicles passed seventy-five per cent of the fleet, the charge would apply to all vehicles and replace fuel excise. In the meantime, fuel-powered cars would pay an extra carbon surcharge of half a cent to one and a half cents per kilometre.

McKell's chief executive, Edward Cavanough, argued that families in the outer suburbs drive the furthest and have the fewest alternatives, so a flat per-kilometre charge would hit them hardest.

And interestingly, a lot of EV owners don't object. Research released by Transport Australia found 71 per cent of electric and hybrid drivers support a road-user charge – the highest support of any group surveyed. The debate isn't really about whether EVs should pay. It's about whether everyone else should pay more too.

### ch08
So what could this actually cost you? Let me be clear: these are illustrations based on the published proposals, not confirmed prices. None of them is law at the federal level yet.

Say you drive an electric car 15,000 kilometres a year in New South Wales. At the legislated NSW rate of 3.095 cents, that's about $464 a year.

Now say the charge was set at the level that would roughly match what a petrol car pays in excise. Secondary reporting of a Parliamentary Budget Office costing puts that at about 5.3 cents per kilometre. Over 15,000 kilometres, that's around $795 a year.

Under the electric vehicle association's weight-based plan, that 2.2-tonne ute or SUV doing 13,000 kilometres would pay $858 a year. If it's a diesel ute, that comes on top of fuel excise. A ute using around nine litres per hundred kilometres would burn about 1,170 litres over that distance – roughly $628 in excise at today's rate. Together, that's nearly fifteen hundred dollars a year in road taxes for one vehicle.

And under Tesla's proposal, newly registered petrol, diesel and hybrid cars would also pay both – the exact rate would depend on what governments decided the real cost of roads is.

That's why this matters to every driver, not just EV owners. The difference between the proposals is the difference between a few hundred dollars a year and well over a thousand.

### ch09
So who's right? Here are the strongest arguments on both sides.

Supporters say a road-user charge is simply fairer. Everyone uses the roads, so everyone should pay for them. Heavier vehicles cause more wear, so weight-based charges make sense. And without reform, the money for roads, potholes and highways will keep shrinking as the fleet goes electric.

Critics say a charge on top of fuel excise is a double tax, at a time when Australians are already struggling with the cost of living and record fuel prices. Regional and outer-suburban drivers, who travel the longest distances and have no public transport, would pay the most. Some worry about privacy, if kilometres are tracked by GPS rather than odometer readings. EV advocates warn that charging electric cars too early could slow the switch away from petrol and diesel. And the High Court ruling means any state scheme could face a legal challenge.

There's also the question of how the money is collected. The simplest approach is odometer readings – at rego renewal, at a service, or when a car is sold. That's what New Zealand and the UK rely on. GPS tracking can be more precise and could allow peak-hour or city-based pricing, but it raises bigger privacy concerns. And any system has to stop people winding back odometers or under-reporting their distance.

Both sides agree on one thing: fuel excise alone won't pay for Australia's roads forever. The fight is over who pays, how much, and when.

### ch10
So what should you do right now?

First, don't panic. As of September 2026, no road-user charge was being collected anywhere in Australia, and the federal government has not legislated one.

Second, if you're buying an electric car or a plug-in hybrid in New South Wales, factor in a possible charge from July 2027. At around three cents per kilometre, budget roughly four to five hundred dollars a year for typical driving. It still usually works out cheaper than fuel – but it's not nothing.

Third, keep good records of your odometer readings and servicing. Every proposal so far relies on distance, and accurate records protect you if you're ever charged.

And fourth, have your say. Federal inquiries and state reviews take public submissions. Motoring clubs, dealer groups, EV groups and car companies are all lobbying hard. Ordinary drivers should too.

Here's the timeline to watch. NSW's charge is due from the 1st of July 2027, unless it's delayed, changed or challenged in court. The motoring clubs want a federal charge legislated in the 2027 budget. And the next federal election will decide which party gets to write the rules.

So, could every Aussie driver end up paying a new road tax? It's being seriously proposed – by Tesla, by electric vehicle groups, by car dealers and by motoring clubs. The federal government says it's focused on EVs, and it isn't promising a date. But the old system is running out of road, and change is coming.
