And one more thing for anyone thinking about salary packaging. Neither of these cars gets the fringe benefits tax exemption on a novated lease. Plug-in hybrids lost that exemption on the 1st of April 2025. Only fully electric cars still qualify. So if you're comparing these two through a lease, the tax treatment is the same – but if you're also considering a fully electric SUV, that could change the maths a lot.

Third, and this is the big one: resale value.

Toyota's reputation for holding value is famous. A 2025 analysis by Pickles and Datium Insights found that a two-year-old Toyota RAV4 kept about eighty-eight per cent of its value. That's one of the best results of any car in Australia.

We don't yet have long-term resale data for the new H7 – it's brand new. But the broader story is clear: newer brands, and models that get replaced quickly, often lose value faster. The old H7 was replaced after just over a year. And when a model gets a quick facelift or a big price cut, the value of the older version usually takes a hit.

One Cars24 reader put it bluntly in the comments on the H7's launch: there's "no promotional discount for a car delivered in Nov/Dec," and it "will lose value as soon as we hit 2027." That's one buyer's opinion, not data. But it's a fair point to think about. If you buy any car in November or December, it'll be a year older on paper just weeks later.

Insurance is worth checking too. In April 2026, Compare the Market reported that average premiums for battery electric vehicles had risen about ten per cent in a year, and that average premiums for some plug-in hybrids – like the BYD Sealion 6 – had risen about eighteen per cent. Premiums vary a lot by insurer, model, postcode and driver, so get real quotes for both cars before you decide.

So when you compare the two, don't just look at the price you pay. Look at the price you'll get back when you sell. A car that's nine thousand dollars cheaper but loses ten thousand dollars more in value isn't actually cheaper.
