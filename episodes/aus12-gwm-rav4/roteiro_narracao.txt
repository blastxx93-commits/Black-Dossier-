### ch01
First, a bit of context about who GWM is. Great Wall Motors – GWM – has been selling cars in Australia for eighteen years, and in October 2026 it was celebrating that anniversary with price cuts across its range. Its Haval Jolion small SUV has become one of the best-selling cars in Australia in 2026. And like most Chinese brands, it's been growing fast while some long-established brands go backwards.

You can see that shift in the official sales numbers. In September 2026, according to VFACTS, Geely sales were up 870 per cent on the same month a year earlier, Omoda Jaecoo was up 344 per cent, Leapmotor 106 per cent and BYD 61 per cent. Toyota, by comparison, was down 3.5 per cent. Toyota is still the biggest brand in the country by a long way – but the pressure is coming from every direction.

First, let's meet the challenger.

The new GWM Haval H7 is a boxy, five-seat mid-size SUV. It's actually the second H7 GWM has sold here in a short time. CarExpert reported that the outgoing model was replaced after "a little over a year" on sale – and to the end of July 2026, GWM had delivered only 636 of the old H7s, compared with 8,759 of its Haval H6. So the new one has a lot to prove.

It's also bigger than you might expect. CarExpert lists the five-seater at 4,680 millimetres long – 4,800 including the spare wheel carrier on the tailgate – and 1,950 millimetres wide, with a 2,738-millimetre wheelbase. That boxy shape is a big part of its appeal. It looks more like a rugged off-roader than a typical family SUV.

It comes in two powertrains, both with all-wheel drive as standard.

The hybrid uses a 1.5-litre turbo petrol engine with electric motors, for 182 kilowatts and 670 newton-metres, with claimed fuel use of 7.0 litres per hundred kilometres.

The plug-in hybrid uses the same 1.5-litre turbo petrol engine, but with a much bigger 33.7-kilowatt-hour battery. Combined output is a huge 320 kilowatts and 670 newton-metres. GWM claims 0 to 100 kilometres an hour in 4.9 seconds, an electric-only range of 137 kilometres, and a combined range of 844 kilometres on the Chinese WLTC test.

And GWM says interest has been strong. It reported around 1,500 expressions of interest after the H7's debut at the Everything Electric show in September. GWM Australia's head of marketing, Steve Maciver, said: "We have high expectations for the all-new Haval H7."

### ch04
Let's put them side by side.

Plug-in hybrid versus plug-in hybrid. The H7 Lux PHEV: $49,990 drive-away, 320 kilowatts, all-wheel drive, 137 kilometres of claimed electric range. The RAV4 XSE PHEV front-wheel drive: $58,840 before on-road costs, 201 kilowatts, 121 kilometres of claimed electric range.

And for families who mostly do short trips, a claimed 137 kilometres of electric range is a big deal. If you charge at home every night, many commutes and school runs could be done without burning any petrol at all.

Let's do a quick illustration – and I want to be clear, these are example numbers, not official figures. Say you drive forty kilometres a day, about 14,600 kilometres a year. A petrol SUV using seven litres per hundred kilometres, at two dollars a litre, would cost around $2,044 a year in fuel. If you could do most of that driving on electricity charged at home, and your plug-in hybrid used around twenty kilowatt-hours per hundred kilometres at thirty cents per kilowatt-hour, that's around $876 a year. Your own numbers will depend on your electricity plan, your driving and the car. But it shows why electric range matters so much for daily drivers.

### ch06
First, towing. Australians love to tow. CarExpert lists the new H7's braked towing capacity at 1,500 kilograms, for both the hybrid and the plug-in hybrid. That's fine for a small trailer or a light camper. But if you're planning to tow a large caravan or a boat, check the exact towing figures for every car you're considering before you buy – and remember that plug-in hybrids use more fuel and electricity when towing.

Second, warranty. GWM offers a seven-year, unlimited-kilometre warranty on its vehicles, according to its Australian website. And its hybrid and plug-in hybrid batteries are covered for eight years with unlimited kilometres. One detail in the fine print: for plug-in hybrids and EVs, GWM says a battery state of health of seventy per cent or more isn't considered a warranty defect.

Toyota's base warranty is five years with unlimited kilometres. CarsGuide reported that Toyota extends engine and driveline cover to up to seven years, and hybrid battery cover to up to ten years, as long as the car is serviced and maintained according to the warranty and service book. So on paper, the warranties are closer than they first look – but the conditions are different, and it's worth reading them both.

### ch07
And one more thing for anyone thinking about salary packaging. Neither of these cars gets the fringe benefits tax exemption on a novated lease. Plug-in hybrids lost that exemption on the 1st of April 2025. Only fully electric cars still qualify. So if you're comparing these two through a lease, the tax treatment is the same – but if you're also considering a fully electric SUV, that could change the maths a lot.

Third, and this is the big one: resale value.

Toyota's reputation for holding value is famous. A 2025 analysis by Pickles and Datium Insights found that a two-year-old Toyota RAV4 kept about eighty-eight per cent of its value. That's one of the best results of any car in Australia.

We don't yet have long-term resale data for the new H7 – it's brand new. But the broader story is clear: newer brands, and models that get replaced quickly, often lose value faster. The old H7 was replaced after just over a year. And when a model gets a quick facelift or a big price cut, the value of the older version usually takes a hit.

One Cars24 reader put it bluntly in the comments on the H7's launch: there's "no promotional discount for a car delivered in Nov/Dec," and it "will lose value as soon as we hit 2027." That's one buyer's opinion, not data. But it's a fair point to think about. If you buy any car in November or December, it'll be a year older on paper just weeks later.

Insurance is worth checking too. In April 2026, Compare the Market reported that average premiums for battery electric vehicles had risen about ten per cent in a year, and that average premiums for some plug-in hybrids – like the BYD Sealion 6 – had risen about eighteen per cent. Premiums vary a lot by insurer, model, postcode and driver, so get real quotes for both cars before you decide.

So when you compare the two, don't just look at the price you pay. Look at the price you'll get back when you sell. A car that's nine thousand dollars cheaper but loses ten thousand dollars more in value isn't actually cheaper.

### ch08
Before we get to Toyota's counter-attack, there's one more thing worth considering: the dealer and service network. Toyota has one of the largest dealer networks in Australia, including in regional towns. That matters for servicing, warranty repairs and crash repairs. As we saw in our video on BYD after the crash, a great car can still be a frustrating ownership experience if parts take weeks or the nearest approved repairer is hours away. GWM has been in Australia for eighteen years and has a substantial network, but before you buy any car, check where your nearest service centre and approved repairer actually are.

And here's the twist: Toyota isn't standing still.

In the first three months of 2027, Toyota is adding a cheaper RAV4 GXL plug-in hybrid. Toyota hasn't announced the price yet. CarExpert estimated it could be around $53,990 before on-road costs, based on the gap between the hybrid and plug-in hybrid versions of the RAV4. That's an estimate, not an official figure. It's expected to use the same 201-kilowatt plug-in hybrid system with a claimed 121 kilometres of electric range, and to become the most affordable RAV4 plug-in hybrid. CarExpert also said it's expected to undercut the Mitsubishi Outlander plug-in hybrid, which starts at $58,990 before on-road costs.

Why is Toyota moving so fast? Partly because of the government's emissions rules. Under the New Vehicle Efficiency Standard, the target for passenger cars drops from 117 grams of carbon dioxide per kilometre in 2026 to 92 grams in 2027. Plug-in hybrids help car makers hit those targets. Toyota is already earning credits thanks to its hybrids – but it needs them, because its utes and big four-wheel drives are well above the targets. Every RAV4 plug-in hybrid it sells helps balance the books.

So in 2027, the gap between the RAV4 PHEV and the H7 PHEV could shrink – although if that estimate is right, the RAV4 would still be more expensive once on-road costs are added.

GWM is moving too. The seven-seat H7, due in 2027, gets a longer 2,850-millimetre wheelbase and stretches to 4,912 millimetres long. That puts it into the territory of bigger family SUVs – a part of the market where Toyota doesn't sell a RAV4 at all.

And GWM won't be the only one fighting for RAV4 buyers. The plug-in hybrid SUV market is packed with Chinese rivals – from BYD, Chery, Geely and Omoda Jaecoo – many of them with big batteries, lots of equipment and sharp drive-away prices. In September 2026, BYD was Australia's second best-selling brand. The pressure on Toyota is real.

### ch09
So, which one should you buy? Here's how I'd decide.

Buy the GWM Haval H7 plug-in hybrid if price, power and electric range matter most to you. If you do lots of short trips, can charge at home, want all-wheel drive and a long warranty, and plan to keep the car for a long time – so resale value matters less – the H7 offers a lot of car for the money.

Buy the Toyota RAV4 plug-in hybrid if resale value, brand reputation and dealer network matter most. Toyota's history of holding value and its huge service network, especially in regional Australia, are real advantages. And if you can wait until early 2027, the cheaper GXL plug-in hybrid could narrow the price gap.

And think about how you'll actually use the battery. A plug-in hybrid only saves you real money if you plug it in. If you can't charge at home, or you mostly do long highway trips, a regular hybrid – like the H7 hybrid or the RAV4 hybrid – may make more sense, and it's cheaper to buy. With petrol and diesel prices as volatile as they've been in 2026 – diesel hit as high as $3.50 a litre in some parts of Australia in May, according to CarExpert – the right choice depends on your driving, not the brochure.

On the test drive, pay attention to a few things. How does the car drive when the battery is flat and the petrol engine is doing the work? Is the engine noisy? How easy are the touchscreen and the driver assistance systems to live with? And how much boot space is left once the charging cable is in there? These are the things that make a car pleasant or annoying after the first week.

And whichever you choose, do these four things. Test drive both. Compare drive-away prices, not sticker prices. Check towing capacity if you tow. And look at the real running costs: fuel, electricity, servicing and insurance.
