Two hundred and forty-three. That's how close BYD came to beating Toyota in Australia in June 2026. Two hundred and forty-three cars, in a single month, separating the most powerful car brand in the country from a Chinese company most Australians hadn't heard of five years ago.

Toyota has been Australia's best-selling brand since 2003 – the year it overtook Holden. Holden. The brand that was once as Australian as meat pies and footy. Seventeen years after Toyota passed it, Holden was gone.

The Prado, the Kluger, the Yaris – down forty, fifty, even seventy per cent in some months. ### ch01
First, you need to understand just how dominant Toyota has been.

In 2024, Toyota sold 241,296 new vehicles in Australia – an all-time record. In 2025, almost one in every five new vehicles sold in this country was a Toyota. Its market share was above twenty per cent.

Toyota's rise is one of the great business stories in Australian history. It built cars here for decades, including the Camry at its factory in Altona, Victoria, until local manufacturing ended in 2017. It took the market lead from Holden in 2003 and never gave it back. For more than twenty years, through recessions, a pandemic and a supply crisis, Toyota has finished every single year as Australia's number one car brand.

Think about the names. The HiLux, which for years fought the Ford Ranger for the title of Australia's best-selling vehicle. The RAV4, the country's favourite family SUV. The LandCruiser, a legend from the outback to the city. The Prado, the Corolla, the Camry, the Kluger. For millions of Australians, buying a Toyota wasn't just a choice. It was the default – the car that held its value, never let you down and could be fixed in any country town.

That reputation shows up in the resale numbers. A 2025 analysis by Pickles and Datium Insights found a two-year-old RAV4 kept about 88 per cent of its value – one of the best results of any car in Australia. The LandCruiser also kept around 88 per cent, and the HiLux about 85.

Toyota didn't just lead the market. It was the market. And that's exactly why what happened in 2026 has shocked the industry.

### ch02
Here's the slide, in Toyota's own numbers.

To the end of May 2026, Toyota delivered 76,017 vehicles in Australia – down 24.6 per cent on the same period in 2025. Its share of the new-car market fell from 20.7 per cent to 16 per cent. In May alone, Toyota sales were down almost 31 per cent.

And it was across the range. According to CarExpert, by the end of May, the LandCruiser Prado was down 45 per cent for the year, and in May itself it fell almost 64 per cent. The Kluger was down almost 40 per cent for the year, and 70.7 per cent in May. The Yaris was down 44.4 per cent. The Yaris Cross, almost 32. RAV4 sales were down 47 per cent year to date. Even the HiLux was down 13 per cent. And LandCruiser 70 Series ute sales were down 82 per cent.

Toyota's explanation for the Prado was simple. A spokesperson said: "The Prado May result reflects lower supply on this very popular model." And not everything was falling. CarExpert reported that LandCruiser 300 and 70 Series wagon sales combined were actually up 29.3 per cent over the first five months of the year. In other words, where Toyota could get cars, people were still buying them.

By September, things had improved, but the year is still well down. Year to date, Toyota has sold 152,944 vehicles, down 15.9 per cent. Its market share is 16.7 per cent, down from 19.9 a year earlier. Toyota is still comfortably number one – but it's selling almost 29,000 fewer cars than it did at the same point last year.

For any other brand, that would be a bad year. For Toyota, it's a historic one.

### ch03
And while Toyota was falling, someone else was rising very fast.

In June 2026, Toyota sold 19,124 vehicles. BYD sold 18,881. The gap was just 243 cars. Toyota held 13.7 per cent of the market that month. BYD had 13.5. A Chinese brand that only started selling cars here a few years ago was almost level with the most powerful car company in Australia.

The head of the Federal Chamber of Automotive Industries, Tony Weber, said: "The Australian automotive market has shifted on its axis during the first months of 2026." BYD's chief operating officer, Stephen Collins, said: "We asked our customers to back us in, and they did, and we delivered."

Now, to be fair, BYD hasn't caught Toyota since. In September, Toyota sold 17,682 vehicles to BYD's 8,191. Year to date, Toyota has sold roughly double BYD's total. But BYD's growth – up 102 per cent this year – shows how quickly the ground can move.

And it isn't only BYD. Chinese-built cars now make up almost four in ten new vehicles sold in Australia. China overtook Japan as Australia's biggest source of new cars in February 2026 – for the first time since 1998. Most of the sales Chinese brands have gained have come from Japanese brands. And the biggest Japanese brand of all is Toyota.

But here's an important detail that often gets lost. Toyota is actually doing better than most of its Japanese rivals. Year to date, Toyota is down 15.9 per cent. Mazda is down 16.2. Mitsubishi is down 30.2. Subaru is down 29.3. And Nissan is down 33.3 per cent. If Toyota is in trouble, the rest of Japan's car industry in Australia is in much deeper trouble.

### ch04
To understand why people are comparing Toyota to Holden, you need to remember how Holden fell.

Holden was the pride of Australian motoring for most of the twentieth century. The Commodore was the country's favourite car for years. Holden and Ford's rivalry defined Bathurst, and generations of families drove nothing else.

But in 2003, Toyota overtook Holden as Australia's top-selling car maker. Holden kept selling large numbers of cars, but buyers were moving to SUVs and utes, and to Japanese, Korean and then Chinese brands. In 2013, Holden announced it would end local manufacturing. The last Australian-built Commodore rolled off the line in 2017. The imported cars that replaced it never caught on in the same way.

Then, on the 17th of February 2020, General Motors announced it would retire the Holden brand altogether. GM's international operations boss, Julian Blissett, pointed to "highly fragmented right-hand-drive markets", and said GM "regret[s] that we could not prioritize the investment required for Holden" to succeed. GM called the decision "agonizing". By the end of 2020, Holden was gone.

The lesson most people take from Holden is this: a famous badge can carry a brand for a long time – but not forever. When the products stop matching what buyers want, loyalty runs out, and it can run out faster than anyone expects.

It's worth remembering that Holden's decline wasn't sudden, even if the end was. For years, the warning signs were there: falling market share, fewer models that buyers really wanted, and a parent company that was losing interest. At each step, plenty of people said Holden was too big and too Australian to disappear. Until it did.

### ch06
So, is Toyota the next Holden? Let's start with the case for the prosecution.

In May 2026, CarsGuide's Stephen Ottley wrote the column that started this debate. His argument was that Toyota risks repeating Holden's mistakes – relying on its badge and on updated versions of familiar nameplates, while Chinese brands pour into the market with all-new cars at a ferocious pace. He wrote that "there are no guarantees in this business and fortunes can suddenly change for even the biggest brand."

There's evidence to support that worry. Chinese brands now offer far more electric and plug-in hybrid choices, often with more power and more equipment for less money. The number of Chinese models on sale in Australia is expected to grow from 72 to around 120 by 2027. And the market isn't growing – total sales for the first nine months of 2026 were almost exactly flat. Every new rival's sale is coming from someone.

Price is a big part of it. The cheapest RAV4 plug-in hybrid starts at $58,840 before on-road costs. GWM's new Haval H7 plug-in hybrid launched at $49,990 drive-away – with more power, all-wheel drive and a longer claimed electric range. Toyota buyers have always been willing to pay a little more for the badge and the resale. The question is how much more – and whether that gap is now too big.

Then there's regulation. Under the New Vehicle Efficiency Standard, the emissions target for utes and vans drops sharply in 2027. Toyota's sales boss, John Pappas, has called those targets "really challenging", and Toyota has said it "cannot rule out" price increases. For a brand that sells huge numbers of diesel utes and four-wheel drives, that's a real risk.

And there's the question of whether loyalty is weakening. Toyota's sales of icons like the Prado and Kluger fell hard in 2026 – and some of those buyers will have discovered other brands while they waited. Once a buyer tries something else and likes it, they don't always come back.

And the brand count keeps growing. Pappas himself has pointed out that "In the past five years the number of brands sold in Australia has grown significantly" – there are now nearly seventy. Every one of those brands is fighting for a slice of the same pie. Even if Toyota keeps its customers, it will be very hard to win back the twenty per cent share it had in 2025.

### ch07
Now, the case for the defence – and it's strong.

First, Toyota says most of the 2026 drop was about supply, not demand. The new-generation RAV4 was delayed from August 2025 to April 2026, and there was no full HiLux production run in the first quarter. In July, John Pappas said: "This has predominantly been a production and stock related issue." He added: "We don't determine global production." Toyota secured two extra shipments totalling 30,000 vehicles, and at one stage, wait times for top-selling models were three to six months. A brand with empty showrooms because it can't get enough cars is not the same as a brand nobody wants.

There's another telling statistic. In early 2026, accounting firm BDO reported that a typical Toyota dealer was selling around 72 new cars a month – the highest of any major brand. A BYD dealer averaged 48. Toyota's network is still the most productive in the country.

Second, the numbers are already turning. In September, the RAV4 was the second-best-selling vehicle in the country, with 4,624 sales – up 81 per cent. In October, Pappas said Toyota's order books were still "very, very healthy". Asked whether Toyota was planning for decline, he said: "No. We don't plan to be declining. We plan to continue to grow."

Third, and most importantly, Ottley himself said the comparison only goes so far. In his words: "Toyota is certainly in no danger of collapsing like Holden did, and it has the tools it needs to reverse its sales skid, but it must act."

And the differences with Holden are huge. Holden depended on a parent company in Detroit that decided it was no longer worth the investment. Toyota is one of the biggest car makers in the world, with more than twenty years of experience selling hybrids. Holden lost its best-selling product when local manufacturing ended. Toyota's best sellers – RAV4, HiLux, LandCruiser – are all still here, and the RAV4 and HiLux have both just been replaced with new generations.

### ch08
So what is Toyota actually doing to fight back?

The new RAV4 is the centrepiece. It arrived in 2026 with Toyota's first-ever RAV4 plug-in hybrid, and in July, plug-in hybrids made up 39 per cent of RAV4 sales – about 2,170 cars – making it the best-selling plug-in hybrid in the country. A cheaper RAV4 GXL plug-in hybrid is due in the first months of 2027.

The new HiLux arrived with a 48-volt mild-hybrid diesel and, for the first time, a fully electric version. And Toyota is doing something it rarely needs to do: offering deals. In October 2026, it launched a four-thousand-dollar deposit bonus on HiLux SR5 models bought through Toyota Finance – a move CarsGuide described as rare.

Pappas has said Toyota is "targeting a very strong, record fourth quarter sales for 2026", with extra supply arriving. Toyota has forecast around 230,000 sales for the year, and expects its sales to stabilise in 2027. And it still expects to finish 2026 as Australia's number one brand.

Toyota has also set a target for thirty per cent of its sales to be zero- or low-emission vehicles by 2030, and it has hybrids in almost every part of the market, from the Yaris Cross to the LandCruiser Prado. That matters, because the emissions rules reward exactly the kinds of cars Toyota has spent two decades perfecting.

That's not the behaviour of a company in denial. It's the behaviour of a giant that's been badly bruised – and knows it.

### ch09
So what does this mean if you own a Toyota, or you're thinking of buying one?

If you already own a Toyota, there's no reason to panic. Toyota has one of the biggest dealer and service networks in the country, and its cars still hold their value better than almost anything else on the market. Nothing in Toyota's 2026 numbers changes your warranty, your servicing or your parts supply.

If you're buying, this could actually be your moment. For years, Toyota buyers paid full price and waited months for a car. Now supply is improving, the competition is fierce, and Toyota is offering deals it rarely offered before. Ask for a written drive-away price, compare it with a Chinese rival, and use that comparison to negotiate. A brand that has lost market share needs your sale more than it used to.

But keep an eye on resale. Toyota's famous resale values depend on strong demand for used Toyotas. If more buyers switch to other brands, and if new Toyota prices rise because of emissions rules, the used market could change too. It hasn't happened yet – but it's worth watching.

And if you've been waiting months for a Toyota, now is a good time to call the dealer again. With extra shipments arriving and a record quarter targeted, stock should be better than at any point this year. Ask whether a demonstrator or an earlier build is available – it can save you time and money.

### ch10
So, is Toyota the next Holden?

On the evidence, no – not yet, and probably not soon. Toyota is still the biggest brand in Australia by a long way. Its biggest models are brand new, its sales are recovering as supply improves, and it has the money, the technology and the network that Holden never had at the end.

But the era of Toyota's easy dominance is over. A Chinese brand came within 243 cars of it in a single month. Its market share has fallen from around twenty per cent to under seventeen. And the competition is only getting stronger.

Here are the warning signs to watch. If Toyota's share falls below fifteen per cent for a full year. If BYD or another rival beats Toyota in a full calendar month. If Toyota starts cutting models without replacements. Or if its famous resale values start to slip. Any one of those would be a big story. Two or three together would be a crisis.
