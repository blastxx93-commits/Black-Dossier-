### ch01
First, let's look at what's actually happening, because the numbers from September 2026 are remarkable.

Australians bought 108,778 new vehicles in September. That's up slightly on the same month last year. So the market isn't collapsing. People are still buying cars. What's changing is who they're buying from.

Look at the brands that grew. BYD, up sixty-one per cent, now Australia's second best-selling brand for the year. Geely, up 870 per cent. Omoda and Jaecoo, up 344 per cent. Zeekr, up more than sixteen hundred per cent. Leapmotor more than doubled.

Now look at the other side. Even big, established names went backwards. Kia, down nineteen and a half per cent in September. Mazda, down nearly nineteen. Hyundai, down twenty-two. Among the top ten brands, the only ones that grew were Tesla and the Chinese brands.

There's another big shift too. In September, electric vehicles made up 24.2 per cent of all new car sales – more than petrol cars, for the second month in a row. Petrol sales fell thirty-six per cent year-on-year. Diesel fell eighteen. If your range is mostly petrol and diesel, 2026 is a very hard year to be selling cars.

And the market is crowded. Around seventy brands are now fighting for roughly 1.2 million sales a year. Every sale a new brand wins comes out of someone else's pocket. So why is this happening now? There are three big forces hitting at once.

The first is price. Chinese brands arrived with well-equipped hybrids and EVs at prices the established brands struggle to match. When a family can get a plug-in hybrid SUV with a big screen and a long warranty for the same money as a basic petrol SUV, plenty of them are making the switch.

The second is fuel. Petrol and diesel are losing share every month, and the New Vehicle Efficiency Standard now pushes car makers to sell lower-emission vehicles or pay penalties. Brands with old, thirsty ranges are being squeezed from both sides – by buyers and by the rules.

The third is the sheer number of choices. A few years ago, Australia had around fifty brands. Now there are about seventy. A dealer body boss has already warned that more brands will leave. The pie isn't getting much bigger. It's just being cut into thinner and thinner slices.

So who's losing the most? Let's count down.

### ch02
Number ten: Deepal. And this one proves it's not simply old brands versus new Chinese brands.

Deepal is an electric brand from Chinese giant Changan, and it launched in Australia with big ambitions and a stylish range. But 2026 has been brutal. By the end of May, Deepal had sold just 594 cars for the year. In September, it sold sixty-three – down almost thirty-four per cent on the same month last year.

A big part of that was a stop-sale order on the E07, a quirky electric SUV with a ute-style tray that was meant to grab attention. Losing a key model to a stop-sale, in your first years in a brand-new market, is about as hard as it gets.

The lesson here is simple. Being Chinese and electric doesn't guarantee success in Australia. The newcomers are fighting each other just as hard as they're fighting Toyota.

### ch03
At number nine: KGM – the brand most Aussies still know as SsangYong.

KGM changed its name after a change of ownership in Korea, and it's been trying to rebuild ever since. But in September 2026 it sold just 189 vehicles, down thirty-seven per cent on the year before. That follows a twenty-four per cent drop across 2025.

KGM's strength has always been tough, good-value SUVs and the Musso ute. The problem is that this is exactly the space where GWM, LDV, BYD and now Chery are throwing everything they've got – more technology, plug-in hybrids, longer warranties, and very sharp drive-away prices.

Changing a brand's name is hard enough. Doing it in the most crowded market in its history is even harder.

### ch04
Number eight: Alfa Romeo. Beautiful cars. Passionate fans. Almost no buyers.

In September 2026, Alfa Romeo sold twenty-one cars in Australia, down almost sixty per cent. Through the first five months of the year it managed fewer than two hundred.

Alfa sits inside Stellantis Australia, alongside Fiat, Jeep and the Chinese EV brand Leapmotor. And that's the real question for Alfa. How long can a global company justify dealers, parts, marketing and compliance for a brand selling a couple of hundred cars a year in one market?

For now, Alfa Romeo is still here, and existing owners are supported. But with numbers like these, its future in Australia will be decided in Europe, not in Aussie showrooms.

### ch05
At number seven: Suzuki. And 2026 has been a rough year for the little brand.

Suzuki built its name here on small, affordable, fun cars – the Swift, the Vitara, and of course the cult-favourite Jimny. But by the end of May 2026, Suzuki sales were down twenty-two per cent. In September, they were down more than thirty-nine.

Part of it is safety ratings. The new Fronx small SUV launched strongly, then received a one-star ANCAP rating after a rear seatbelt failure in crash testing. Sales were halted while Suzuki's engineers investigated – and months later, in March 2026, the investigation was still dragging on. Suzuki's local boss even suggested that an earlier one-star result for the Swift may have scared off as many as one in five potential buyers. That's the brand's own estimate, but it shows how much a safety score matters.

The other part is price. Suzuki relies on cheap small cars, and that's exactly where Chinese rivals now offer similar or better equipment for similar money. Even the Jimny was down twelve per cent in the first five months.

### ch06
Number six: Volkswagen. This one surprises people, because Volkswagen is one of the biggest car makers on the planet.

But in Australia, it's shrinking fast. VW sold about 29,000 vehicles in 2025, down more than twenty per cent. By the end of May 2026 it was down another seventeen per cent. And in September, Volkswagen sold 1,599 cars – down almost thirty-seven per cent on the same month last year.

Look inside those numbers and it gets worse. In the first five months, the T-Roc was down fifty-six per cent. The T-Cross, down forty-nine. The Amarok ute, down more than thirty-three.

At a launch event in May, Volkswagen's passenger car boss in Australia, Piergiorgio Minto, said something very telling. Paraphrasing his point: once you're selling fewer than ten thousand vehicles a year, you're a niche brand. And he made it clear Volkswagen doesn't want to be niche.

The brand admits it has had gaps in its range, and that new models have often reached Australia years after their global debut. There's a new-generation T-Roc and more hybrids and plug-ins on the way. But the clock is ticking.

### ch08
Number five: Subaru.

Subaru has some of the most loyal owners in the country, especially in the bush and among outdoor families. But 2026 has been tough. Sales for the first five months were down twenty-two per cent – almost four thousand fewer cars than the year before. In September, Subaru sold 2,009 vehicles, down more than forty-two per cent.

To put that in perspective, Subaru sold more than 46,000 cars in 2023. Now it has slipped behind Geely, Omoda Jaecoo and even BMW on the sales charts.

Subaru's importer says this is partly planned. Managing director Blair Read described it as a rationalisation of the range – the Liberty is gone, STI models are paused, and the Forester and Outback were both replaced within twelve months. Subaru says it will have nine models in showrooms by the end of 2026, five of them hybrid or electric.

That's the plan. But in a market where buyers are rushing to cheaper electrified SUVs, Subaru needs that new range to work – and quickly.

### ch09
At number four: Nissan. Ten years ago, Nissan was one of the biggest players in Australia. In 2012 it sold almost eighty thousand cars here.

In 2026, it's fighting for survival on the sales charts. To the end of July, Nissan had delivered 15,445 vehicles – down almost thirty-four per cent. The Navara ute was down forty-nine per cent. The Qashqai was down a staggering seventy-three and a half per cent. Even the X-Trail, Nissan's best seller, was down fifteen. In September, the brand was still down thirty per cent.

Nissan has already cut the Juke and the Pathfinder from its local range, and the new Leaf has been postponed indefinitely. Globally, the company has been through a massive restructure, with factory closures and job cuts.

And here's the remarkable part. Nissan's new Oceania boss, Steve Milette, has said publicly that 2026 is the low point, and that he expects growth to return from next year. The plan includes hybrid X-Trails, the new Patrol, and possibly plug-in hybrid utes and SUVs built with Chinese partner Dongfeng.

When a brand's own boss calls this year the bottom, you know just how hard 2026 has been.

### ch10
Number three: Mitsubishi.

Mitsubishi used to be the smart value choice for Aussie families and tradies. In 2018 it sold almost 85,000 cars here. In 2024 it was the fifth best-selling brand in the country.

By the end of May 2026, Mitsubishi was down more than twenty-six per cent. And in September, it sold 2,769 vehicles – down forty-one and a half per cent.

What went wrong? The range shrank. The Pajero Sport and the Eclipse Cross were dropped in 2025, partly because of new safety rules. The new ASX is now a rebadged Renault, with a much higher starting price, and it sold just 695 units in the first five months of 2026. The Outlander, Mitsubishi's best seller, now faces plug-in hybrid rivals like the BYD Sealion 6. In fact, the only Mitsubishi not going backwards this year has been the Triton ute.

And here's the core problem. Mitsubishi's whole identity was value. But in 2026, the value end of the market belongs to Chinese brands with bigger ranges, more technology, and in many cases lower prices. A new boss started in January, and a new Pajero and a small EV are on the way. Mitsubishi needs them badly.

### ch11
Number two: Jeep. If you want to see what a long, slow decline looks like, this is it.

Remember the hook? Sixteen Jeeps sold in September 2026, down eighty-five per cent. Across the first eight months of the year, Jeep sold just 378 vehicles in Australia.

Back in 2014, Jeep sold more than thirty thousand cars here. That's a fall of around ninety-nine per cent in just over a decade. The once-popular Grand Cherokee was axed in 2025. The dealer network has shrunk from more than seventy to around forty-four, and some dealers have said publicly they feared the brand was being run down.

But there's a twist, and it only came out in October 2026. Jeep's parent company, Stellantis, has partnered with Chinese giant Dongfeng. Reports say future Jeep and Peugeot models could be built in China, in Dongfeng's Wuhan factory, as plug-in hybrids or EVs – aiming for sharper prices. So the brand famous for American off-roaders may end up being rescued by Chinese manufacturing. That tells you everything about where this market is heading.

### ch12
And number one: Peugeot.

In July 2026, Inchcape – the company that imported and distributed Peugeot in Australia for almost a decade – announced it was walking away from the brand.

The numbers explain why. Peugeot sold just 373 vehicles to the end of May 2026, down thirty-five per cent, and more than half of those were commercial vans. In all of 2025, it sold 1,350. Back in 2007, Peugeot sold 8,807 cars here. And in September 2026, it sold eighty-nine.

A facelifted 308 hatch was meant to arrive in the first half of 2026. It never came. Instead, right after the announcement, Peugeot slashed prices across its passenger range – drive-away discounts from six thousand dollars all the way up to $14,718. The flagship 5008 GT hybrid dropped by ten thousand dollars.

Stellantis insists Peugeot is not leaving Australia. It says the brand has a strong future here and will continue after a transition period of around a year. And as we just saw, Peugeot is also part of that new Dongfeng plan. But Inchcape already dropped Peugeot's sister brand, Citroën, back in 2024. Losing your importer is the clearest warning sign a brand can get – and that's why Peugeot is number one on this list.

### ch14
So what happens if you own a car from a brand that pulls back – or pulls out?

First, the good news: you're not left on your own. When Citroën stopped selling new cars, its importer confirmed it would keep honouring warranties on cars already sold. Peugeot owners are being told the same during the transition, and Jaguar retailers are still servicing and honouring warranty claims.

Second, the law is on your side. Under the Australian Consumer Law, manufacturers and importers must make spare parts and repair facilities available for a reasonable time after you buy. That doesn't disappear just because sales slow down.

Third, the real risk is resale value. Cars from brands that are shrinking – or look like they might leave – usually lose value faster. If you're buying one, use that to negotiate hard. Some of the biggest discounts in 2026 have come from exactly these brands. And if you're selling, it may pay to sell sooner rather than later.

Finally, before you buy any car from a smaller brand, ask the dealer three things. How long is the warranty, and who backs it? Where is the nearest service centre? And how quickly can they get common parts? The answers will tell you how much risk you're taking on.

And one more thing worth remembering: a struggling brand isn't automatically a bad car. Some of the brands on this list make genuinely excellent vehicles. A Subaru Forester or a Volkswagen Golf doesn't suddenly become a worse car because fewer people are buying them. But when sales fall, dealers close, service networks shrink, and resale values soften. So the question isn't just "is this a good car?" It's "will this brand still look after me in five years?
