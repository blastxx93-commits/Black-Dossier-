So why did Tesla finally pull the plug? Tesla hasn't given a detailed public reason beyond that email. But the numbers tell the story very clearly.

Start with sales. Musk once predicted the Cybertruck could sell 250,000 a year. The reality has been very different. According to estimates reported by CarExpert, around 38,965 were sold in 2024. That fell to about 20,237 in 2025. And from January to September 2026, it was around 11,586 – down another twenty-eight per cent.

Then there's the price. The truck that was supposed to start at $39,900 now sells for around eighty thousand US dollars for the dual-motor version in America. EV Central estimated that in Australia, it would likely have cost around $130,000 before on-road costs and local taxes. That's a very hard sell.

Then there's right-hand drive. The Cybertruck is built only in left-hand drive. It isn't sold in any right-hand-drive market. Australia would likely have been the main one – other right-hand-drive markets like the UK and Japan aren't natural homes for a truck this big either. Building a right-hand-drive version just for us would mean spending a fortune for a tiny number of sales.

And finally, the size of the market. The whole full-size American pickup segment in Australia – trucks like the Ram, the Chevrolet Silverado and the Ford F-150 – sells roughly ten thousand vehicles a year. Even if Tesla grabbed a big slice of that, it would never justify the cost of re-engineering and certifying the Cybertruck for local roads.

Put all of that together, and the decision was almost made for them.

And there's one more factor: the Cybertruck's track record in America. According to the official US recall database, the Cybertruck has been recalled eleven times since it launched. In April 2024, it was recalled because the accelerator pedal pad could come loose and get trapped by the interior trim – which could cause the truck to keep accelerating. In March 2025, more than 46,000 trucks were recalled because an exterior trim panel could detach while driving. In October 2025, more than 63,000 were recalled because the front parking lights were too bright to meet US rules. And in April 2026, there was a recall over brake rotor stud holes that could crack, allowing a wheel stud to separate.

Recalls happen to every brand, and a recall is the system working. But for a low-volume truck that would have needed expensive local engineering, every extra problem made the Australian business case even weaker.
