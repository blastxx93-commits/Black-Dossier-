Let's look at the big American pickups first, because that's the segment the Cybertruck would have competed in.

Australia already has the Ram 1500, the Chevrolet Silverado and the Ford F-150, all converted to right-hand drive for our market. In March 2026, the F-150 sold 270, the Ram 1500 sold 181, and the Silverado 172. These are big, expensive trucks, and they sell in small numbers.

And they've had their own problems this year. In October 2026, Ford recalled the F-150 in Australia over a fire risk. CarExpert notes Ford issues more recalls here than any other car maker.

And there's a reason these trucks cost so much here. They aren't built in right-hand drive at the factory. The Ram 1500 and the Chevrolet Silverado are remanufactured in Melbourne by the Walkinshaw Automotive Group, and the F-150 is converted by RMA Automotive, also in Melbourne. Each truck arrives in left-hand drive and is partly taken apart and rebuilt with the steering on the right. It's skilled, expensive work – and it's a big part of why these pickups cost more than a hundred thousand dollars in many versions.

That's exactly the work the Cybertruck would have needed. Except with its unusual steer-by-wire system, its giant central screen and its stainless steel body, it would have been even more complicated – unless Tesla built a right-hand-drive version itself.

The lesson is simple. Even the established American trucks are a niche in Australia. The Cybertruck would have been a niche within a niche.

And Tesla isn't the only company that has quietly walked away from a promise in 2026. Isuzu has cancelled its plans to bring the electric D-Max to Australia. Nissan has indefinitely postponed the new-generation Leaf here, and cut the Juke and the Pathfinder from its range. Jaguar stopped selling new petrol cars in Australia on the 31st of December 2025, and still has no confirmed local launch date for its new electric models – which means it sold zero new cars here in September. And Volvo has axed the S60 sedan and the V60 Cross Country wagon from its local line-up.

In a market this crowded – with around seventy brands fighting for roughly 1.2 million sales a year – car makers are being ruthless. If a model can't sell in big enough numbers, it doesn't come, or it doesn't stay.
