### ch00_hook
One hundred and fifty dollars. That's what thousands of Australians paid in November 2019 to reserve a spot in the queue for the most talked-about vehicle on the planet – the Tesla Cybertruck.

They waited through a pandemic. They waited while the reservation page quietly disappeared in 2022. They watched Tesla parade a Cybertruck around Australian showrooms in 2024. They heard Tesla's local boss say he was personally confident it would come.

Then, on Friday the 23rd of October 2026 – almost seven years after they paid – the email arrived. Quote: "After careful consideration, plans to produce Cybertruck for Australia and New Zealand have been discontinued."

That's it. No Cybertruck. Not now. Not ever, according to Tesla's own plans.

So what actually happened? Why did Tesla walk away from Australia? What does the refund look like? And with the Cybertruck gone, which electric and American utes can Aussies actually buy in 2026? Stay with me, because the real reason the Cybertruck died says a lot about where the whole ute market is heading.

### ch01
To understand why this hurts, you have to go back to the start.

In November 2019, Elon Musk walked on stage in Los Angeles and revealed the Cybertruck. Stainless steel body. Armoured glass – which famously cracked on stage when a metal ball was thrown at it. And a promised starting price in the United States of just $39,900, with the top tri-motor version promising more than 500 miles – around 800 kilometres – of range.

The internet went wild. Reservations opened almost immediately, including in Australia, with a $150 refundable deposit. Tesla has said tens of thousands of people reserved between 2019 and 2021.

For Aussies, the idea made sense. We love utes. We love towing. And here was an electric pickup that promised to do it all – with a futuristic look nothing else on the road could match.

But there was a problem from day one. The Cybertruck was designed as a left-hand-drive American truck. Australia drives on the left, and our cars need the steering wheel on the right. Converting a vehicle like this isn't a quick job. It means re-engineering the dashboard, the steering, the pedals, and testing it all again.

And when the Cybertruck finally reached American customers at the end of 2023 – four years after the reveal – it was more expensive and had less range than originally promised. The dream and the reality had already started to drift apart.

### ch02
For a few years, Australian reservation holders lived on hope.

In May 2022, Tesla closed the local reservation page. It didn't refund deposits automatically. It just replaced the page with a "get updates" button. Most people simply forgot about their $150.

Then, in 2024, the hope came roaring back. Tesla Australia imported a Cybertruck and took it on a national roadshow, showing it at events and showrooms around the country. People queued to take photos. That same year, Musk told shareholders they might be able to certify the Cybertruck for other markets "sometime next year".

In March 2025, Tesla's Australia and New Zealand country director, Thom Drew, said he was "personally confident" the Cybertruck would come – possibly as early as 2026. Within about a month, his tone had changed. He said he was "not confident it's coming, but we are discussing it."

By May 2025, the local team had been briefed on the Australian Design Rule changes that would be needed – including bumper widths, external lighting, and of course right-hand drive.

And then came the silence. Until October 2026.

### ch03
So why did Tesla finally pull the plug? Tesla hasn't given a detailed public reason beyond that email. But the numbers tell the story very clearly.

Start with sales. Musk once predicted the Cybertruck could sell 250,000 a year. The reality has been very different. According to estimates reported by CarExpert, around 38,965 were sold in 2024. That fell to about 20,237 in 2025. And from January to September 2026, it was around 11,586 – down another twenty-eight per cent.

Then there's the price. The truck that was supposed to start at $39,900 now sells for around eighty thousand US dollars for the dual-motor version in America. EV Central estimated that in Australia, it would likely have cost around $130,000 before on-road costs and local taxes. That's a very hard sell.

Then there's right-hand drive. The Cybertruck is built only in left-hand drive. It isn't sold in any right-hand-drive market. Australia would likely have been the main one – other right-hand-drive markets like the UK and Japan aren't natural homes for a truck this big either. Building a right-hand-drive version just for us would mean spending a fortune for a tiny number of sales.

And finally, the size of the market. The whole full-size American pickup segment in Australia – trucks like the Ram, the Chevrolet Silverado and the Ford F-150 – sells roughly ten thousand vehicles a year. Even if Tesla grabbed a big slice of that, it would never justify the cost of re-engineering and certifying the Cybertruck for local roads.

Put all of that together, and the decision was almost made for them.

And there's one more factor: the Cybertruck's track record in America. According to the official US recall database, the Cybertruck has been recalled eleven times since it launched. In April 2024, it was recalled because the accelerator pedal pad could come loose and get trapped by the interior trim – which could cause the truck to keep accelerating. In March 2025, more than 46,000 trucks were recalled because an exterior trim panel could detach while driving. In October 2025, more than 63,000 were recalled because the front parking lights were too bright to meet US rules. And in April 2026, there was a recall over brake rotor stud holes that could crack, allowing a wheel stud to separate.

Recalls happen to every brand, and a recall is the system working. But for a low-volume truck that would have needed expensive local engineering, every extra problem made the Australian business case even weaker.

### ch04
So what do Aussie reservation holders actually get?

Tesla says reservations will be cancelled and deposits refunded in full. Customers will get a separate email to confirm their refund details, and EV Central reports the money should arrive within thirty days.

And Tesla has added a sweetener. Reservation holders who order a new Model 3 or Model Y get one year of Full Self-Driving – Supervised – for free. At the current price of $149 a month, that's worth $1,788.

Now, it's worth being clear about what that is. "Supervised" means exactly that. The driver must stay alert, keep their hands ready and remain fully responsible for the car at all times. It's a driver assistance feature, not a car that drives itself.

If you paid a deposit back in 2019 and you've changed your email address, or you can't find the message, contact Tesla directly. A $150 refund isn't life-changing, but it's your money, and after seven years you're entitled to it.

### ch06
Here's the interesting part. Tesla isn't struggling in Australia. It's the Cybertruck that didn't fit.

In September 2026, Tesla sold 5,654 cars in Australia, up more than twenty-one per cent on the same month last year. Electric vehicles made up 24.2 per cent of all new car sales that month – more than petrol cars, for the second month in a row. And with a fuel crisis pushing diesel towards three dollars a litre in some capital cities, more Aussies than ever are thinking about going electric.

You can see it in the insurance data too. NRMA Insurance says requests for electric vehicle insurance quotes were up eighty per cent in the first nine months of 2026. And NRMA's own survey found the share of Australians actively considering a battery electric car rose from twenty per cent in 2024 to thirty-one per cent in 2026. The demand for electric vehicles in Australia is real. It just isn't demand for a $130,000 stainless steel truck.

In fact, 2026 has been a record year for Tesla here. In June, Tesla sold 8,670 vehicles in Australia – the strongest month in its local history, according to Electric Vehicle Council figures. The Model Y alone sold 8,072 that month, and 20,396 in the first half of the year – up more than ninety-five per cent. Tesla's first-half total was 23,588, up almost sixty-seven per cent. A big part of that came from the new six-seat Model Y L.

It's not all good news. The Model 3 has gone backwards, down forty-seven per cent in June. And BYD's Sealion 7 has become a serious rival, with 7,786 deliveries through May. But overall, Tesla is having one of its best years ever in Australia.

So Tesla's message is clear. It would rather put its effort into the Model Y and Model 3 – cars that already sell in big numbers here – than into a niche, left-hand-drive truck that would cost a fortune to convert.

That's a business decision. But for the people who waited seven years, it's still a disappointment. And it raises a bigger question: if not the Cybertruck, then what?

### ch07
Let's look at the big American pickups first, because that's the segment the Cybertruck would have competed in.

Australia already has the Ram 1500, the Chevrolet Silverado and the Ford F-150, all converted to right-hand drive for our market. In March 2026, the F-150 sold 270, the Ram 1500 sold 181, and the Silverado 172. These are big, expensive trucks, and they sell in small numbers.

And they've had their own problems this year. In October 2026, Ford recalled the F-150 in Australia over a fire risk. CarExpert notes Ford issues more recalls here than any other car maker.

The lesson is simple. Even the established American trucks are a niche in Australia. The Cybertruck would have been a niche within a niche.

And Tesla isn't the only company that has quietly walked away from a promise in 2026. Isuzu has cancelled its plans to bring the electric D-Max to Australia. Nissan has indefinitely postponed the new-generation Leaf here, and cut the Juke and the Pathfinder from its range. Jaguar stopped selling new petrol cars in Australia on the 31st of December 2025, and still has no confirmed local launch date for its new electric models – which means it sold zero new cars here in September. And Volvo has axed the S60 sedan and the V60 Cross Country wagon from its local line-up.

In a market this crowded – with around seventy brands fighting for roughly 1.2 million sales a year – car makers are being ruthless. If a model can't sell in big enough numbers, it doesn't come, or it doesn't stay.

### ch08
Now let's talk about the vehicle Australians were really hoping the Cybertruck would be: an electric ute.

Here's the reality in 2026. Electric utes make up less than one per cent of Australian ute sales. According to CarExpert, there were 157,531 ute deliveries to September, and diesel and plug-in hybrids still dominate.

Toyota launched the HiLux BEV, the electric version of the country's most famous ute. But it sold roughly 300 between May and early July, with fewer than ten going to private buyers. KGM's Musso EV has sold just over 300 since its launch late last year. And Isuzu has cancelled its plans to bring the D-Max EV to Australia altogether. As Isuzu's Rod Caldwell put it: "Customers don't want it."

Not everyone agrees. In September 2026, MG confirmed the U9 EV, an all-electric dual-cab ute, priced from $78,990 before on-road costs. It claims 430 kilometres of range, 325 kilowatts of power, and – crucially for Aussie buyers – 3,500 kilograms of braked towing. It can charge from twenty to eighty per cent in about forty-two minutes on a fast charger, and carry a payload of 685 kilograms. Deliveries start in the last quarter of 2026. MG says customers here have been "yearning" for a vehicle like this.

And then there's the plug-in hybrid middle ground, led by the BYD Shark 6, which was the third best-selling 4x4 pick-up in Australia across the first half of 2026 – a remarkable result for a ute that only arrived at the start of 2025. GAC has confirmed its upcoming ute will be electrified only, and Chery's Stockman plug-in hybrid is due late in 2026.

So the electric ute is coming. It just doesn't look like a stainless steel spaceship.

### ch09
So what does all this mean for you?

If you had a Cybertruck reservation, check your email – including your spam folder – for Tesla's message, and follow the refund process. If you've moved or changed email address, contact Tesla directly with any details you have from 2019.

If you're thinking about a big American pickup, remember they're expensive, thirsty and sold in small numbers – so check recalls, service locations and resale values before you buy.

If you want an electric ute, think hard about how you'll use it. Range drops when you tow heavy loads. If you do long trips with a caravan, a plug-in hybrid may suit you better for now. If you mostly do short runs and charge at home, a fully electric ute could save you a lot on fuel – especially with fuel prices where they are in 2026.

And there's a bigger lesson about deposits. A reservation is not a promise. Before you put money down for a car that doesn't exist yet, ask whether the deposit is fully refundable, how you'll get it back, and whether there's any date by which the company has to deliver. Seven years is a long time to wait for a car that never arrives.

Under the Australian Consumer Law, if a business takes your money for something and then can't supply it, you're generally entitled to get that money back. And any contract term that lets a business keep your deposit unfairly can be challenged as an unfair contract term. So if a car company ever cancels a model you've paid for and doesn't offer a refund, don't just accept it. Put your request in writing, and if you get nowhere, contact your state's fair trading office or the ACCC.

And if you're one of the Australians who still had a Cybertruck reservation, here's the practical part. Keep a copy of Tesla's email. Log in to your Tesla account and check that the reservation shows as cancelled. Note the date, because Tesla says the refund should arrive within thirty days. If it doesn't, follow up in writing, keep the reply, and only then escalate. A hundred and fifty dollars might not sound like much, but it's your money, and you've already waited seven years for it.

### ch10
So, the Tesla Cybertruck is officially dead in Australia. Seven years, a $150 deposit, a national roadshow and a lot of hope – and it ends with an email and a refund.

So what are the lessons from the Cybertruck saga?

Lesson one: a reveal is not a product. A concept on a stage, a price on a slide and a reservation button don't mean a car is coming to Australia – especially if it's only built in left-hand drive.

Lesson two: right-hand drive matters. Australia is a relatively small right-hand-drive market. When a car maker has to choose where to spend engineering money, we're not always first in line.

Lesson three: the best car for you is one you can actually buy, service and resell here. Hype doesn't fix a recall, find a part or hold its value.

The truth is, the Cybertruck was always going to struggle here. Left-hand drive only, a huge price, a tiny segment and falling sales in America. Tesla made the business call. And the electric ute race in Australia will be fought by more practical machines.
