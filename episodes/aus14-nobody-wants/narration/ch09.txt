Before we move on, a quick-fire round of cars that just missed the list – because the pattern is everywhere once you start looking.

The Mercedes-Benz C-Class, once a staple of every leafy suburb: 39 registrations in September, down almost 58 per cent. The Volvo XC90 seven-seater: 33, down 62. The Mazda CX-90: 36, down a third. The Hyundai Sonata: 16. The Skoda Superb, one of the most spacious cars you can buy for the money: ten, down more than half. And the Honda Civic, a nameplate that used to be everywhere: 55, down 38 per cent.

Notice something? Most of these are sedans, big seven-seaters or European models – exactly the kinds of cars being squeezed hardest by cheaper SUVs, hybrids and the flood of new Chinese brands. The Australian market isn't shrinking. It's being reshuffled, and some very famous names are on the wrong side of that reshuffle.

Now, before you assume every big sales drop means a car is unloved, here are the cars that look like disasters in the data but tell a very different story.

The Toyota LandCruiser 70 Series. In September, its sales were down 96 per cent. Does that mean buyers have turned their backs on it? The opposite. Toyota has repeatedly paused orders because of strong demand and limited production, and new Australian regulations forced a production pause. Buyers want them; they just can't get them.

The Toyota Fortuner. Down 94 per cent. That's because Toyota confirmed in late 2025 that the Fortuner was being axed in Australia, with no replacement. Dealers are simply selling the last cars.

The GWM Ora electric hatch. One sale last month, down almost 99 per cent. It's been dropped, and GWM is replacing it with the new Ora 5 SUV.

And the Volkswagen T-Roc. Down 84 per cent – but a new-generation model is due here in 2027, so buyers are holding off.

The lesson? A sales collapse can mean a car is unloved, or it can mean a car is being replaced or simply can't be built fast enough. Before you buy a heavily discounted car, find out which one it is.
