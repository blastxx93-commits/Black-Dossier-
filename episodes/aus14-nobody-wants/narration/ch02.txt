Number twelve: the Volkswagen ID.4.

Volkswagen's electric SUV found just 47 buyers in September, down 73 per cent on the same month last year. Its little brother, the T-Cross, managed 29, down 81 per cent.

And Volkswagen knows it has a problem. In May 2026, the director of Volkswagen Passenger Vehicles Australia, Piergiorgio Minto, warned about what happens when sales keep falling. In his words: "Whenever you start selling less than 10,000 vehicles a year… you're niche." Volkswagen sold roughly 29,000 vehicles here in 2025, down more than 20 per cent. Keep falling at that rate, and the brand that gave us the Golf could become a niche player within five years.

Volkswagen's product boss, Arjun Nidigallu, was just as candid. He admitted that "having that something for everything hasn't been the case for a while." The ID.4 is a good example. It reached Australian showrooms almost four years after its global debut. By then, cheaper electric SUVs from China had already arrived, with more range, more screens and lower prices.

Volkswagen has been cutting prices on its electric range for months, especially on the ID. Buzz van. But when the competition is this fierce, a discount alone isn't enough.

Number eleven: the Omoda 9.

This is the one that proves Chinese brands aren't immune. The Omoda 9 is Omoda Jaecoo's flagship – a big plug-in hybrid SUV with nearly 400 kilowatts, sold in a single trim at $61,990 before on-road costs. Last month it found 35 buyers, down 61.5 per cent. Its own sister models are flying, but at sixty-two thousand dollars, buyers are asking a tougher question: would I rather have a Toyota, a Kia or a BYD for that money? Being Chinese is no longer enough on its own. The price still has to make sense.
