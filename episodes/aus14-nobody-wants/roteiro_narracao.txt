### ch01
First, the big picture, because it explains everything that follows.

Australians bought 108,778 new vehicles in September 2026, according to the official VFACTS figures. That's up 1.8 per cent on the same month a year earlier. So this isn't a market where nobody is buying cars. It's a market where buyers are moving – fast.

Electric cars outsold petrol cars for the month, with 26,287 sales, up 117 per cent. Plug-in hybrid sales more than doubled. Geely's sales jumped 870 per cent. BYD finished second overall, behind only Toyota.

And look at which models are winning. The Ford Ranger was the country's best seller with 5,062. Then the Toyota RAV4, the Tesla Model Y and the Toyota HiLux. But right behind them: the BYD Sealion 7 with 2,687, the Chery Tiggo 4 with 2,059, the GWM Haval Jolion with 2,018 and the Geely EX5 with 2,008 – up 620 per cent. Four Chinese models inside the national top ten.

Now look at who's going backwards. Subaru, down 42.6 per cent. Mitsubishi, down 41.5. Volkswagen, down almost 37. Nissan, down 30. Hyundai, down 22. Kia and Mazda, close to 20 each.

When money moves this quickly, some cars get left behind. And a car that stops selling doesn't just disappear. It usually sits in a dealer's yard, often paid for with borrowed money, losing value every single week. That's when the discounts start.

A couple of ground rules before the list. All the monthly figures come from VFACTS data published through CarExpert's new-car sales database. I've only included cars that are still on sale. Models that have already been axed, I'll deal with separately, because a car that's being run out is a very different story from a car that buyers are ignoring.

### ch02
Number twelve: the Volkswagen ID.4.

Volkswagen's electric SUV found just 47 buyers in September, down 73 per cent on the same month last year. Its little brother, the T-Cross, managed 29, down 81 per cent.

And Volkswagen knows it has a problem. In May 2026, the director of Volkswagen Passenger Vehicles Australia, Piergiorgio Minto, warned about what happens when sales keep falling. In his words: "Whenever you start selling less than 10,000 vehicles a year… you're niche." Volkswagen sold roughly 29,000 vehicles here in 2025, down more than 20 per cent. Keep falling at that rate, and the brand that gave us the Golf could become a niche player within five years.

Volkswagen's product boss, Arjun Nidigallu, was just as candid. He admitted that "having that something for everything hasn't been the case for a while." The ID.4 is a good example. It reached Australian showrooms almost four years after its global debut. By then, cheaper electric SUVs from China had already arrived, with more range, more screens and lower prices.

Volkswagen has been cutting prices on its electric range for months, especially on the ID. Buzz van. But when the competition is this fierce, a discount alone isn't enough.

Number eleven: the Omoda 9.

This is the one that proves Chinese brands aren't immune. The Omoda 9 is Omoda Jaecoo's flagship – a big plug-in hybrid SUV with nearly 400 kilowatts, sold in a single trim at $61,990 before on-road costs. Last month it found 35 buyers, down 61.5 per cent. Its own sister models are flying, but at sixty-two thousand dollars, buyers are asking a tougher question: would I rather have a Toyota, a Kia or a BYD for that money? Being Chinese is no longer enough on its own. The price still has to make sense.

### ch03
Number ten: the Renault Koleos.

The Koleos is a mid-size family SUV, the kind of car Australians buy by the tens of thousands. Toyota sold more than 4,600 RAV4s in September alone. Renault found homes for eighteen Koleos. That's down 70.5 per cent.

And it's not because it's expensive. The Koleos has been advertised from $36,990 drive-away – cheap for a mid-size SUV in 2026. Even at that price, buyers are walking past it. This generation has been around for years, and back in 2025 Renault Australia's Glen Sealey said a "different vehicle" may replace it, "maybe in 2026". Nothing has been confirmed, and the market has clearly moved on.

That's the first big lesson of this list. A low price can't save a car that feels old. Buyers in 2026 are walking into showrooms expecting hybrid or electric options, big screens and the latest safety tech. If a car doesn't tick those boxes, they'll pay a few thousand more for something newer.

Number nine: the Mazda CX-70.

Mazda's big two-row SUV was supposed to be a premium flagship. Instead, it's become one of the slowest-selling cars in its class. Sixteen sales in September. Just 182 for the whole year so far. To put that in perspective, Mazda sold more than 3,500 of the smaller CX-60 over the same period.

In July 2026, Mazda slashed CX-70 prices by between $3,700 and $8,051 depending on the variant. It didn't fix the problem. And in late October, Mazda confirmed the CX-70 is ending production for the United States, its biggest market. Mazda Australia says the car will continue here, and that it "still has a small but loyal fanbase". But unnamed sources told CarsGuide it will leave the local market soon. Mazda's own global report has called Australia a "tough environment".

### ch04
Number eight: Genesis.

Hyundai's luxury brand has some genuinely impressive cars, but the numbers are brutal. Genesis sold just 66 cars in total across Australia last month – down 54.5 per cent. The GV80 wagon moved two. The GV70, its best seller, managed 46.

The discounts tell the story. In July, Genesis extended end-of-financial-year deals with up to $7,000 off the GV70, and up to $10,000 off the GV80. That's serious money to take off a luxury SUV – and it still isn't moving them in big numbers. Luxury buyers are notoriously loyal to badges they already know, like Mercedes-Benz, BMW and Lexus. Breaking into that club takes years, and Genesis is learning that the hard way.

Number seven: the BMW iX.

BMW's large electric SUV used to be a status symbol. Last month, it found only seven buyers, down almost 88 per cent. Part of the reason is BMW itself. Its new iX3 arrived this year with a much sharper price, and in April BMW cut up to $8,900 from the smaller iX1 and iX2. When a brand's own newer, cheaper electric SUVs look like better value, the old flagship gets squeezed.

That's the second big lesson. Sometimes a car's worst enemy isn't a rival brand. It's the next model in its own showroom.

Number six: the Polestar 3.

This is a beautifully built, fast electric SUV with a huge price tag. The rear-motor version starts at $116,700 before on-road costs, and the dual-motor at $131,100. In September, Polestar sold six of them in Australia, down 88.5 per cent.

Even Polestar's global boss admits the brand got its pricing wrong. In May 2026, chief executive Michael Lohscheller told CarsGuide that when Polestar was set up, it was "maybe a bit too high and too niche and maybe also a bit too expensive." He also admitted the company is still "in a loss-making situation." That's an extraordinary thing for the head of a car company to say out loud.

Number five: the Jeep Wrangler and Gladiator.

These are icons – the original off-roaders. But last month, the Wrangler sold nine and the Gladiator ute sold five. The Wrangler was down 77 per cent and the Gladiator 80 per cent. The whole Jeep brand sold sixteen cars for the month.

Price is a big part of it. CarSauce reported that the cheapest four-door Wrangler went from around $37,000 in 2014 to $75,950 before on-road costs. At that price, buyers are comparing it with a LandCruiser Prado, a Ford Everest, or a Chinese 4x4 with a lot more equipment. Jeep says it's committed to Australia. But right now, the showroom traffic isn't there.

### ch07
Number four: the Alfa Romeo Tonale and Stelvio.

Alfa Romeo has one of the most passionate fan bases in the car world. But passion doesn't pay the bills. In September, the Tonale small SUV registered two. The bigger Stelvio also registered two. The updated Tonale starts at $64,990 before on-road costs – for a small SUV. Alfa has run deals with more than ten thousand dollars off in the past, and the brand as a whole sold just 21 cars that month.

Think about that for a moment. Two Tonales in a month, while a single Chinese small SUV, the Chery Tiggo 4, sold more than two thousand. Brand heritage counts for a lot less than it used to.

Number three: the Fiat 500.

The cute little Fiat 500 was once a common sight in every city. In September 2026, the Fiat 500 and Abarth line found two buyers, down almost 95 per cent. Fiat already tried the price-cut route with the electric 500e. In 2025, it dropped the 500e La Prima from $52,500 before on-road costs to $38,990 drive-away. Fiat said the deal was to "stimulate sales and clear out existing stock." When a brand says the words "clear out existing stock" out loud, you know things are tough.

### ch08
Number two: the Peugeot 308.

This one is almost hard to believe. The Peugeot 308 is a stylish, well-reviewed small car, sold as a petrol hatch and as an electric e-308. In September, it found two buyers. For the whole of 2026 so far, the tally is seventeen. Seventeen cars in nine months, across the entire country.

And the 308's problem goes beyond the car. In July 2026, Peugeot's importer, Inchcape, agreed to end its partnership with Stellantis in Australia. Stellantis says Peugeot "has a strong future in Australia". But if you're a buyer, uncertainty like that makes you nervous – and nervous buyers don't sign contracts.

And number one: the Hyundai Ioniq 9.

The car we started with. One sale in September. Sixty-five for the whole of 2026 so far, and only forty in 2025, its launch year. This is a seven-seat electric SUV from one of the biggest brands in the country – a brand that sold more than five thousand cars last month alone.

So what went wrong? Price is part of it – the top-spec Calligraphy is listed at around $128,000 drive-away in Sydney. Competition is part of it too, with cheaper Chinese seven-seaters arriving. But Hyundai's own boss has been brutally honest. At the Ioniq 9's launch in August 2025, Hyundai Australia's new chief executive, Don Romano, said: "We do a terrible job with our EVs." He said Hyundai's electric market share was "extremely low relative to our market share of total vehicles." More than a year later, the sales numbers show how big that job still is.

### ch09
Before we move on, a quick-fire round of cars that just missed the list – because the pattern is everywhere once you start looking.

The Mercedes-Benz C-Class, once a staple of every leafy suburb: 39 registrations in September, down almost 58 per cent. The Volvo XC90 seven-seater: 33, down 62. The Mazda CX-90: 36, down a third. The Hyundai Sonata: 16. The Skoda Superb, one of the most spacious cars you can buy for the money: ten, down more than half. And the Honda Civic, a nameplate that used to be everywhere: 55, down 38 per cent.

Notice something? Most of these are sedans, big seven-seaters or European models – exactly the kinds of cars being squeezed hardest by cheaper SUVs, hybrids and the flood of new Chinese brands. The Australian market isn't shrinking. It's being reshuffled, and some very famous names are on the wrong side of that reshuffle.

Now, before you assume every big sales drop means a car is unloved, here are the cars that look like disasters in the data but tell a very different story.

The Toyota LandCruiser 70 Series. In September, its sales were down 96 per cent. Does that mean buyers have turned their backs on it? The opposite. Toyota has repeatedly paused orders because of strong demand and limited production, and new Australian regulations forced a production pause. Buyers want them; they just can't get them.

The Toyota Fortuner. Down 94 per cent. That's because Toyota confirmed in late 2025 that the Fortuner was being axed in Australia, with no replacement. Dealers are simply selling the last cars.

The GWM Ora electric hatch. One sale last month, down almost 99 per cent. It's been dropped, and GWM is replacing it with the new Ora 5 SUV.

And the Volkswagen T-Roc. Down 84 per cent – but a new-generation model is due here in 2027, so buyers are holding off.

The lesson? A sales collapse can mean a car is unloved, or it can mean a car is being replaced or simply can't be built fast enough. Before you buy a heavily discounted car, find out which one it is.

### ch10
So what does all this mean for you?

If you're buying, cars that aren't selling are where the biggest deals live. We've seen Mazda cut up to $8,051, Genesis offer up to $10,000 off, BMW trim up to $8,900, and Fiat drop the electric 500e to under thirty-nine thousand dollars drive-away to clear stock. If one of these cars genuinely suits you, ask for a drive-away price in writing, ask how long the car has been in stock, and ask whether a newer model is coming soon. Old stock is often where the dealer has the most room to move. And the end of the year, when dealers chase their annual targets, can be one of the best times to ask.

It's also worth asking about demonstrators. When a model is slow-moving, dealers often have demo cars with a few thousand kilometres on them, still covered by most of the new-car warranty. On a car that's already heavily discounted, a demo can take off another chunk. Just check the build date, the warranty start date and the service history, and make sure the price reflects any wear and tear.

And always compare. If a slow seller is ten thousand dollars off, line it up against the best-selling alternatives in the same class, with their deals included. Sometimes the discounted car is a genuine bargain. Sometimes the popular car, at full price, is still better value once you add in resale.

But be careful. A car that's hard to sell new is usually hard to sell used, too. Expect faster depreciation, and plan to keep it longer. Check the warranty, the service plan, and how far you'll have to travel for servicing and parts – especially for brands with small dealer networks or uncertain futures.

And if you already own one of these cars, don't panic. Low new-car sales don't change your warranty or your consumer rights. Keep your service history up to date, and if you're thinking of selling, get a few valuations before you trade it in.
