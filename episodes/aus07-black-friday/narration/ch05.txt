Now, the traps. And trap number one is the oldest trick in retail: the fake "was" price.

The ACCC watches Black Friday closely. After the 2024 sales, it issued infringement notices to Michael Hill, MyHouse and Hairhouse Online, totalling $59,400, over "sitewide" sale ads that the ACCC alleged excluded some products. Those weren't car companies, but the lesson is the same. A big "save" number only means something if the "before" price was real.

With cars, the trick usually looks different. A dealer might compare the drive-away price with the manufacturer's list price before on-road costs. Or compare it with a price nobody has actually paid for months. So before Black Friday, write down the drive-away price of the car you want today. Then compare it with the Black Friday price. That's your real saving.

And here's something a lot of buyers don't realise. There's a big difference between a "drive-away" price and a price "before on-road costs". On-road costs include things like stamp duty, registration, compulsory third party insurance and dealer charges. Depending on the car and the state you live in, they can add thousands of dollars. So a deal advertised before on-road costs can look much cheaper than a drive-away deal that's actually better. Always compare drive-away with drive-away.

And even an advertised drive-away price isn't set in stone. You can still ask for a breakdown of it, and you can still negotiate. A Black Friday price is the dealer's opening offer, not the final word.

Trap number two: the demo or runout car dressed up as a sale. When Savvy reviewed last year's Black Friday car deals, its managing editor Thomas Perrotta warned that "some manufacturers may just be selling demo, runout or plate clearance models." A demo can be a great buy – but only if it's priced like a used car, because it's already been registered and driven. Always ask how many kilometres it has, when it was first registered, and when the warranty started.
