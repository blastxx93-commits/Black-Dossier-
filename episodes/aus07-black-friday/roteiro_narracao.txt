### ch02
So why could 2026 be even bigger?

Reason one: competition. Australia now has around seventy car brands fighting for about 1.2 million sales a year. New Chinese brands keep arriving, and they're using price as their main weapon. In September 2026, BYD sold 8,191 cars and was the second best-selling brand in the country. GWM is pushing towards the top five. To keep up, everyone else has to sharpen their pencil.

Reason two: the government's New Vehicle Efficiency Standard. Since July 2025, car makers whose fleets produce too much carbon dioxide face penalties. In the first six months alone, Mazda's liability was estimated at around $25.4 million, Nissan's at around $10.7 million, Subaru's at almost $7 million and Hyundai's at about $4.2 million. Meanwhile, BYD, Toyota and Tesla built up millions of credits. And in 2026, the targets get tighter. That gives brands a big reason to push electric and hybrid cars out of the door – and discounting is the fastest way to do it. Hyundai, for example, cut up to $13,857 from some versions of the Kona Electric earlier this year. And the government's Clean Energy Finance Corporation committed sixty million dollars to Hyundai Capital Australia, to support discounted interest rates on Hyundai and Kia electric cars.

At the same time, the cheap end of the EV market has exploded. In February 2026, CarsGuide listed seven electric cars available for under forty thousand dollars drive-away, including the BYD Dolphin, the BYD Atto 2, the MG4, the GWM Ora, the Chery E5, the Leapmotor B10 and the Hyundai Inster. A few years ago, that list would have been almost empty.

Reason three: fuel. With petrol and diesel prices high in 2026, more buyers are looking at hybrids and EVs, and brands know it. That's why you're now seeing fuel cards offered as a sweetener on petrol cars too. In 2025, battery electric cars made up about 8.3 per cent of new car sales. By September 2026, it was more than twenty-four per cent in a single month. That's a massive shift in a very short time.

And reason four: the calendar. Black Friday is the last big sales event before the end of the year. And on the 1st of January, every unsold 2026-built car becomes, in the eyes of buyers, a year older. Dealers want those cars gone before that happens.

### ch05
Now, the traps. And trap number one is the oldest trick in retail: the fake "was" price.

The ACCC watches Black Friday closely. After the 2024 sales, it issued infringement notices to Michael Hill, MyHouse and Hairhouse Online, totalling $59,400, over "sitewide" sale ads that the ACCC alleged excluded some products. Those weren't car companies, but the lesson is the same. A big "save" number only means something if the "before" price was real.

With cars, the trick usually looks different. A dealer might compare the drive-away price with the manufacturer's list price before on-road costs. Or compare it with a price nobody has actually paid for months. So before Black Friday, write down the drive-away price of the car you want today. Then compare it with the Black Friday price. That's your real saving.

And here's something a lot of buyers don't realise. There's a big difference between a "drive-away" price and a price "before on-road costs". On-road costs include things like stamp duty, registration, compulsory third party insurance and dealer charges. Depending on the car and the state you live in, they can add thousands of dollars. So a deal advertised before on-road costs can look much cheaper than a drive-away deal that's actually better. Always compare drive-away with drive-away.

And even an advertised drive-away price isn't set in stone. You can still ask for a breakdown of it, and you can still negotiate. A Black Friday price is the dealer's opening offer, not the final word.

Trap number two: the demo or runout car dressed up as a sale. When Savvy reviewed last year's Black Friday car deals, its managing editor Thomas Perrotta warned that "some manufacturers may just be selling demo, runout or plate clearance models." A demo can be a great buy – but only if it's priced like a used car, because it's already been registered and driven. Always ask how many kilometres it has, when it was first registered, and when the warranty started.

### ch06
Trap number three is the big one: finance.

You'll see headlines like "finance from 1.99 per cent". Savvy warned that these low-rate deals often come with short terms of thirty-six to forty-eight months, deposits of up to thirty-five per cent, or a large balloon payment at the end. On a fifty-thousand-dollar car, Savvy estimated repayments could be more than $490 a week, or about $2,125 a month. As Perrotta put it: "The devil is in the details."

And there's another catch. If you apply for dealer finance and don't qualify for the advertised rate, you might be offered a standard five-year loan at a much higher rate – Savvy said often at or above ten per cent – after a credit enquiry has already been made on your file.

To see why this matters, let's do some simple maths. Borrow forty thousand dollars over five years at seven per cent, and you'll pay about $792 a month, or around $47,500 in total. Borrow the same amount at ten per cent, and it's about $850 a month – roughly $51,000 in total. That's around three and a half thousand dollars more, which can easily wipe out a Black Friday discount.

So here's what to do. Get pre-approved for a car loan from your bank or a broker before Black Friday. Then you can compare the dealer's offer properly. Always compare the comparison rate, not just the headline rate. Ask about the balloon. And work out the total amount you'll pay over the life of the loan – not just the weekly repayment.

Savvy also warned about one more thing that catches people out: using buy now, pay later on Black Friday shopping. Lenders can see it, and it can hurt your car loan application. So if you're planning to buy a car, go easy on the other Black Friday sales.

### ch07
Trap number four: the sweetener that's worth less than it looks.

Cashback, gift cards, fuel cards, free servicing and free accessories all sound great. And sometimes they are. Three years of free servicing on a Jaecoo J7, for example, was worth around nine hundred dollars last year. But a fuel card or a gift card is not the same as money off the price. In 2024, BYD's Black Friday deal included a free wall charger valued at a thousand dollars – but installation wasn't included, and installing a charger can cost hundreds more. And some of these extras come with conditions – a certain finance company, a certain delivery date, or no other offers allowed on top.

A simple rule: always ask the dealer what the drive-away price would be without the extras. Sometimes you'll get a bigger saving by negotiating the price down instead.

Trap number five: the delivery deadline. Many Black Friday car offers say the car must be delivered by a certain date – often the 30th of November or the 31st of December. If the car you want isn't in stock and has to be shipped, ask what happens if it arrives late. Do you still get the deal? Get the answer in writing on your contract, not just a verbal promise from the salesperson.

And be careful with deposits. If you pay a deposit to lock in a Black Friday price, ask whether it's refundable, and under what conditions. As we saw with the Cybertruck, a deposit is only as good as the paperwork behind it.

Trap number six: deals that are tied to someone else. Last year, some of the best-looking offers – like three thousand dollars cashback on BYD models through Savvy – were only available if you took out a car loan through that particular broker. That can still be a good deal. Just make sure the loan itself is competitive, because a cheap car with an expensive loan isn't cheap.

### ch08
Now, if you're thinking about an electric car, there's one more date you need to know – and it has nothing to do with Black Friday.

Right now, electric cars bought through a novated lease can be exempt from fringe benefits tax, as long as they're under the luxury car tax threshold for fuel-efficient vehicles, currently $91,387. That's called the Electric Car Discount, and it can save some buyers thousands of dollars a year.

But in May 2026, the government confirmed it's being wound back. The full exemption continues until the 31st of March 2027. From the 1st of April 2027, only EVs costing $75,000 or less keep the full exemption, while more expensive EVs get a twenty-five per cent discount instead. Existing leases are protected. And remember, plug-in hybrids already lost the exemption on the 1st of April 2025.

So if you're looking at an EV over $75,000 on a novated lease, the timing matters more than any Black Friday sale. Talk to your employer and your lease provider about the dates before you sign.

### ch09
So how do you actually get a good deal this Black Friday? Here's my checklist.

Step one: decide on the car before the sales start. Know the model, the variant and the colours you'd accept. Black Friday is designed to make you buy on impulse. Don't let it.

Step two: record today's drive-away price from at least three dealers, in writing. That's your benchmark. And ask each one for the breakdown, so you can see what's the car and what's on-road costs.

Step three: get finance pre-approved, and compare comparison rates, not headline rates.

Step four: when the deal drops, read the fine print. Is it for new cars only, or demos too? Does it end on a specific date? Does the car have to be delivered by the 30th of November, or the 31st of December? Is it limited to stock on hand?

Step five: ask for the build date. As we saw with BYD earlier this year, a car's build date affects its resale value. A car built in 2026 and sold in December will be a year older on paper just weeks later. That's fine if the discount reflects it.

Step six: keep the trade-in separate. Get a written price for the new car first, then talk about your trade-in. Otherwise, a big discount on one side can quietly disappear on the other.

Step seven: check the boring stuff. How much will servicing cost? How long is the warranty? Is there a recall on the model? You can check recalls on the Product Safety Australia website.

And remember, a sale price doesn't reduce your rights. Under the Australian Consumer Law, a car bought on Black Friday has exactly the same consumer guarantees as one bought at full price. If it turns out to have a major fault, the discount doesn't change your right to a repair, a replacement or a refund. We covered how that works in our video on faulty new cars.

And step eight: be ready to walk away. Black Friday ends, but car deals don't. The end of December is often another big push, as dealers chase year-end targets. If the deal isn't right, there'll be another one.

### ch10
So which brands are most likely to discount? Nobody can say for sure until the deals are announced. But it's worth watching the brands under the most pressure. The ones with emissions penalties to manage, like Mazda, Nissan, Subaru and Hyundai. The Chinese brands fighting for market share, like GWM, Chery, Geely and Omoda Jaecoo. And any brand with a new model about to arrive, because the outgoing model has to be cleared. If you're flexible on the brand, that's where the biggest savings are likely to be.

So, are Black Friday car deals worth it in Australia in 2026? They can be. The market is crowded, Chinese brands are cutting prices, emissions rules are pushing EVs and hybrids, and dealers want 2026-built stock gone before January. That's a great setup for buyers.

But a Black Friday banner doesn't make a deal good. The real saving is the difference between the price you could get today and the price you pay on the day – including the finance.
