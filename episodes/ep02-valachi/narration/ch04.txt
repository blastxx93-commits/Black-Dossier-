CHAPTER FOUR. The kiss.

In 1959, Valachi was convicted on federal narcotics charges and sentenced to fifteen years.

That same year, the man at the top of his family was convicted too. Vito Genovese, the boss at the center of our first file, received fifteen years in a federal narcotics case.

They ended up in the same place: the federal penitentiary in Atlanta, Georgia.

It is hard to imagine a worse place for a nervous man. The Atlanta penitentiary held hundreds of inmates, and among them were men who owed favors to the Genovese family, and men who would do anything to earn one.

For a boss like Genovese, prison was not the end of power. Messages went in and out with visitors and lawyers. Orders could still be given. And inside the walls, he had people around him.

And Genovese, according to Valachi, had come to suspect that Valachi was talking to the government.

Valachi later described a conversation in his cell. Genovese spoke about a rotten apple that has to be removed from the barrel. And then, Genovese kissed him on the cheek.

Valachi understood it as a kiss of death.

He became afraid of the prison food. He stayed close to the walls. He watched every face in the yard. He became convinced that a particular inmate, a mobster named Joseph DiPalermo, had been sent to kill him.

Outside, there were reasons to be afraid. That April, Valachi's longtime boss, Tony Bender, had walked out of his home in New Jersey and was never seen again. In that world, everyone knew what that meant.

If Tony Bender could disappear, so could a soldier in a prison yard.

On a day in June 1962, near a construction area in the prison yard, Valachi picked up a length of iron pipe.

He attacked a man from behind.

The man was not Joseph DiPalermo. He was John Saupp, an inmate with no connection to the Mafia at all. Saupp died a few days later.

Joe Valachi had killed the wrong man.

Now he was facing a murder charge, a death sentence from his own family, and the rest of his life behind bars.

He had one move left.
