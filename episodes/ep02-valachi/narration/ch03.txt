CHAPTER THREE. Thirty years a soldier.

For the next three decades, Joe Valachi was not a boss. He was not a star. He was a soldier.

He worked in a crew run by Anthony Strollo, known as Tony Bender. He ran slot machines and the numbers, an illegal neighborhood lottery that brought in steady cash a few cents at a time. He owned pieces of small businesses. He loaned money. At times, he dealt in narcotics.

And when the family asked, he did other things. Investigators would later suspect him of involvement in many killings over the years. Years later, before the Senate, he summed up what he did for the organization in a few cold words:

I just go out and kill for them.

That is important. The man about to expose the Mafia was not a hero. He was not a victim who stumbled into the wrong crowd. He was one of them.

But he was also something the bosses had never worried about: a low-ranking man who had been in the room for almost everything. The war. The initiation. The rise of Luciano. The quiet takeovers of rackets across New York.

He had watched the organization grow for thirty years. And he remembered.

One case from those years shows exactly how close he stood to the top.

In 1953, according to later accounts, Vito Genovese wanted a man named Steven Franse dead. Franse had been close to Genovese's wife, Anna, who had left Genovese and taken him to court, airing details of his business in public. Genovese blamed Franse.

On June 18th, 1953, Valachi invited Franse to a restaurant he owned in the Bronx. Two other men were waiting inside. Franse never left.

Valachi would later describe the murder himself. It was the kind of work a loyal soldier did without asking questions.

And it was exactly the kind of secret that could get a soldier killed, once his boss stopped trusting him.
