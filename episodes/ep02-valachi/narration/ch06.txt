CHAPTER SIX. Live from the Senate.

On September 25th, 1963, the Senate's Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, led by Senator John McClellan, opened its hearings on organized crime.

The first witness was not Valachi. It was the attorney general, Robert Kennedy.

McClellan set the tone in his opening statement. The existence of such a criminal organization as Cosa Nostra, he said, is frightening.

Two days later, Valachi arrived.

He sat at the witness table in a plain suit, wearing glasses, speaking in the rough New York accent of the streets he grew up on. Over several days of testimony, he walked the senators, and the country, through the organization from the inside.

There were families. Each family had a boss. Below the boss were lieutenants, the caporegimes. Below them, the soldiers. Above all the families sat a commission of bosses who settled disputes.

Large charts were placed in the room, showing the families of New York and the men in them. The chart for the Genovese family alone listed more than a hundred names.

He described the initiation ceremony. He described the code of silence. He described murders, and the reasons for them.

He named names. Bosses, lieutenants, soldiers. Men who were still running businesses in New York, still eating in the same restaurants, still living in the same neighborhoods. Some of them were watching on television.

He also claimed that Genovese, from prison, had ordered the deaths of other inmates who were expected to testify against him.

Not every answer was clean. Valachi mixed up dates. He told some stories differently from one day to the next. He sometimes spoke about events he had only heard about. Senators pressed him, and sometimes he grew irritated.

But the core of what he said held together. And no one in that room had ever heard anything like it from the inside.

At one point, Senator McClellan asked him why he had never simply walked away.

Valachi's answer became one of the most quoted lines of the hearings.

Once you are in, you can't get out. They will hunt you.

Across the country, families watched on black-and-white television sets as a gangster calmly described a world most people had been told did not exist.

The cameras did what Apalachin had started six years earlier. The Mafia was no longer a rumor, or a headline.

It now had a face. And a voice.
