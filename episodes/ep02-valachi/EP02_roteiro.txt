September 27th, 1963.

Washington, D.C. A government car pulls up to the United States Capitol. Around it, roughly two hundred federal marshals.

They are not guarding a president. They are not guarding a king.

They are guarding a career criminal pushing sixty, a man with a gravel voice and a prison haircut. A man who, a year earlier, had killed a stranger in a prison yard because he was sure the stranger had come to kill him.

Inside a Senate hearing room, television cameras are waiting. Millions of Americans are about to hear something no member of the Mafia had ever said out loud, in public, under oath.

That the organization was real. That it had a name. That it had ranks, rules and rituals. And that once you were in, you could never get out.

His bosses had put a price on his head. One hundred thousand dollars, he said.

He talked anyway.

This is Black Dossier. And this... is the Valachi file.

---

CHAPTER ONE. A boy from East Harlem.

Joseph Valachi was born in 1904, in East Harlem, New York. His parents were poor immigrants from the Campania region of southern Italy. Years later, he would describe a hard childhood, and a violent, hard-drinking father.

He did not start in the Mafia. He started small.

As a young man he drove for a gang of burglars called the Minutemen. Their method was simple: smash a store window, grab what they could, and disappear. Valachi was the getaway driver.

He was arrested more than once. In 1923, after a robbery went wrong, he was sent to Sing Sing prison.

Sing Sing sat on the banks of the Hudson River, north of the city. It was one of the most notorious prisons in America. For a young street thief from Harlem, it was also a school. Valachi served only part of his sentence, about nine months, before he was released.

He came out with a record, a reputation, and a growing list of people who knew his name.

Prison did not change his direction. It sharpened it.

Inside, and back out on the streets of East Harlem, Valachi learned who really ran things. Not the street gangs. Not the burglars. Above them, there were older men. Quieter men. Men who did not need to break windows, because other people paid them.

In New York at the end of the 1920s, those men were about to go to war with each other.

And Joe Valachi was about to pick a side.

---

CHAPTER TWO. The war of 1930.

By 1930, the Italian underworld of New York was divided between two powerful bosses.

On one side, Joe Masseria. Old-fashioned, heavy-handed, and used to being obeyed.

On the other, Salvatore Maranzano. A newcomer from Castellammare del Golfo, in Sicily. The same coastal town that produced so many of the men in our first file.

Historians call what followed the Castellammarese War. For more than a year, men from both sides were ambushed in restaurants, in garages, on the street.

Valachi did not start on Maranzano's side by accident. His first connection was to the crew of Gaetano Reina, a boss who controlled ice distribution in the Bronx, among other rackets. When Reina was shot dead in early 1930, his men were pushed into the war. Two years later, Valachi would marry Reina's eldest daughter, Carmela.

Loyalty in that world was personal. It was also temporary.

Valachi fought on Maranzano's side.

And that same year, 1930, he was brought to a private house, for a ceremony he would describe, more than three decades later, in remarkable detail.

He said around forty men were there. On a table in front of him lay a gun, a thirty-eight, and a knife. Maranzano presided. Another man pricked Valachi's finger with a pin and squeezed until the blood came out.

The man holding the pin was Joe Bonanno. Remember that name.

Then Maranzano spoke. According to Valachi, he said the blood meant they were now one family. And he warned them: you live by the gun and the knife, and you die by the gun and the knife.

Valachi was now a made man.

The war ended in April 1931, when Joe Masseria was shot to death in a restaurant in Coney Island. Maranzano declared himself the boss of all bosses. Valachi became one of his bodyguards.

It lasted about five months.

In September 1931, Maranzano was murdered in his own office. The men behind it, by most accounts, answered to an ambitious young gangster named Charles Luciano. Lucky Luciano.

Valachi switched sides, as many did. He was now a soldier in the family that would one day carry the name of a man he would come to fear more than anyone alive.

Vito Genovese.

---

CHAPTER THREE. Thirty years a soldier.

For the next three decades, Joe Valachi was not a boss. He was not a star. He was a soldier.

He worked in a crew run by Anthony Strollo, known as Tony Bender. He ran slot machines and the numbers, an illegal neighborhood lottery that brought in steady cash a few cents at a time. He owned pieces of small businesses. He loaned money. At times, he dealt in narcotics.

And when the family asked, he did other things. Investigators would later suspect him of involvement in many killings over the years. Years later, before the Senate, he summed up what he did for the organization in a few cold words:

I just go out and kill for them.

That is important. The man about to expose the Mafia was not a hero. He was not a victim who stumbled into the wrong crowd. He was one of them.

But he was also something the bosses had never worried about: a low-ranking man who had been in the room for almost everything. The war. The initiation. The rise of Luciano. The quiet takeovers of rackets across New York.

He had watched the organization grow for thirty years.

One case from those years shows exactly how close he stood to the top.

In 1953, according to later accounts, Vito Genovese wanted a man named Steven Franse dead. Franse had been close to Genovese's wife, Anna, who had left Genovese and taken him to court, airing details of his business in public. Genovese blamed Franse.

On June 18th, 1953, Valachi invited Franse to a restaurant he owned in the Bronx. Two other men were waiting inside. Franse never left.

Valachi would later describe the murder himself. It was the kind of work a loyal soldier did without asking questions.

And it was exactly the kind of secret that could get a soldier killed, once his boss stopped trusting him.

And he remembered.

---

CHAPTER FOUR. The kiss.

In 1959, Valachi was convicted on federal narcotics charges and sentenced to fifteen years.

That same year, the man at the top of his family was convicted too. Vito Genovese, the boss at the center of our first file, received fifteen years in a federal narcotics case.

They ended up in the same place: the federal penitentiary in Atlanta, Georgia.

It is hard to imagine a worse place for a nervous man. The Atlanta penitentiary held hundreds of inmates, and among them were men who owed favors to the Genovese family, and men who would do anything to earn one.

For a boss like Genovese, prison was not the end of power. Messages went in and out with visitors and lawyers. Orders could still be given. And inside the walls, he had people around him.

Inside, Genovese still had power. And Genovese, according to Valachi, had come to suspect that Valachi was talking to the government.

Valachi later described a conversation in his cell. Genovese spoke about a rotten apple that has to be removed from the barrel. And then, Genovese kissed him on the cheek.

Valachi understood it as a kiss of death.

He became afraid of the prison food. He stayed close to the walls. He watched every face in the yard. He became convinced that a particular inmate, a mobster named Joseph DiPalermo, had been sent to kill him.

Outside, there were reasons to be afraid. That April, Valachi's longtime boss, Tony Bender, had walked out of his home in New Jersey and was never seen again. In that world, everyone knew what that meant.

If Tony Bender could disappear, so could a soldier in a prison yard.

On a day in June 1962, near a construction area in the prison yard, Valachi picked up a length of iron pipe.

He attacked a man from behind.

The man was not Joseph DiPalermo. He was John Saupp, an inmate with no connection to the Mafia at all. Saupp died a few days later.

Joe Valachi had killed the wrong man.

Now he was facing a murder charge, a death sentence from his own family, and the rest of his life behind bars.

He had one move left.

---

CHAPTER FIVE. The deal.

Valachi reached out to the federal government.

Over the following months, he was moved, isolated, and questioned at length by federal agents. He was held under heavy guard, because if the Genovese family could reach him, he was a dead man.

He was eventually brought to Washington, D.C., and kept in a jail cell, away from every other prisoner. A Justice Department lawyer, William Hundley, took responsibility for keeping him alive. Agents sat with him day after day, filling notebooks.

Valachi claimed that Genovese had put a price on his head: one hundred thousand dollars to whoever silenced him.

He was sentenced to life in prison for the killing in Atlanta, not death. And he began to talk.

He talked about the war of 1930. He talked about the ceremony with the gun and the knife. He talked about who reported to whom.

And he gave the government a name.

For decades, investigators and journalists had used the word Mafia. Valachi said the members themselves called it something else.

Cosa Nostra. Our thing.

To the Department of Justice, this was extraordinary. The attorney general of the United States, Robert F. Kennedy, the same man we met in our first file working for a Senate committee, was now running the war on organized crime. He would call Valachi's information the biggest single intelligence breakthrough yet in combating organized crime and racketeering in the United States.

But information inside a government file can be denied. Information on television cannot.

So the government made a decision that no one had ever made before.

They would put a made man in front of the cameras.

---

CHAPTER SIX. Live from the Senate.

On September 25th, 1963, the Senate's Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, led by Senator John McClellan, opened its hearings on organized crime.

The first witness was not Valachi. It was the attorney general, Robert Kennedy.

McClellan set the tone in his opening statement. The existence of such a criminal organization as Cosa Nostra, he said, is frightening.

Two days later, Valachi arrived.

He sat at the witness table in a plain suit, wearing glasses, speaking in the rough New York accent of the streets he grew up on. Over several days of testimony, he walked the senators, and the country, through the organization from the inside.

There were families. Each family had a boss. Below the boss were lieutenants, the caporegimes. Below them, the soldiers. Above all the families sat a commission of bosses who settled disputes.

Large charts were placed in the room, showing the families of New York and the men in them. The chart for the Genovese family alone listed more than a hundred names.

He described the initiation ceremony. He described the code of silence. He described murders, and the reasons for them.

He named names. Bosses, lieutenants, soldiers. Men who were still running businesses in New York, still eating in the same restaurants, still living in the same neighborhoods. Some of them were watching on television.

He also claimed that Genovese, from prison, had ordered the deaths of other inmates who were expected to testify against him.

Not every answer was clean. Valachi mixed up dates. He told some stories differently from one day to the next. He sometimes spoke about events he had only heard about. Senators pressed him, and sometimes he grew irritated.

But the core of what he said held together. And no one in that room had ever heard anything like it from the inside.

At one point, Senator McClellan asked him why he had never simply walked away.

Valachi's answer became one of the most quoted lines of the hearings.

Once you are in, you can't get out. They will hunt you.

Across the country, families watched on black-and-white television sets as a gangster calmly described a world most people had been told did not exist.

The cameras did what Apalachin had started six years earlier. The Mafia was no longer a rumor, or a headline.

It now had a face. And a voice.

---

CHAPTER SEVEN. What changed, and what didn't.

Here is the uncomfortable truth about the Valachi hearings.

They did not put a single boss in prison.

Valachi's testimony never led directly to the conviction of a major Mafia leader. Some of what he said was hearsay. He was describing events from decades earlier, from the point of view of a soldier, not a boss. Even inside the Justice Department, there were doubters. One senior official, William Hundley, would later call Cosa Nostra the most overrated thing since the Communist Party.

And the timing could not have been worse.

Less than two months after the hearings began, on November 22nd, 1963, President John F. Kennedy was assassinated in Dallas. His brother Robert, the man who had pushed the war on organized crime harder than any attorney general before him, was devastated. The campaign lost momentum.

But the hearings changed something that could not be undone.

They changed what the public believed. And they changed what lawmakers were willing to do.

Other witnesses backed up much of what Valachi described. Police officials from New York, Detroit, Chicago, Tampa and Buffalo told the subcommittee about similar organizations in their own cities.

In the years that followed, Congress gave investigators new weapons. In 1968, a federal law allowed court-ordered wiretaps in organized crime cases. In 1970 came the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act. RICO. A law that would let prosecutors go after an entire criminal enterprise, not just one crime at a time.

In the 1980s, those tools would help put the heads of New York's crime families in prison.

Another idea that grew out of this era was simple: if the government wanted insiders to testify, it had to keep them alive. In the early 1970s, the federal Witness Security Program was formally created. Generations of future informants would disappear into new names and new cities, the way Valachi never could.

The names Valachi gave to the New York families stuck, too. Investigators began referring to them by the names of their bosses in the 1960s: Genovese, Gambino, Lucchese, Bonanno and Colombo. Journalists followed. Today, they are still known as the Five Families.

Joe Valachi did not take down the Mafia.

But he helped hand the government the map.

---

CHAPTER EIGHT. The last soldier.

After the hearings, Valachi never walked free again.

He spent his final years in federal custody, moved from prison to prison under protection. At one point, he tried to take his own life. He survived.

In his cell, he wrote. Hundreds of handwritten pages about his life, which he called The Real Thing. The Justice Department first encouraged the project, then moved to block its publication. Eventually, journalist Peter Maas used those pages and his own interviews with Valachi to write The Valachi Papers, published in 1968. A few years later, it became a Hollywood film starring Charles Bronson.

As for Vito Genovese, the man whose kiss started it all, he never got out. He died in federal custody in 1969, still officially the boss of his family.

Joe Valachi died on April 3rd, 1971, at a federal prison in La Tuna, Texas, near El Paso. He was sixty-six years old. The cause was a heart attack.

The bounty on his head was never collected.

Why did he talk? Valachi said he wanted to expose an organization that had ruined his life. His critics said he wanted revenge on Genovese, and protection from a possible death sentence. The honest answer is probably all of it.

But think back to 1930. To that private house. The gun, the knife, the pin, the forty men in the room.

And the man who pricked Valachi's finger.

Joe Bonanno.

By the time Valachi testified, Bonanno was the boss of his own family, one of the five Valachi named. And just one year later, in October 1964, on a rainy night on Park Avenue in New York, Joe Bonanno would simply vanish.

Kidnapped, his lawyer said.

For nineteen months, one of the most powerful men in the American Mafia was gone. And when he finally walked back into the world, he would find a war raging inside his own family.
