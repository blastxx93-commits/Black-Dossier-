### ch00_hook
Imagine this. You buy a brand-new BYD. A few months later, you hit a kangaroo on the motorway at 110 kilometres an hour. Your insurance accepts the claim. The parts are ordered. And then… nothing. Eleven weeks later, your car is still sitting at the smash repairer. And the supplier tells you the missing parts are another eight weeks away.

That's not a made-up story. It's what one BYD owner described publicly on an Aussie car forum in early 2025 – and it wasn't the only one. Parts delays, calls that go unanswered, and long waits just to get a car looked at became some of the most common complaints in BYD owner groups.

Today, BYD is one of the biggest car brands in Australia. So what went wrong after the sale? How expensive is a BYD to repair and insure, really? And has BYD actually fixed it? Because in October 2026, BYD made a very big announcement. Stay with me to the end, because what it means for you depends on which BYD you drive.

### ch01
First, let's understand how fast this happened. BYD arrived in Australia with one model, the Atto 3 electric SUV. Within a few years, it had a full range: the Dolphin, the Seal, the Sealion 6 and Sealion 7, the Shark 6 ute, and now even a budget EV, the Atto 1, priced from $19,990.

In the first half of 2026, BYD sold more than 52,000 cars in Australia. In March, it broke into the top three brands in the country. By October, BYD said it had more than 150,000 customers here.

That's incredible growth. But it created a problem that every fast-growing brand eventually hits. Selling cars is one thing. Fixing them is another. Every one of those 150,000 cars will need servicing, tyres, windscreens, and – sooner or later – crash repairs. And the network to support them didn't grow as fast as the sales did.

### ch02
Let's start with parts. Australia is a small market compared to China, Europe or the United States. When a part isn't stocked locally, it has to be shipped, and that can take weeks or months.

BYD's own executives have been open about this. When Stephen Collins took over as BYD Australia's chief operating officer, he spoke publicly about how the brand needed to shake off its reputation for long service and parts delays. CarsGuide reported that months-long parts delays and unanswered emails and calls were among the most common complaints in owner groups, with some posts attracting thousands of responses.

And there was another issue. Mark Harland, the boss of BYD's premium brand Denza, admitted that in the early days, even basic things like repair training and the right tools weren't in place everywhere. Even if a workshop had the part, did they have the training to fit it on an EV? Not always.

### ch03
Now, to be fair, this isn't only a BYD problem. In July 2026, the Australian Automotive Dealer Association released an independent review by Emeritus Professor Jenny Buchan. The review found that customers across the industry face long delays to get their cars fixed – especially electric vehicles.

Dealers told the review that workshop backlogs could stretch to six to eight weeks just to diagnose a problem. Not fix it. Just find out what's wrong. Combine that with slow parts and, often, no loan car, and owners can be left without transport for a very long time.

The report also pointed at the wave of new brands. Australia has more than seventy car brands competing for around 1.2 million sales a year. Denza, Geely, IM Motors and GAC have all arrived recently. Not all of them have the parts warehouses, trained technicians and dealer networks that the long-established brands built over decades.

### ch04
So what about cost? You'll see lots of scary numbers online about BYD repair bills. Here's what we can actually back up.

EVs and plug-in hybrids are generally more expensive to repair after a crash. A senior figure in BYD's collision repair network told the industry magazine Paint and Panel that EV repair costs currently sit in a category more like high-end luxury cars. That pushes insurance premiums up. The battery is the big one – if it's badly damaged, a car can be written off, because the battery is such a large share of the car's value.

It's not just the battery, either. Take the Shark 6. A rear bumper isn't just plastic anymore. It carries the reversing camera and parking sensors. A low-speed car park bump that used to cost a few hundred dollars can turn into new sensors plus recalibration.

But here's the part a lot of videos leave out. Insurance comparison site Compare the Market quoted twenty popular hybrids, plug-ins and EVs across eleven insurers. The BYD Dolphin averaged about $1,425 a year – less than a Toyota Corolla hybrid. The Sealion 7 came in just under a RAV4 hybrid. The Shark 6 was higher, at around $2,071 a year, but still well below the two Teslas, which were over $3,000.

So the honest answer is: BYD repairs can cost more than an old petrol car, especially for crash damage. But BYD insurance isn't automatically more expensive than its Japanese rivals. It depends heavily on the model.

### ch06
There's one more thing worth mentioning. In 2026, BYD recalled 32,009 Shark 6 utes in Australia because of possible abnormal wear in the spare wheel assembly. BYD advised affected owners to remove the spare wheel if they wanted to keep driving before the fix.

Recalls happen to every brand – Toyota, Ford, all of them. A recall is actually the system working. But for a brand already fighting a reputation for slow after-sales support, a recall of more than thirty thousand utes adds pressure on dealers and workshops that were already busy.

### ch07
So, has BYD done anything about it? Yes – and it's big.

On the 20th of October 2026, BYD Australia announced that it now holds more than 580,000 parts for BYD and Denza models on Australian soil. That's around 95 per cent of the parts for both ranges, kept in stock locally. The value of those parts is more than $25 million at any time, spread across more than 24,000 square metres of warehouses in four cities.

The biggest is in Melbourne, with about 15,000 square metres and a team of around forty people. BYD says dealers close to a warehouse should get in-stock parts within twenty-four hours, and remote or rural areas within about a week. Importantly, BYD says it now keeps Blade battery packs in local stock – and batteries are one of the slowest parts to get when they have to come by ship.

Chief operating officer Stephen Collins summed up the message like this: "Our commitment to Australian customers doesn't end at the handover. It starts there."

BYD also set up its own approved collision repair network early, and many of those shops were already trained on Teslas, so they had the EV equipment and experience. That's a smart shortcut.

### ch08
Now, if you're stuck waiting on a repair right now, here's something every Aussie should know. Under the Australian Consumer Law, manufacturers and importers must make spare parts and repair facilities available for a reasonable time after you buy a product. That applies even if you didn't buy it directly from them.

The problem, as the dealer association's review pointed out, is that "reasonable" is vague. The review called for clearer rules, including on battery degradation and replacement. Until that happens, the best thing you can do is keep everything in writing, contact the manufacturer's head office directly – not just the dealer – and if it drags on, go to your state's fair trading office.

There's also the Motor Vehicle Service and Repair Information Sharing Scheme, which came in from 2022. It requires car makers to share repair information with independent workshops. But the Insurance Council has raised concerns that some brands still restrict parts sales to their own dealers, which limits how many workshops can actually do the job.

### ch09
So, is BYD's repair problem real? Yes. Owners waited far too long for parts in the early years, and the brand itself has admitted it. Are BYD cars more expensive to repair? For crash damage on EVs and plug-ins, usually yes – but insurance on models like the Dolphin and Sealion 7 is competitive, and sometimes cheaper than the Toyota equivalent.

And has it been fixed? BYD has put real money into the problem – $25 million of parts and four warehouses is not a press release with nothing behind it. The real test is whether owners notice the difference over the next twelve months. Because a warehouse full of parts only helps if the workshops, the technicians and the phone lines keep up too.

If you're buying a BYD today, ask the dealer three questions. Where is the nearest approved collision repairer? How long are current service bookings? And what's the loan car policy if the car is off the road? The answers will tell you more than any brochure.
