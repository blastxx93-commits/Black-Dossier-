### ch02
Number ten: the KGM Rexton.

The Rexton is a big, rugged, diesel four-wheel-drive seven-seater from the brand Australians used to know as SsangYong. It's built for towing and for the bush, and it's priced from about fifty-two thousand dollars drive-away up to just over sixty-four thousand for the top model.

On paper, that's great value against a Toyota Prado or a Ford Everest. But look at the numbers. In the first nine months of 2026, the Rexton found 371 buyers. In the same period last year, it was 1,125. That's a fall of 67 per cent. By the end of October, CarExpert's database had the Rexton on 403 for the year, still down two thirds.

GoAuto put it brutally. Of all the slow sellers it looked at, the Rexton has "the dubious honour" of both the highest sales and the steepest decline. And while the Rexton sold 371, the Ford Everest sold more than sixteen thousand. That's almost forty-four Everests for every Rexton.

Part of the problem is the price climb. For 2026, the Rexton got more equipment – but it also got more expensive. And at the same time, Chinese brands have flooded this exact part of the market with cheaper seven-seat SUVs and plug-in hybrids. When the value brand stops being the cheapest option, buyers start asking why they should take the risk.

### ch03
Number nine: the Skoda Octavia.

This is the first of three Skodas on this list, and that tells you something already.

The Octavia is a car that motoring journalists have loved for years. It's practical, it's well built, it drives nicely, and the wagon is one of the most useful family cars you can buy. In 2026, Skoda even added a new mild-hybrid version to the range.

But buyers aren't listening. The Octavia sold 340 cars in the first nine months of the year, down from 557 in the same period of 2025. That's a fall of 39 per cent. Prices start at around forty-three and a half thousand dollars drive-away, and the hot RS goes past sixty-five thousand.

Here's the comparison that hurts. In the same segment, the Toyota Camry sold 9,471. That's almost twenty-eight Camrys for every Octavia.

The problem isn't that the Octavia is a bad car. It's that it's a petrol car, a European car, and a sedan or wagon – three things the Australian market is moving away from at the same time. Buyers who want something practical are buying hybrid SUVs. Buyers who want something new are walking into Chinese showrooms. And the Octavia is stuck in the middle.

### ch04
Number eight: the Jeep Wrangler.

The Wrangler is an icon. It's the original off-roader, and you can spot one from a mile away. But in the first nine months of 2026, Australians bought just 247 of them, down from 345 in the same period last year. That's a fall of more than 28 per cent.

Price is the big issue. The cheapest four-door Wrangler went from around thirty-seven thousand dollars back in 2014 to almost seventy-six thousand before on-road costs. At that money, buyers are cross-shopping a Prado, an Everest, or a Chinese four-by-four with a lot more equipment.

GoAuto worked out that the Ford Everest outsold the Wrangler by more than sixty-five to one this year.

And look at the rest of the Jeep showroom. In October, the Jeep Compass sold one car. One. The Grand Cherokee and the Avenger sold zero. Right now, Jeep is offering a four-thousand-dollar deposit contribution on the Wrangler Rubicon four-door, according to the deals tracker CarSorted. When a brand starts paying part of your deposit, that tells you how hard the cars are to move.

### ch05
Number seven: the Skoda Karoq.

Skoda number two. The Karoq is a sensible mid-size SUV, and CarExpert rated it "very good" in a recent review, praising its easy driving, low servicing costs and sharp drive-away pricing – from $40,990 drive-away.

But the sales chart tells a sad story. The Karoq sold 752 cars in 2024. Then 470 in 2025. And in the first nine months of 2026, just 232, down 32 per cent on the same period last year.

To put that in perspective, it's in the same segment as the Toyota RAV4 – Australia's best-selling SUV. GoAuto worked out that the RAV4 is outselling the Karoq by almost one hundred and thirty-five to one. CarExpert's review even pointed out that the Karoq is being outsold by newer or niche cars like the Deepal S07, the Skoda Elroq, the Mini Countryman and the Volvo EX30.

The reviewer's verdict was revealing too: underpowered, a tight back seat, some dated tech, and a car that "feels like a rental". That's exactly the kind of car that gets crushed when buyers have dozens of shiny new hybrids and electric SUVs to choose from at the same money.

### ch06
Number six: the Ford Mustang Mach-E.

This one might be the strangest on the list. It has one of the most famous badges in the world. It's an electric SUV, in a year when electric car sales in Australia are exploding. And yet, nobody wants it.

The Mach-E sold 206 cars in the first nine months of 2026, down more than 34 per cent on the same period last year. In 2025, it found fewer than five hundred buyers in total.

And it's not for lack of trying. Ford has already cut the price. EV Central reported drive-away offers of sixty thousand dollars for the Select, seventy-five thousand for the Premium and eighty-nine thousand for the GT – cuts of up to around nine and a half thousand dollars on the earlier list prices. That reviewer described the price cuts as a sign that Ford was "desperate to shift" stock.

Compare that with America, where Ford sold more than fifty thousand Mach-Es in 2025. So why is Australia so different? Simple: competition. In Australia, a Mach-E is fighting the Tesla Model Y, the BYD Sealion 7, the Geely EX5 and a dozen other electric SUVs, many of them cheaper and newer. In October alone, the Model Y sold 3,105 cars. The Mach-E sold 206 in nine months.

### ch07
Number five: the Mazda CX-70.

The CX-70 is Mazda's big, premium five-seat SUV, with a powerful straight-six engine. It's built for the American market – and in Australia, it has never caught on.

It sold 182 cars in the first nine months of 2026, down from 278 in the same period last year. That's a fall of almost 35 per cent. GoAuto noted that in the large SUV class above eighty thousand dollars, the Land Rover Defender sold 3,098 – that's seventeen Defenders for every CX-70.

And this is despite a major price cut. In July 2026, Mazda slashed CX-70 prices by between $3,700 and $8,051, depending on the variant. It didn't fix the problem.

Then in late October, Mazda confirmed the CX-70 is ending production for the United States – its biggest market. Mazda Australia says the car will continue here, and that it still has a small but loyal fanbase. But when a car is built mainly for America and America no longer gets it, buyers here will be asking how long it's really going to last.

### ch08
Number four: the Hyundai Sonata.

Here's a car whose sales are collapsing, and Hyundai's response is… to put the price up.

In Australia, the Sonata is now sold only as the sporty N Line sedan. It found just 179 buyers in the first nine months of 2026, down 37 per cent on the same period last year. The Toyota Camry outsold it by almost fifty-three to one.

And then, from the first of October, Hyundai raised the Sonata N Line's price by five hundred dollars, to fifty-seven thousand dollars before on-road costs. That's more than sixty-one thousand drive-away in Sydney.

Why would you raise the price of a car that isn't selling? Hyundai blamed "global economic factors, manufacturing, logistics and local policy". That local policy is Australia's New Vehicle Efficiency Standard, which sets limits on average CO2 emissions. According to reports, Hyundai was fined 4.2 million dollars for missing its 2025 target, and the targets get tougher in 2026 and 2027. A big petrol sedan doesn't help Hyundai's numbers – so there's very little reason for it to discount one.

And the bigger picture for Hyundai is ugly. In October, the whole brand was down almost 29 per cent. Its sister brand Kia is now outselling it for the year.

### ch10
Number two: the Kia EV9.

This is the most expensive car on the list. The Kia EV9 is a big, seven-seat electric SUV, and in 2026 Kia added a new GT version priced at $129,250 – the most expensive Kia ever sold in Australia. The range starts at $97,000 before on-road costs.

### ch11
And number one: the Honda Accord.

The car we started with. Fifty-nine sales in the first nine months of 2026, down from 78 in the same period last year. That's fewer than seven cars a month, across the entire country.

The Accord is now sold here as a single hybrid model, the e:HEV RS, at around $64,900 drive-away. That's a lot of money for a mid-size sedan, in a market that is falling out of love with sedans. For comparison, a Toyota Camry hybrid starts at much less – and Toyota sold 9,471 of them. That's over one hundred and sixty Camrys for every Accord.

Honda's business model in Australia is part of the story. Since 2021, Honda has sold its cars at fixed, no-haggle drive-away prices through an agency model, with far fewer dealerships. It said it wanted fewer, more profitable sales – and with the Accord, it certainly has fewer.

And now Honda is trying to move them. Its end-of-year plate clearance includes a thousand-dollar gift card on the Accord hybrid, plus the first five services free. Meanwhile, CarExpert reported that the updated 2027 Accord will cost a thousand dollars more.

GoAuto made an important point about this whole list: these sales gaps aren't necessarily about product quality. And that's true. The Accord is a good car. It just costs too much, in a body style Australians are abandoning.

### ch13
So what does all this mean for you?

If you're buying, cars that aren't selling are where the biggest deals live. When a model sits in a dealer's yard, it costs the dealer money every week, and the end of the year is when dealers are chasing their targets. If one of these cars genuinely suits you, ask for a drive-away price in writing, ask how long that car has been in stock, and ask whether a newer model is on the way. Old stock and demonstrators are where dealers usually have the most room to move.

But be careful. A car that's hard to sell new is usually hard to sell used, too. Expect faster depreciation, and plan to keep it longer. Check the warranty, the service plan, and how far you'll have to travel for parts and servicing – especially with smaller brands.

And if you already own one of these cars, don't panic. Low new-car sales don't change your warranty or your consumer rights. Keep your service history up to date, and if you're thinking of selling, get several valuations before you accept a trade-in offer.
